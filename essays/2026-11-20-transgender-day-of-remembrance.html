Major occasion · 20 November 2026

Transgender Day of Remembrance

Transgender Day of Remembrance is kept every 20 November to honour transgender people whose lives have been lost in acts of anti-transgender violence. It is a day for grief, dignity and attention to individual lives. Memorial gatherings often include the reading of names, allowing a person to be remembered as someone who belonged to a family or community, with relationships, hopes and an everyday life. The date gives that work of remembrance a recurring place in the year, across national borders and different local traditions.

GLAAD traces the observance to a vigil started in 1999 by transgender advocate Gwendolyn Ann Smith. It honoured Rita Hester, a transgender woman killed in 1998, and remembered other transgender people lost to violence since her death. From that beginning grew an annual practice that communities could adopt for themselves. It was not created by a government decree. Its history rests in people deciding that deaths which could otherwise be overlooked needed to be acknowledged publicly and remembered with care.

The day follows Transgender Awareness Week, observed from 13 to 19 November. Those dates serve related purposes but have different tones. The week creates opportunities to learn about transgender people’s lives, contributions and experiences; the day of remembrance provides space to mourn. UCL’s current account of the 2026 observance places both in the calendar and describes displaying the transgender flag on its Portico building through 20 November. A visible symbol can mark institutional recognition, while the quieter work of remembrance takes place within and beyond that setting.

There are specific UK gatherings today. The University of Salford advertises a vigil beginning in the University House foyer at 5.15pm UK time, with hot drinks before participants walk to the Trans Remembrance Tree for a reading of names. Its listed event ends at 8pm. That sequence gives a useful sense of how a memorial can work: people come together, move into a shared place and make time for names to be spoken. Anyone attending should check the organiser’s page for the latest practical details.

Elsewhere, GLAAD describes vigils organised by transgender advocates and LGBTQ organisations in community centres, parks, places of worship and other venues. The form is local rather than centrally prescribed. A gathering can include readings and silence; a person can also mark the date privately. No single attendance figure or global list can express the whole meaning of the day. The important connection is between the public acknowledgement of violence and the individual people whom friends, relatives and communities are trying to keep in memory.

For JT, a thoughtful way to approach today is to read the history, listen to the people organising remembrance and follow their lead about participation. That might mean joining a nearby vigil or simply making room for a conversation without expecting someone to explain their grief. It is possible to acknowledge the seriousness of the occasion without reducing transgender lives to suffering alone. The people remembered had fuller lives than the violence that ended them. Keeping their names and humanity in view is a small, deliberate act against forgetting, and the reason this date continues to matter.
