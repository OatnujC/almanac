Dr Sun Yat-sen’s Birthday (Taiwan)

Today, 12 November, is the birthday of Sun Yat-sen, the revolutionary who helped bring down China’s last imperial dynasty and became the first provisional president of the Republic of China. In Taiwan, where the Republic of China government has been based since 1949, the day is an official memorial day, known in Chinese as 國父誕辰紀念日, the birthday of the Father of the Nation. It also carries two other labels: Chinese Cultural Renaissance Day and Doctors’ Day. Unusually for a figure so tied to one political party, Sun is honoured on both sides of the Taiwan Strait.

From village boy to doctor

Sun was born on 12 November 1866 in Cuiheng, a village in Xiangshan county in Guangdong province, southern China. The county was later renamed Zhongshan in his honour, after the name he is best known by in Chinese, Sun Zhongshan. His family were poor farmers. In 1879 his elder brother, Sun Mei, who had emigrated to Hawaii as a labourer, brought him to Honolulu, where he studied at a British missionary school and then at Oahu College. It was his first deep contact with Western ideas, and it changed him. His brother disliked his growing interest in Christianity and sent him home, but Sun went on to school in Hong Kong and was baptised there.

He then chose medicine. After starting at a medical school in Guangzhou, he transferred in 1887 to the new College of Medicine for Chinese in Hong Kong, and graduated in 1892 at the top of his class. One of his teachers there was the Scottish surgeon James Cantlie, a friendship that would later save his life. Sun briefly practised medicine in Macau and Guangzhou, but, as Britannica puts it, he trained as a doctor but became a revolutionary. That early career is the reason his birthday doubles as Taiwan’s Doctors’ Day.

The revolutionary years

Sun grew up as China, under the Qing dynasty, was suffering repeated humiliation at the hands of foreign powers. In 1894 he founded the Revive China Society in Honolulu, and the following year he plotted an uprising in Guangzhou. It was discovered before it began, and Sun fled into an exile that lasted 16 years.

The most famous episode of those years happened in London. In October 1896 Sun was seized by officials of the Chinese legation and held prisoner there, apparently so that he could be shipped back to China. He managed to get word out to Cantlie, his old teacher, who raised the alarm; the British Foreign Office intervened and Sun was released after 13 days. The story made headlines and made Sun internationally famous. He then spent months reading in the British Museum library, working out the ideas that became his Three Principles of the People: nationalism, democracy and the people’s livelihood.

In 1905 in Tokyo he became head of a new revolutionary coalition, the United League, or Tongmenghui. Uprising after uprising failed. But the Qing government was weakening, and when an army revolt broke out in Wuchang on 10 October 1911 the provinces fell away one by one. Sun hurried back from abroad and became the first provisional president of the new Republic of China, sworn in at Nanjing at the start of 1912. To secure the abdication of the child emperor and avoid civil war, he soon stepped aside in favour of the powerful general Yuan Shikai. The years that followed were turbulent: Sun founded the Kuomintang, or Nationalist Party, fell out with Yuan, went into exile again, and later set up a rival government in Guangzhou, which he led from 1923. He died of cancer in Beijing on 12 March 1925, aged 58, with China still divided.

Why he is honoured today

Sun’s reputation outlived his chaotic political career. For the Kuomintang, which took the Republic of China government to Taiwan after losing the civil war to the Communists in 1949, he became Guofu, the Father of the Nation. His portrait hangs in official buildings in Taiwan, and the Republic of China’s constitution opens by invoking the teachings he bequeathed. The anniversary of his death, 12 March, is also kept in Taiwan, and coincides with Arbor Day.

In mainland China he is honoured too, as a pioneer of the revolution. His great mausoleum stands on Purple Mountain outside Nanjing, his portrait is set up in Tiananmen Square for major national celebrations, and his significant birthdays have been marked with official ceremonies in Beijing.

Chinese Cultural Renaissance Day

The extra name for 12 November has a Cold War story behind it. In 1966 Mao Zedong launched the Cultural Revolution on the mainland, with its campaign to destroy the old customs, culture, habits and ideas. In response, according to an account of the day published by a Republic of China cultural site, some 1,500 political and cultural figures in Taiwan launched a Chinese Cultural Renaissance Movement and asked that Sun’s birthday be designated Chinese Cultural Renaissance Day. The movement, chaired by President Chiang Kai-shek, set out to present Taiwan as the guardian of traditional Chinese culture, promoting classical learning, Confucian ethics and the arts at a time when they were under attack across the strait. Taiwan’s official calendar of memorial days and festivals, maintained by the Directorate-General of Personnel Administration, still lists both Dr Sun Yat-sen’s Birthday and Chinese Cultural Renaissance Day on 12 November.

How it is marked

Despite all this, 12 November is not a day off. It is one of Taiwan’s official memorial days, but under the rules governing them only six, such as National Day on 10 October, come with a holiday. Sun’s birthday used to be a public holiday, but from 2000 it became a day to commemorate rather than a day off, and since 2017 it has not been a statutory holiday for private-sector workers either. Today offices, schools and businesses open as normal, while government bodies and schools hold commemorative activities.

The focus for many is the National Dr Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall in Taipei, a large hall with a sweeping, upturned Chinese roof, opened in 1972, which is home to a giant seated statue of Sun and serves as a busy cultural centre with exhibitions and performances. Medical associations, meanwhile, use Doctors’ Day to honour the profession and present awards to doctors.

Elsewhere, Sun’s memory is kept in the many places his travels touched. Hong Kong has a Dr Sun Yat-sen Museum and a heritage trail through the streets where he studied and plotted. There are Sun Yat-sen memorials, museums and gardens in places he passed through, from Singapore and Penang to Honolulu and Vancouver. For a man who spent so much of his life on the move, it is fitting that his birthday is remembered so far from the village where he was born.
