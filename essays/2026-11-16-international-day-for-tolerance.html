Major occasion · 16 November 2026

International Day for Tolerance

Today is the International Day for Tolerance, one of the oldest and most quietly important days on the United Nations calendar. It asks a simple question that never stops being difficult: how do people who believe, look, speak and live differently manage to share one world in peace?

Why 16 November?

The date is tied closely to UNESCO, the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization, and to London. In November 1945, only months after the end of the Second World War, delegates from around 44 governments met in London for a conference to create an organisation for education and culture, an idea provided for in the new UN Charter. The conference ran from 1 to 16 November 1945, and on its last day, 16 November, the delegates signed UNESCO's constitution. (Sources differ slightly on exactly how many states signed that day; UNESCO's own accounts give figures of 37 and 41.) The constitution came into force a year later, on 4 November 1946, once twenty countries had ratified it.

The founders had lived through two world wars in a single generation, and they were convinced that political and economic treaties alone could not keep the peace. The famous opening of the constitution puts it this way: since wars begin in the minds of men, it is in the minds of men that the defences of peace must be constructed. UNESCO was meant to build those defences through schools, science, culture and the free flow of ideas.

Fifty years later, UNESCO marked its golden anniversary in the most fitting way it could. Its General Conference, meeting in Paris from 25 October to 16 November 1995, adopted the Declaration of Principles on Tolerance on 16 November 1995, the fiftieth anniversary of the constitution's signing. 1995 had been declared the United Nations Year for Tolerance, and it was also the 125th anniversary of the birth of Mahatma Gandhi. Article 6 of the declaration solemnly proclaimed 16 November as the annual International Day for Tolerance. The following year, in 1996, the UN General Assembly adopted Resolution 51/95, inviting member states to observe the day.

What the declaration says tolerance is

The word tolerance can sound rather grudging in English, as if it simply means putting up with something you dislike. The 1995 declaration deliberately set out a warmer, more demanding meaning. Its first article defines tolerance as respect, acceptance and appreciation of the rich diversity of our world's cultures, our forms of expression and ways of being human, and calls it harmony in difference. It says tolerance is not only a moral duty but also a political and legal requirement, and describes it as the virtue that makes peace possible.

Just as importantly, the declaration says what tolerance is not. It is not concession, condescension or indulgence, and it does not mean tolerating social injustice or giving up one's own convictions. It means accepting that people are naturally diverse and have the right to live in peace and to be as they are. The text was written against the background of the early 1990s, with wars in the former Yugoslavia and genocide in Rwanda fresh in everyone's mind, and it speaks plainly about the dangers of racism, discrimination, the marginalisation of minorities and refugees, and violence.

The declaration's answer is above all education. Article 4 calls education the most effective means of preventing intolerance, starting with teaching people what their shared rights and freedoms are, so that they will respect and protect the rights of others. It asks for teaching that helps young people develop independent judgement, critical thinking and ethical reasoning, so that they can resist influences that lead to fear and exclusion of others.

The Madanjeet Singh Prize

To mark the Year for Tolerance and Gandhi's anniversary, UNESCO also created a prize for the promotion of tolerance and non-violence, now known as the UNESCO-Madanjeet Singh Prize after the Indian diplomat, artist and writer who funded it. It is awarded every two years, traditionally around 16 November, to people or organisations whose work in science, art, culture or communication has promoted tolerance and non-violence.

How the day is marked

There are no fireworks or feasts for the International Day for Tolerance. Instead it is marked through schools, universities, museums, faith groups and governments, which use the day for talks, exhibitions, interfaith gatherings and lessons on human rights and living together. The UN Secretary-General and the Director-General of UNESCO usually issue messages. Several governments list it among their official commemorative days; South Africa, for example, describes it on its government website. Some countries, such as the United Arab Emirates, which has a Ministry of Tolerance and Coexistence, give it particular prominence.

UNESCO at 81

Today is also, quietly, UNESCO's 81st birthday by the signing of its constitution, and London can take a small share of the credit. The organisation that grew from that 1945 conference now runs the World Heritage List, the lists of intangible cultural heritage, programmes on literacy, science and press freedom, and much more. In the UK, the UK National Commission for UNESCO looks after the country's relationship with it.

It is worth pausing on the day's central idea. Tolerance in the declaration's sense is not passive. It asks each of us to be curious about people unlike ourselves, to stand up when others are treated unfairly, and to teach children to do the same. Thirty-one years after the declaration was written, that feels as relevant as ever.
