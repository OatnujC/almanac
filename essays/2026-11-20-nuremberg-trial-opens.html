Minor occasion · 20 November 2026

The opening of the Nuremberg trial

On 20 November 1945, the International Military Tribunal opened the trial of leading Nazi officials at the Palace of Justice in Nuremberg. Today is the 81st anniversary. Germany had surrendered only months earlier, and the scale of Nazi crimes demanded a response. The trial attempted to make senior leaders answer for their actions through an international legal process. It is remembered as a landmark, while the history also requires attention to its limits, its evidence and the many victims whose experiences could not be contained within one courtroom.

The tribunal was established by the London Agreement and Charter signed on 8 August 1945 by the United States, the United Kingdom, France and the Soviet Union. Each power provided judges and a prosecution team. The agreement followed wartime discussions about how perpetrators should be punished. It created a means to prosecute major figures whose responsibility extended across national boundaries. The machinery had to be built: charges, procedures, translation and the rights of defendants all needed an agreed form before proceedings could begin.

The charges concerned crimes against peace, war crimes and crimes against humanity, together with a conspiracy charge. Twenty-four men were indicted, 22 were tried, and 21 appeared in court because Martin Bormann was tried in his absence. Those numbers refer to different stages, which is why accounts can appear inconsistent when the distinction is left unexplained. The defendants came from different parts of the Nazi leadership. Hitler, Himmler and Goebbels were not among them: they had died by suicide before they could be brought before the tribunal.

Britain had a substantial role. Lord Justice Geoffrey Lawrence presided over the court, Norman Birkett served as the British alternate judge, and Sir Hartley Shawcross led the British prosecution. Proceedings were interpreted into English, French, German and Russian. The charter gave defendants rights including representation, presenting evidence and questioning prosecution witnesses. These practical details matter to the anniversary. The significance was not only that powerful men faced accusation, but that their responsibility was argued through a process intended to be tested and recorded.

The tribunal’s judgment in 1946 convicted 19 defendants and acquitted three. Twelve of those convicted received death sentences; others received prison sentences. Nuremberg did not prosecute every perpetrator, and this first international trial should not be confused with the many subsequent proceedings. The term genocide appeared in the indictment as a description, but was not a separate crime adjudicated under the tribunal’s charter. Keeping that distinction makes the history more precise and shows that the legal language associated with the Holocaust developed through stages.

Today the anniversary is chiefly marked through historical education, archives and reflection on accountability. Readers in the UK and elsewhere can explore the trial’s records and the explanations provided by the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum. It is worth beginning with the structure of the case and then following particular documents or witnesses, rather than treating the courtroom photograph as the whole story. The date recalls a decision to produce a public record and determine individual responsibility after immense violence. That work remains important because remembrance needs evidence as well as moral conviction.
