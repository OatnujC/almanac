Major occasion · Tuesday 10 November 2026

Govardhan Puja (Annakut)

Today, Tuesday 10 November 2026, Hindus across India and around the world celebrate Govardhan Puja, also called Annakut, which means "mountain of food". It is the fourth day of the five-day Diwali festival, and it has a mood all of its own. Where the first days of Diwali are about light, wealth and the goddess Lakshmi, Govardhan Puja is a day of gratitude, built around a story of the young Krishna lifting a whole hill to shelter the people he loved. The way it is celebrated fits the name: temples and homes prepare enormous, tiered displays of vegetarian food as an offering of thanks.

Why it falls today

Govardhan Puja is a lunar date. It is held on Kartika Shukla Pratipada, the first lunar day of the bright fortnight of the month of Kartika, which begins straight after the new moon of Diwali. Usually that means the day right after Diwali night. This year is a little different. Diwali's main night was Sunday 8 November, but the new moon day, Amavasya, was still running at sunrise on Monday 9 November and only gave way to Pratipada at around 12:31 pm New Delhi time that afternoon. Because a Hindu calendar day takes the lunar day that is in force at sunrise, the first morning in Pratipada is today, so Drik Panchang and other calendars place Govardhan Puja on 10 November, with Monday as a gap day. Pratipada runs until about 2:00 pm New Delhi time today, and the favoured morning window for the puja in Delhi was roughly 6:40 to 8:50 am local time.

Dates like this can shift by a day between regions and calendars, because sunrise and the exact moment a lunar day begins vary with place. For 2026, though, the major almanacs agree on today for Govardhan Puja. The fifth and last day of Diwali, Bhai Dooj, follows tomorrow in most reckonings.

The story of the hill

The festival's story comes from the Bhagavata Purana, one of the great Hindu scriptures about the god Vishnu and his incarnations. Krishna spent his childhood among the cowherds of Braj, the countryside around Mathura and Vrindavan in what is now Uttar Pradesh. Every autumn, the cowherds made offerings to Indra, the god of rain and storms, to keep the rains coming.

The young Krishna persuaded them to stop. Their cows, their pastures and their livelihood, he argued, came from the land around them, and especially from Govardhan Hill, so that was what they ought to honour. The villagers agreed and made their offering to the hill instead. Indra was furious at being passed over and sent days of thunderstorms and torrential rain to flood the village. To protect everyone, Krishna lifted Govardhan Hill and held it up, traditionally on the little finger of one hand, like a giant umbrella. People and cattle sheltered beneath it for seven days until Indra, seeing that nobody was harmed, admitted defeat and the storms stopped.

For devotees the story carries a gentle message: God protects those who take refuge in him, and gratitude belongs to what actually sustains us. The great heaps of food offered today are a reminder of the hill itself, and of the feast the villagers prepared for it.

How it is marked

In many homes, families make a small model of Govardhan Hill from cow dung, a traditional and sacred material in rural India. They decorate it with grass and twigs for trees, little figures of cows and cowherds, flowers and clay lamps, then worship it and walk around it. Women in some families fast for part of the day.

The heart of Annakut is the food. Devotees prepare a huge variety of vegetarian dishes, often the famous chhappan bhog, or 56 offerings, and arrange them in steps in front of the temple images. Sweets usually go nearest the deities, with lentils, vegetables, savoury fried snacks and breads on the lower tiers, and a mound of rice or other grains in the middle to stand for the hill. After the offering, the food is shared out as prasad, blessed food, so the whole community eats together.

In Braj itself, Govardhan Hill near Mathura becomes a great pilgrimage site. Pilgrims walk the parikrama, a circuit around the hill of more than ten miles lined with shrines, and the most devoted complete it by repeated full-body prostrations, which can take well over a week. The festival is especially important to Vaishnava traditions devoted to Krishna, including the Pushtimarg, Gaudiya (the tradition of ISKCON, the Hare Krishna movement) and Swaminarayan traditions. Swaminarayan temples are known for spectacular Annakut displays, with monks arranging hundreds of dishes from early morning.

Other names for the same day

The same lunar day is celebrated in different ways across India. In Gujarat it is Bestu Varas, the Gujarati New Year, when Vikram Samvat 2083 begins today. In Maharashtra and parts of the south it is Bali Pratipada or Padwa, honouring the generous demon king Bali and, for many couples, celebrating marriage. Some calendars also mark it as Dyuta Krida, a day of dice games recalling a game between Shiva and Parvati.

In Britain

British Hindus mark Annakut at temples up and down the country, from Leicester to the London suburbs. The BAPS Shri Swaminarayan Mandir in Neasden, north-west London, is known for its large Annakut display in the days around Diwali, when visitors of every background come to see the tables of food and share in the blessed offering. If you pass a Hindu temple this week, there is a good chance it will be full of sweets, lamps and people wishing each other a happy new year.
