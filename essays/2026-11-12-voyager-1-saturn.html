Minor occasion · Thursday 12 November 2026

Voyager 1 at Saturn (1980)

On 12 November 1980, NASA’s Voyager 1 spacecraft had one of the busiest days any space probe has ever had. Early in the day, by Universal Time, it flew close past Titan, Saturn’s largest moon, and late that evening it made its closest approach to Saturn itself, sweeping about 78,000 miles above the planet’s cloud tops. The pictures and measurements it sent home changed how we see the ringed planet, and the manoeuvre flung the spacecraft on the path that has made it the most distant human-made object in existence. Fittingly, this anniversary falls just days before another Voyager milestone.

The road to Saturn

The two Voyager probes were launched from Cape Canaveral in 1977, Voyager 1 on 5 September. They were built to explore Jupiter and Saturn, taking advantage of a rare alignment of the outer planets, one that NASA says comes round only about once every 175 years, which let a spacecraft use the gravity of one planet to swing on to the next. Voyager 1 carried eleven science instruments, from wide-angle and narrow-angle cameras to spectrometers, a magnetometer and detectors for cosmic rays and plasma.

Two weeks after launch it looked back and took the first single-frame picture of the Earth and Moon together. It crossed the asteroid belt without incident and flew past Jupiter on 5 March 1979, returning around 19,000 pictures, discovering two new moons and a thin ring around the giant planet. Then came a 17-month cruise. Voyager 1 began watching Saturn on 22 August 1980, while still 68 million miles away.

A choice that shaped the mission

Mission planners faced a dilemma. Titan was one of the most intriguing objects in the solar system, the only moon known to have a thick atmosphere, and scientists badly wanted a close look. But to get it, Voyager 1 would have to pass behind Saturn’s south pole, and the planet’s gravity would then hurl it up and out of the plane in which the planets orbit. That meant no more planetary encounters for Voyager 1. They chose Titan. Its twin, Voyager 2, took the other road and went on to Uranus and Neptune.

What Voyager saw

As Voyager 1 closed in, its pictures of Saturn’s rings grew ever more astonishing. The broad rings that astronomers had seen from Earth for centuries turned out to be made of thousands of narrower ringlets. The thin outer F ring, discovered by Pioneer 11 only the year before, appeared braided, its particles tugged into twisted strands by two small shepherd moons that orbit on either side of it. Saturn’s atmosphere proved stormier and more dynamic than expected, made mostly of hydrogen with about 11 per cent helium, and Voyager measured winds of up to 1,100 miles an hour. It pinned down the length of Saturn’s day at 10 hours 39.4 minutes.

On 12 November, about 18 hours before its closest approach to Saturn, the spacecraft passed within 4,034 miles of Titan. Its cameras saw only a featureless orange haze hiding the surface, with separate layers of haze floating high above. But its instruments found an atmosphere made mostly of nitrogen, with methane and other hydrocarbons, a surface pressure about 60 per cent higher than on Earth, and a bitter surface temperature of about minus 180 degrees Celsius (93 kelvin). Titan, it turned out, was a frozen world with a thick, Earth-like nitrogen sky, and planetary scientists have been fascinated by it ever since.

Voyager also studied Saturn’s icy moons, Mimas with its enormous crater a third as wide as the moon itself, smooth Enceladus, two-faced Iapetus with one dark hemisphere and one bright, and Tethys, Dione, Rhea and Hyperion. It found three new moons, raising the number known at the time to 15. Four days after the closest approach it took a famous picture looking back at Saturn as a thin crescent, its night side faintly lit by sunlight bouncing off the rings, a view impossible from Earth.

Onwards and outwards

Saturn’s gravity gave Voyager 1 enough speed to escape the solar system altogether. On 14 February 1990, shortly before its cameras were switched off for good to save power, it turned round and took a family portrait of the planets, including the image of Earth as a tiny speck that became known as the Pale Blue Dot. In August 2012 it crossed the heliopause, the edge of the bubble of particles blown out by the Sun, and became the first spacecraft in interstellar space.

Voyager 1 is still talking to us. NASA says that on Wednesday 18 November 2026, six days after this anniversary, it will reach one light-day from Earth, more than 16 billion miles away, so that a radio signal takes a full 24 hours to reach it. NASA is marking the milestone by inviting people around the world to flash Voyager’s call sign, VGR, in Morse code on their phones after sunset on 17 November, and it is preparing a documentary for the mission’s 50th anniversary in 2027. Like its twin, Voyager 1 carries a gold-plated record of sounds, music and greetings from Earth, just in case anyone out there ever finds it.

The Saturn encounter left a scientific legacy too. Titan’s hidden surface left questions that the later Cassini mission set out to answer: it orbited Saturn from 2004 and dropped the European Huygens probe through Titan’s haze to land on its surface in 2005. But it was Voyager 1, on this day in 1980, that first showed us how strange and beautiful the Saturn system really is.
