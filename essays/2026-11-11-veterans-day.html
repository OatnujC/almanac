Major occasion · Wednesday 11 November 2026

Veterans Day (United States)

Today is Veterans Day in the United States, a federal holiday on which Americans thank everyone who has served in the country's armed forces. Federal offices, post offices and most banks are closed, the President lays a wreath at Arlington National Cemetery, and towns across the country hold parades and ceremonies. It shares its date and its origin with Britain's Armistice Day, but over the past century it has grown into something rather different.

A small spelling note first: officially it is "Veterans Day", with no apostrophe, because it is a day for honouring veterans rather than a day belonging to them.

From Armistice Day

The story begins with the same moment as Remembrance Day: 11am on 11 November 1918, when the Armistice ended the fighting of the First World War. In November 1919 President Woodrow Wilson proclaimed the first anniversary as Armistice Day, a day for reflection on the heroism of those who died, with parades, public gatherings and a brief pause in business at 11am.

In 1926 Congress passed a resolution calling for the anniversary to be commemorated with ceremonies each year, and in 1938 an Act of Congress made 11 November a legal federal holiday, "dedicated to the cause of world peace" and to be known as Armistice Day. At that point it was still mainly a day to honour veterans of the First World War.

On 11 November 1921, America also buried its own Unknown Soldier, a serviceman killed in France whose identity was never known, at Arlington National Cemetery, in a tomb that has been guarded around the clock ever since.

Why the name changed

The Second World War put sixteen million Americans in uniform, and the Korean War followed soon afterwards. Many people felt it no longer made sense for the day to honour only the veterans of one war. A Second World War Navy veteran in Birmingham, Alabama, Raymond Weeks, organised a "National Veterans Day" celebration there in 1947 and campaigned for the change across the country.

In 1954 Congress amended the 1938 law, replacing the word "Armistice" with "Veterans", and President Dwight D. Eisenhower signed it. From then on, 11 November was a day to honour American veterans of all wars. Weeks was later honoured by President Ronald Reagan for his part in creating the holiday.

The brief move to October

In 1968 the Uniform Monday Holiday Act moved several federal holidays to Mondays to create three-day weekends, and Veterans Day was shifted to the fourth Monday in October, starting in 1971. It was not popular. Many states kept 11 November anyway, and veterans' organisations argued that the date itself mattered. In 1975 President Gerald Ford signed a law returning Veterans Day to 11 November from 1978, and there it has stayed. When it falls on a weekend, federal workers get the nearest Friday or Monday off, but this year it lands on a Wednesday.

Veterans Day and Memorial Day

Americans have two holidays that can look similar from the outside. Memorial Day, in late May, honours those who died while serving. Veterans Day honours everyone who served, in war or in peace, and is largely a day of thanks to the living. That makes it more celebratory than Britain's Armistice Day, with parades, school assemblies, free meals for veterans at restaurants and flags on homes and public buildings.

How it is marked today

The national ceremony takes place at Arlington National Cemetery, where a wreath is laid at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier at 11am, followed by a service in the Memorial Amphitheater. This year's presidential proclamation, issued on 10 November, calls on officials to display the flag and invites Americans to mark the day with public ceremonies and private reflection. The Department of Veterans Affairs organises a National Veterans Day Committee that coordinates the national observance and supports regional celebrations around the country.

Across the US, communities hold parades, some of them very large, such as New York's along Fifth Avenue. Schools invite veterans to speak to pupils, and many families simply use the day to thank parents and grandparents who wore the uniform. The day sits alongside Armistice and Remembrance ceremonies in Britain, France, Canada and Australia, all tracing back to that same morning in 1918.
