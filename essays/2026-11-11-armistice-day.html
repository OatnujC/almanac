Major occasion · Wednesday 11 November 2026

Armistice Day (Remembrance Day)

At eleven o'clock this morning, much of Britain will stop. Offices go quiet, trains and shops pause, radio stations hand the airwaves over to silence, and people stand at war memorials in towns and villages across the country. This is Armistice Day, also called Remembrance Day, and it always falls on 11 November, whatever day of the week that turns out to be. It marks the moment in 1918 when the fighting of the First World War came to an end, and it has become the day on which the UK and the Commonwealth remember everyone who has served and died in conflict since.

It is easy to muddle Armistice Day with Remembrance Sunday, which this year fell three days ago, on 8 November. The Sunday is the big national occasion, with the King, politicians and veterans gathered at the Cenotaph in Whitehall. Armistice Day is the anniversary itself, quieter and more everyday: the silence is kept wherever people happen to be, at work, at school or on the street.

Why 11 o'clock on the 11th day of the 11th month

By the autumn of 1918 Germany and its allies were exhausted. Bulgaria, the Ottoman Empire and Austria-Hungary had already stopped fighting, revolution was spreading through Germany, and the Kaiser abdicated on 9 November. In the early hours of 11 November, German delegates met the Allied commander, Marshal Ferdinand Foch, in a railway carriage in the forest of Compiègne, north of Paris, and signed an armistice: an agreement to stop fighting while a proper peace was negotiated. It was signed shortly after 5am, but it was agreed that the guns would fall silent at 11am.

That gap of a few hours had a terrible cost. Fighting went on along the Western Front right up to the deadline, and men were killed that very morning. The formal peace, the Treaty of Versailles, was not signed until June 1919, but in the minds of the people who lived through it, the war ended at 11am on 11 November 1918. The neat repetition of elevens helped fix the moment in public memory.

How the silence began

The first anniversary came in 1919. The idea of a pause for remembrance had roots in South Africa, where a daily "three minutes' pause" was kept in Cape Town during the final months of the war. The proposal for a two-minute silence in Britain was discussed by the War Cabinet in November 1919, and on 7 November that year King George V issued a letter asking that at 11am on the anniversary "all locomotion should cease" for two minutes, so that everyone could think of the fallen. People did exactly that. Contemporary accounts describe traffic halting and crowds standing bareheaded in the streets. The silence has been kept every year since.

A year later, on 11 November 1920, two great symbols were unveiled together. The Cenotaph in Whitehall, designed by Sir Edwin Lutyens and first built as a temporary structure for the 1919 peace celebrations, was unveiled in its permanent stone form. Its name means "empty tomb", and its inscription reads simply "The Glorious Dead". On the same day, the body of an unidentified British serviceman brought back from France, the Unknown Warrior, was buried in Westminster Abbey, standing in for all those whose bodies were never found or identified.

The poppy

The red poppy comes from the battlefields themselves. Poppies grew in the churned-up ground of Flanders and northern France, and the Canadian doctor John McCrae wrote of them in his 1915 poem "In Flanders Fields". In the United States, Moina Michael campaigned for the poppy as a symbol of remembrance, and Anna Guérin in France took the idea further, selling fabric poppies to raise money. The British Legion, founded in 1921, held its first Poppy Appeal that year, and the poppy has been worn in Britain every November since, with money raised going to support serving personnel, veterans and their families.

After the Second World War

Initially the day was known as Armistice Day. After the Second World War, it broadened to include the dead of that war too, and it has generally been called Remembrance Day since. Today it honours the service and sacrifice of all of the Armed Forces, veterans and their families. The House of Commons Library notes a striking statistic: since 1945 more than 7,000 UK armed forces personnel have died as a result of operations, and there have been only three years since then in which none died on medal-earning operations: 1968, 2016 and 2025.

How it is marked today

In the UK the national two-minute silence runs from 11:00 to 11:02. BBC Radio 4 carries it live, and at St Paul's Cathedral this year the silence is kept inside the cathedral and on the West Steps, where prayers are said and anyone is welcome to join. Many workplaces and schools stop, and in some places the silence is opened and closed by a maroon or the sounding of the Last Post.

The same date is kept across much of the world. In Canada and Australia it is Remembrance Day. In France and Belgium, 11 November is a public holiday, with ceremonies at memorials in every commune. In the United States it is Veterans Day, a celebration of all who have served. And in Poland, by a twist of history, 11 November is Independence Day, because the end of the war coincided with the rebirth of the Polish state.

Small details worth knowing

The first and last British soldiers killed in action on the Western Front, Private John Parr in August 1914 and Private George Ellison on the morning of 11 November 1918, are buried in the same small cemetery at St Symphorien near Mons in Belgium, only a few yards apart. The old rhyme of "the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month" is so familiar that "the eleventh hour" now means the last possible moment, a reminder of how close to the end those final deaths came.

This year marks 108 years since the Armistice. No one who fought in the First World War is still alive, but the silence goes on, now holding the memory of every war since.
