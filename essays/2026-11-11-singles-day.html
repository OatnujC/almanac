Minor occasion · Wednesday 11 November 2026

Singles’ Day (China)

In China, today is Guanggun Jie, Singles' Day, sometimes translated as "Bare Branches Day" or "bare sticks holiday". The date 11.11 is four ones standing side by side, and in Chinese slang a "bare branch" is a single person, so the day looks like four lonely singletons in a row. It began as a joke among university students and has grown into the largest shopping event in the world.

Born in a university dorm

Nanjing University in eastern China is generally credited as the birthplace. The university's own account says that in 1993 four senior students in a dormitory, all single, were chatting about how to escape the dullness of single life and chose 11 November, with its four ones, as a day to celebrate themselves. The idea caught on and spread to other campuses in the 1990s, then into wider society as those graduates went into the workplace.

The Carnegie Endowment's analysis describes the day as a wry acknowledgement that, in a society where adult social life had long revolved around early marriage, there was more to life before marriage than an undefined limbo. It developed its own tongue-in-cheek rituals: dinners and karaoke with fellow singles, matchmaking parties and blind dates, and, crucially, buying small gifts for yourself as consolation for being single.

How shopping took over

That self-gifting instinct turned out to be very good for business. In 2009 the e-commerce company Alibaba adopted the name for a sales promotion on its Tmall platform, involving just 27 vendors. The idea snowballed. Within a few years Singles' Day sales dwarfed America's Black Friday and Cyber Monday combined, with huge discounts, midnight countdowns, live-streamed galas featuring international stars and real-time sales tickers. In 2016 the gala included cameos by David Beckham and Scarlett Johansson, and more than 80 percent of purchases were made on mobile phones.

Rival platforms such as JD.com joined in, and the sales season now stretches over several weeks rather than a single day. Since 2022 Alibaba has stopped publishing its headline sales figure, partly reflecting a more cautious economic mood in China and government pressure on lavish displays of consumption, but the festival remains enormous.

Not just about shopping

Singles' Day has also become a mirror for social change in China. Marriage rates have been falling and young people are marrying later, and the day has become a moment for public discussion of dating, the pressures of family expectations and the freedoms of single life. For many young people it is still a social occasion, a chance to go out with friends and laugh about being unattached, and some couples have adopted the date for weddings or proposals precisely because it is meant to be the day for singles.

A note on the same date elsewhere: in South Korea, 11 November is Pepero Day, when people exchange thin chocolate-dipped biscuit sticks that resemble the number one, a custom built largely around a confectionery brand.

Why it is in the almanac

Singles' Day is now heavily commercial, but unlike many marketing-made shopping days it started as genuine student culture, a funny, slightly melancholy celebration that a generation invented for itself. It is a neat example of how a modern holiday can be born, spread and be transformed within a single lifetime.
