Major occasion · Saturday 14 November 2026

Chhath Puja: Kharna

Today is Kharna, the second day of Chhath Puja, one of the most demanding and beautiful festivals in the Hindu calendar. Chhath is devoted to Surya, the Sun god, and to Chhathi Maiya (also called Shashthi Devi), a mother goddess associated with the protection of children. It is celebrated above all in Bihar, Jharkhand and eastern Uttar Pradesh in India, and in the Terai region of Nepal, and by communities from those regions in Delhi, Mumbai and around the world, including in Britain.

Why it falls now

Chhath follows the Hindu lunar calendar, so its Gregorian date moves each year. The main day falls on Shashthi, the sixth day of the bright (waxing) fortnight of the month of Kartik, which comes six days after Diwali; the name Chhath itself comes from the Hindi word for six. In 2026 Diwali fell on Sunday 8 November, so the four-day festival runs from Friday 13 November to Monday 16 November: Nahay Khay on the 13th, Kharna today, Sandhya Arghya (the evening offering to the setting sun) on Sunday 15th, and Usha Arghya (the offering to the rising sun) with the breaking of the fast on Monday 16th. These dates follow the widely used New Delhi panchang; exact timings of the offerings depend on local sunrise and sunset.

What happens on Kharna

Kharna, also known as Lohanda, is a day of fasting and purification. The person keeping the vow, known as the vratin (very often, though not always, the mother of the family), goes without food and water through the day. In the evening, after worship, they prepare and eat the prasad, the sanctified offering. The classic Kharna dish is a kheer, rice pudding sweetened with jaggery (gur) rather than refined sugar, often called rasiya, eaten with chapatis or puris and bananas. It is traditionally cooked with great care on a fresh earthen stove using mango wood, in a kitchen cleaned specially for the occasion; onion, garlic and salt are avoided.

Family and neighbours are invited to share the prasad, which is considered a blessing to receive. Once the vratin has eaten, they begin the hardest part of Chhath: a fast of about 36 hours without food or even water, lasting through Sunday's evening offering until sunrise on Monday.

The wider festival

The rituals of Chhath are strikingly simple. There are no images or idols at its heart; the object of worship is the Sun itself. On the third day, families walk in procession to rivers, ponds and lakes, carrying bamboo baskets (soop and daura) filled with seasonal fruit, sugarcane, coconuts and the special sweet called thekua, a fried biscuit of wheat flour and jaggery. Standing waist-deep in the water, the vratins offer arghya, water and offerings, to the setting sun, while women sing traditional Chhath songs in Bhojpuri, Maithili and Magahi. Before dawn the next day they return to the water to greet the rising sun, after which the fast is finally broken.

The emphasis is on gratitude: the Sun is thanked for sustaining life on earth, and blessings are sought for the health, long life and well-being of family members, especially children. Many people see the festival's cleanliness, its use of seasonal produce and its focus on rivers as giving it an especially earthy, ecological character.

Origins and stories

Chhath is very old, and its origins are told in several ways. Some traditions link it to the Ramayana, saying that Rama and Sita worshipped the Sun after returning to Ayodhya. Others connect it to the Mahabharata: to Karna, the son of the Sun god, who was said to stand in the water to pray to his father, and to Draupadi, who is said to have kept a similar vow. Historians generally see it as a survival of ancient Vedic sun worship blended with folk traditions of the eastern Gangetic plain.

Chhath today

For many families from Bihar and eastern Uttar Pradesh, Chhath is the festival that brings everyone home, and special trains run to cope with the crowds. In cities far from the Ganges, people gather at artificial ponds, river banks and even beaches; in London and elsewhere in the diaspora, communities hold Chhath celebrations at lakes and riverbanks. Tonight, in kitchens across north India and Nepal, the gur kheer will be bubbling, and the long fast will begin.
