Anniversary of the World Wide Web proposal (1990)

Thirty-six years ago today, on 12 November 1990, two computer scientists at CERN, the European particle physics laboratory near Geneva, sent their managers a short memo with a long document attached. It was headed WorldWideWeb: Proposal for a HyperText Project, and it came from the British physicist Tim Berners-Lee and his Belgian colleague Robert Cailliau. It was the moment the web got its name and its first formal plan, and almost everything you do online today descends from the ideas in it.

Vague, but exciting

The 1990 document was not the first time Berners-Lee had put his idea on paper. In March 1989 he had circulated a proposal called Information Management: A Proposal. CERN had a real problem: thousands of scientists from around the world came and went, working on different computers that could not talk to each other, and information was constantly being lost. Berners-Lee suggested linking documents together with hypertext, text containing links that you can follow from one piece of information to another, across a network. His boss, Mike Sendall, famously wrote on the cover vague, but exciting. It was not a rejection, and Sendall let him carry on, but the idea needed sharpening.

Over the following year it was sharpened. Cailliau, a fellow CERN engineer, joined forces with Berners-Lee, and together they rewrote the plan in more concrete terms. The result was the document dated 12 November 1990.

What the proposal said

The text, which is still online at the World Wide Web Consortium, is refreshingly modest. Hypertext, it explains, is a way to link and access information of various kinds as a web of nodes in which the user can browse at will. It would give people a single way into reports, notes, databases, computer documentation and online help, using servers that already existed at CERN.

The authors proposed two phases. In the first, lasting three months, they would use existing software and hardware and write simple browsers for people’s workstations. In the second, another three months but more open-ended, users would be able to add new material themselves. They asked for four software engineers and a programmer, a workstation for each costing 10,000 to 20,000 (the memo just says 10 to 20k) and around 50,000 in all, plus 30,000 for software licences, visits and consultancy. In other words, they were asking for a small team and a few months to change the world, although nobody, least of all the authors, put it like that.

The document also settled the name. According to CERN’s history of the project, the naming was hotly debated, with The Mesh and The Information Mine among the contenders. WorldWideWeb won.

From memo to web

Berners-Lee did not wait for the paperwork. Around the same time he finished the first version of a program he called WorldWideWeb on a NeXT computer, the workstation made by the company Steve Jobs founded after leaving Apple. It was both a browser and an editor: you could write pages as well as read them, which reflected his belief that the web should be a two-way medium. Alongside it he wrote the first web server software, called httpd, and the first web server ran on that same NeXT machine at CERN. The browser was later renamed Nexus, to avoid confusion with the web itself.

Hardly anybody had a NeXT computer, so in 1991 CERN released a much simpler Line Mode browser that worked on ordinary terminals, bringing the web to far more people. On 28 October 1991 Berners-Lee started the www-talk mailing list, where early web pioneers argued about everything from tables and forms to the image tag that would eventually let pictures appear in pages. Then, on 30 April 1993, CERN put the web’s software into the public domain, meaning anyone could use it without paying royalties. That decision, as much as the technology, is why the web spread so fast. The Mosaic browser arrived the same year, followed by Netscape, Internet Explorer and the rest.

Remembering it today

The anniversary is not a public occasion so much as a date that web historians and the technology community like to mark, along with 12 March, the date of the 1989 memo, which is usually treated as the web’s official birthday. CERN has restored the first website, which explains what the web is and how to use it, and in 2019 a group of developers gathered at CERN to rebuild the original WorldWideWeb browser so that anyone can try it in a modern browser.

It is worth reading the 1990 proposal itself. It is short, practical and full of the cautious language of someone asking for a modest budget. One quotation from Berners-Lee that CERN likes to repeat captures the spirit behind it: if you think surfing hypertext is cool, that’s because you haven’t tried writing it. Thirty-six years on, with the web woven into nearly every part of life, the idea that it began as a request for five people and a few workstations is a nice reminder of how big things can start.
