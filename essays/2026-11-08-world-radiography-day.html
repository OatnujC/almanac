Minor occasion · Sunday 8 November 2026

World Radiography Day — when unseen light changed medicine

On 8 November 1895, working late in his laboratory at the Physical Institute of the University of Würzburg in Germany, the physicist Wilhelm Conrad Röntgen noticed something no one had properly noticed before: a new kind of invisible radiation that could pass through materials that ordinary light could not. He called it “X” rays, the X standing for the unknown. Within weeks, doctors around the world were using it to look inside living bodies without cutting them open. World Radiography Day marks that anniversary, and it is a thank-you to the radiographers and imaging staff who carry on that work every day.

Why 8 November

The day is the anniversary of the discovery itself, so its date is fixed. The International Society of Radiographers and Radiological Technologists has promoted 8 November as World Radiography Day since 2007, and radiographers’ societies around the world, including the UK’s Society of Radiographers, use it to celebrate the profession. The same date is also the International Day of Radiology, supported by radiology societies to recognise radiologists, radiographers and everyone else who works in medical imaging. In 2026 it fell on a Sunday.

The discovery

Röntgen was 50, a careful and rather shy experimental physicist who had studied engineering in Zurich and became director of the Physical Institute in Würzburg in 1888. In the autumn of 1895 he was experimenting with cathode rays, the streams of electrons produced when an electric current passes through a glass tube with most of the air pumped out. He had covered his tube in black cardboard, so that no visible light could escape, and darkened the room.

Yet a screen coated with a fluorescent chemical, barium platinocyanide, began to glow faintly a metre or so away. Something invisible was coming out of the tube, passing through the cardboard and making the screen shine. Over the next few weeks Röntgen worked almost without a break to investigate it. He found that the rays passed through paper, wood and even thin metal, but were stopped by lead, and that they could expose photographic plates. At one point, holding a small piece of lead in the beam, he saw the bones of his own hand on the screen.

He wrote up his findings in a paper, “On a new kind of rays”, submitted to the Würzburg Physical-Medical Society on 28 December 1895, and on 1 January 1896 he posted copies, with photographs, to colleagues around Europe. One of those images became world-famous: the bones of his wife Bertha’s hand, with her wedding ring clearly visible. It is often said that this was taken on the night of the discovery, but the Röntgen Memorial in Würzburg dates it to December 1895. The first newspaper report appeared in Vienna on 5 January 1896, and the news spread around the world within days.

On 23 January 1896 Röntgen presented the discovery in public for the first time, in a lecture to the Physical-Medical Society. During it he X-rayed the hand of the elderly anatomist Albert von Kölliker, who proposed that the new rays be called “Röntgen rays”. In German they still are.

Medicine changes overnight

Almost immediately, doctors saw the possibilities. In Britain, John Hall-Edwards in Birmingham used X-rays to locate a needle in a colleague’s hand on 11 January 1896, one of the first clinical uses anywhere, and the following month he used them to guide an operation. X-ray machines were soon used to find bullets and set broken bones. During the First World War, Marie Curie organised mobile X-ray units, nicknamed “little Curies”, to help surgeons near the front.

The early pioneers paid a price for their enthusiasm, because no one yet understood that X-rays could damage living tissue. Many suffered burns and later cancers. Hall-Edwards lost his left arm and most of the fingers of his right hand to X-ray damage, and some early workers died. There were also passing fads: from the 1920s to the 1950s, shoe shops used X-ray machines so customers could see their feet inside new shoes, until concerns about radiation led to them being banned. Today’s radiography is shaped by those hard lessons. A core part of a radiographer’s job is keeping doses as low as possible while still getting the image that is needed.

A modest man

Röntgen received the first Nobel Prize in Physics in 1901. Shy of public speaking, he declined to give the customary Nobel lecture, and he gave the prize money to the University of Würzburg. He refused to patent his discovery, believing it should benefit everyone. He died in Munich in 1923. His laboratory notes were burned after his death, which is one reason some details of that November evening remain uncertain.

Radiographers and radiologists

The two professions honoured on 8 November are related but different, a distinction many patients never notice. In the UK, radiographers are the registered health professionals who carry out the scans and treatments: diagnostic radiographers produce X-rays, CT, MRI and other images, and therapeutic radiographers plan and deliver radiotherapy for cancer. Radiologists are doctors who specialise in interpreting the images and in carrying out image-guided procedures. Modern hospitals could hardly function without either.

How the day is marked

Imaging departments use the day to open their doors, put up displays, and explain to the public what they do. The Society of Radiographers produces bunting, posters and colouring sheets, and its focus for 2026 is professionalism in radiography, celebrating the values and standards behind good practice. The International Society of Radiology’s message for the International Day of Radiology 2026 highlights how essential imaging is to diagnosis and treatment, and how unevenly access to it, and to trained staff, is spread around the world.

If you have ever had a broken bone checked, a scan before an operation or radiotherapy for cancer, 8 November is a day to remember the professionals behind the machine, and the curious physicist who first saw a screen glowing in a dark room.
