Minor occasion · 19 November 2026

World Pancreatic Cancer Day

Pancreatic cancer gets far less attention than its toll deserves. The World Pancreatic Cancer Coalition calls it one of the world’s deadliest cancers, and estimates that this year about 531,000 people worldwide will be diagnosed with it and nearly 490,000 will die from it. In many parts of the world, fewer than one in ten people diagnosed survive five years. World Pancreatic Cancer Day, held on the third Thursday of November, which this year is 19 November, is the moment when charities across the globe speak with one voice to change that.

How the day began

The day grew out of a decision by patient groups to stop working alone. In 2013, pancreatic cancer advocacy organisations from around the world began talking about how they could support each other and raise global awareness of the disease. As Julie Fleshman, president of the Pancreatic Cancer Action Network in the United States, later put it, “We realized that by working collaboratively, we could have a much greater impact than the sum of our parts.” After a first European meeting in Zurich in May 2014, they launched the first World Pancreatic Cancer Day in November 2014.

The day was a success, and in 2016 the groups went a step further and formally launched the World Pancreatic Cancer Coalition, which held its first meeting in Orlando, Florida, in May that year with around 40 organisations from more than 20 countries. The coalition now runs the day each year. Its membership has kept growing: Pancreatic Cancer UK, which sits on the coalition’s steering committee, describes it as having more than 110 member organisations in 39 countries on six continents. The day falls in November because November is Pancreatic Cancer Awareness Month, and it typically takes place on the third Thursday of the month; the coalition’s timeline counts 2022 as the ninth World Pancreatic Cancer Day, which makes 2026 the 13th.

Why awareness matters so much

The reason for all the noise is that pancreatic cancer is hard to catch early. The coalition’s message for this year is that knowing the signs and symptoms, and the risk factors, can lead to earlier diagnosis, when more treatment options may be available. The exact cause of most cases is unknown, but research has identified a number of risk factors, and the coalition urges people to listen to their bodies and talk to a doctor if something does not feel right. Its recent campaigns have carried titles such as “It’s About Time” and “Hello Pancreas”, the latter focused on early detection.

Turning the world purple

Purple is the colour of the cause, and the most visible part of the day is the lighting of buildings and landmarks in purple. The coalition asks people to “turn your world purple” on 19 November, to wear purple, take photographs and share them online with the hashtag #WPCD. In the UK, Pancreatic Cancer UK runs its Purple Lights campaign, encouraging people to light up their homes or ask a local landmark to glow purple at any point during November.

Pancreatic Cancer UK has also given the day one of its most charming traditions. Since 2017, on World Pancreatic Cancer Day, the charity has taken over St Pancras International station in London and added an “e” to its name, turning it into St PancrEas, to raise awareness and funds. The Popchoir has performed for commuters and travellers there each year, with the support of the station’s operators.

For those affected, the day is also about solidarity. People share their own stories and their reasons for taking part, and charities use the moment to point people towards information and support. Pancreatic Cancer UK, for example, runs a nurse helpline. After more than ten years of World Pancreatic Cancer Day, as the charity puts it, the global campaign is still growing.
