Major occasion · Saturday 14 November 2026

Moby-Dick published in America (1851)

On 14 November 1851, Harper & Brothers of New York put a thick new novel on sale for $1.50. It was called Moby-Dick; or, The Whale, and its author, Herman Melville, was a 32-year-old former sailor who had made his name with lively South Seas adventure stories. Today is the 175th anniversary of that American publication. The book was a commercial disappointment, received mixed reviews, and was largely forgotten by the time Melville died. It is now regularly named among the greatest novels ever written, and its first line, “Call me Ishmael,” is one of the most famous openings in literature.

Two editions, one month apart

Moby-Dick actually appeared twice in quick succession, and the reason was copyright. In 1851 there was no copyright agreement between Britain and the United States, so books by American authors were routinely pirated in Britain. To protect his rights, Melville arranged for the book to come out first in London. Richard Bentley published it there on 18 October 1851, in three ornate volumes, under Melville's original title, The Whale. A few weeks later Harper & Brothers issued the American edition in a single volume, under the new title Melville had settled on at the last minute: Moby-Dick.

The two texts are not the same. Scholars count roughly 600 substantive differences between them. The British edition is about 2,000 words shorter: passages thought too bawdy, too irreverent or too critical of royalty were trimmed, and, most damagingly, the short epilogue was missing. Without it, the book seemed to end with the entire crew drowned, which left British reviewers asking, quite reasonably, how the narrator had survived to tell the tale. The American edition, set from Melville's own manuscript, kept the epilogue, in which Ishmael floats to safety on a coffin turned life buoy. Harper printed 2,915 copies; Bentley only 500.

Where the story came from

Melville knew whaling first-hand. In January 1841 he sailed from Fairhaven, Massachusetts, on the whaler Acushnet, bound for the Pacific. He later deserted in the Marquesas Islands, an experience that fed his first book, Typee. On his travels he heard about the Essex, a Nantucket whaleship that was rammed and sunk by an enraged sperm whale in the Pacific in 1820, leaving its crew adrift for months. That true story, along with sailors' tales of a fearsome white whale, gave him the seed of his plot: Captain Ahab's obsessive hunt for the white whale that had taken his leg.

The book grew far beyond an adventure story while he was writing it. In 1850 Melville moved his family to a farmhouse called Arrowhead near Pittsfield, Massachusetts, and struck up an intense friendship with his neighbour, the novelist Nathaniel Hawthorne. Their conversations encouraged him to aim higher. Moby-Dick became a strange, sprawling mixture of sea yarn, Shakespearean tragedy, sermon, comedy, philosophy and whaling encyclopaedia, with whole chapters on the anatomy of whales and the business of turning them into oil. Melville dedicated it to Hawthorne, “in token of my admiration for his genius.”

Failure, then revival

The reception was disappointing. Some critics admired its power, but many found it baffling or overwritten, and sales were modest. A fire at Harper's warehouse in 1853 destroyed many unsold copies and the plates of his other books. Melville's later novels fared even worse, and he spent nearly two decades working as a customs inspector on the New York docks. When he died in 1891, his obituaries barely mentioned his great whale book.

Its rescue came a generation later. Around the centenary of Melville's birth in 1819, critics and scholars in America and Britain began to rediscover him, and by the 1920s Moby-Dick was being hailed as a masterpiece. Writers such as D. H. Lawrence championed it, and it has never been out of favour since. It has inspired films, most famously John Huston's 1956 version with Gregory Peck as Ahab, as well as operas, paintings, music and endless parodies. Even the coffee chain Starbucks takes its name from Ahab's first mate.

How it is remembered

There is no official holiday for a novel, but Moby-Dick has an unusually devoted following. Every year the New Bedford Whaling Museum in Massachusetts holds a marathon reading of the whole book, around 25 hours, with volunteers taking turns through the night. Melville's farmhouse, Arrowhead, is now a museum, and the Nantucket Historical Association keeps alive the story of the Essex. For anyone wanting to mark the day, the simplest tribute is to open the book at that first line and see how far the current carries you.
