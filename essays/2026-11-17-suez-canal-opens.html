Minor occasion · 17 November 2026

The Suez Canal Opens

At eight o’clock on the morning of 17 November 1869, a French paddle yacht called L’Aigle (The Eagle) steamed out of Port Said and into a 100-mile ditch across the Egyptian desert. On board was Empress Eugénie of France, wife of Napoleon III. Behind her came a long procession of ships, by the Suez Canal Authority’s count 77 of them, 50 of them warships. By evening L’Aigle had anchored at Ismailia, halfway along, and three days later she reached Suez and the Red Sea. The Suez Canal was open, and the voyage from Europe to Asia no longer had to go round Africa.

A Frenchman’s dream

The idea of linking the Mediterranean and the Red Sea was ancient, but the modern canal was the project of Ferdinand de Lesseps, a former French consul in Cairo. In 1854 he won a concession from Said Pasha, Egypt’s ruler under the Ottoman Empire, to cut a canal across the Isthmus of Suez. An international team of engineers drew up the plans, and in 1856 the Suez Canal Company was formed, with the right to run the canal for 99 years after it opened.

Digging began in April 1859. At first it was done by hand with picks and shovels, largely by Egyptian forced labourers; later, European workers arrived with dredgers and steam shovels. Labour disputes and a cholera epidemic slowed the work, and the canal finished four years behind schedule. The waters of the two seas met on 18 August 1869, three months before the grand opening.

A party for emperors

The ruler of Egypt by then, the Khedive Ismail, turned the opening into one of the great royal events of the age. When L’Aigle arrived at Port Said on 16 November, warships of the French, British, Austrian, Prussian, Norwegian, Danish, Italian and Egyptian navies dressed their ships and fired salutes. The Emperor of Austria, the Crown Prince of Prussia and the Prince and Princess of the Netherlands were among the guests, and a Te Deum was sung on a platform at the sea’s edge. At Ismailia the khedive put on lavish festivities.

Behind the pageantry, the passage was nervy. A French naval report described the canal as narrow and in places shallower than planned: just before one bend engineers had found only four metres of depth in early November, and an Egyptian corvette chosen to test the route had run aground several times. L’Aigle made it through by sticking to the deepest channels. When it opened, the canal was only about 25 feet deep and 72 feet wide at the bottom, and fewer than 500 ships used it in its first full year.

From shares to crisis

The canal soon grew into one of the world’s busiest shipping lanes, and Britain, which had been sceptical, became its biggest stakeholder. In 1875 Benjamin Disraeli’s government bought the Egyptian ruler’s shares, and in 1882 Britain invaded Egypt, beginning a long occupation. Even after the 1936 Anglo-Egyptian treaty made Egypt largely independent, Britain kept rights to protect the canal.

In July 1956 Egypt’s President Gamal Abdel Nasser nationalised the canal, hoping its tolls would pay for the Aswan High Dam. Israel invaded in late October, and British and French troops landed in early November. Under pressure from the United States and the United Nations they withdrew, and the Suez Crisis is often seen as the moment Britain’s days as a great imperial power visibly ended. The canal was closed again after the Six-Day War of 1967 and became a front line for eight years, until President Anwar Sadat reopened it in 1975.

The canal today

Today the canal is run by Egypt’s Suez Canal Authority and remains one of the world’s great trade arteries, carrying dozens of ships a day. Its fragility made headlines in March 2021, when the giant container ship Ever Given ran aground and blocked it for six days, and more recently traffic fell sharply when attacks on shipping in the Red Sea pushed many lines back to the long route round the Cape of Good Hope, the very journey the canal was built to avoid.

The 1869 opening left one famous cultural myth. Verdi’s opera Aida is often said to have been written for it. In fact it was commissioned by the khedive for Cairo’s new opera house and first performed in December 1871; the opera house itself opened in 1869 with Verdi’s Rigoletto.
