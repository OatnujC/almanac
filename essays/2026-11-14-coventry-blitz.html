The Coventry Blitz (1940)

On the night of 14 November 1940, at around 7.20pm, the first bombs fell on Coventry. By the time the all-clear sounded at about 6am the next morning, the city's medieval heart had been destroyed, its cathedral was a burnt-out shell, and hundreds of people were dead. It was one of the most destructive single raids on a British city of the Second World War, and its anniversary is remembered in Coventry every year, alongside a message of reconciliation that grew out of the ruins.

Why Coventry, and why that night?

Coventry was a city of engineering and manufacturing. Its factories, many of which had made bicycles and cars before the war, were turning out aircraft parts, engines and munitions, and they were packed close to the homes of the people who worked in them. It had already suffered a series of smaller raids from August 1940 that killed 176 civilians, according to the historian Frederick Taylor. But the operation planned for mid-November was on a completely different scale. The Germans codenamed it Moonlight Sonata, because it was timed for the full moon, which would light up the target.

The raid used a new radio navigation system, the X-Gerät, in which beams transmitted from the French coast crossed over the target, guiding pathfinder aircraft to drop incendiaries and mark the city for the waves of bombers behind them. GCHQ's own account notes that British intelligence had read Enigma decrypts warning of an unusual operation called Moonlight Sonata, but that the target was only confirmed on the afternoon of 14 November, when the beams were found to intersect over Coventry. Countermeasures failed. The persistent story that Winston Churchill knowingly sacrificed Coventry to protect the secret of Enigma has been examined in detail by historians and is generally regarded as a myth.

The night itself

According to the Diocese of Coventry, 515 German bombers took part. Over about 11 hours they dropped some 500 tonnes of high explosives, 30,000 incendiaries and 50 landmines. The city's water, gas, electricity and telephone networks were knocked out early, leaving firefighters struggling through cratered streets with little water. More than 200 fires burned across the city. At about 8pm the Cathedral Church of St Michael, a great medieval parish church that had become a cathedral in 1918, was hit by incendiaries and engulfed in flames.

The official death toll was 554, with 863 seriously injured, though the exact number of dead has never been precisely confirmed; other sources give 568, and some people were never accounted for. More than 43,000 homes, just over half the city's housing, were damaged or destroyed, along with a third of its factories. The devastation was so great that German propaganda coined a word for it: coventrieren, to “coventrate” a city. King George VI visited soon afterwards and was said to have been moved to tears by the ruins.

Father forgive

What happened next has given the anniversary its particular meaning. In the smoking ruins of the cathedral, the stonemason Jock Forbes noticed two charred roof beams that had fallen in the shape of a cross and bound them together. A local vicar, the Revd Arthur Wales, made another cross from three medieval roof nails, the first Cross of Nails. And the cathedral's provost, Richard Howard, chalked two words on the wall behind the altar: “Father forgive.” Not “forgive them”, but simply “Father forgive”, recognising that everyone shares in the wrongs that lead to war. Weeks later, in a BBC broadcast on Christmas Day 1940, he spoke of seeking not revenge but reconciliation.

Ruins and rebirth

After the war, Coventry made those words its mission. The city formed one of the first twinning links in the world with Stalingrad in 1944, and later with Dresden, itself devastated by Allied bombing in 1945. The ruins of the old cathedral were kept as a memorial, and beside them rose Basil Spence's modern cathedral, consecrated in May 1962; Benjamin Britten's War Requiem was written for the occasion. Crosses of Nails have since been given to churches and communities around the world, including in Germany, and the Community of the Cross of Nails now links partners working for peace across many countries.

How it is remembered

Every year on 14 November, Coventry holds services and moments of remembrance in the cathedral ruins, where the words “Father forgive” are still inscribed behind the altar. Each weekday at noon the cathedral says the Coventry Litany of Reconciliation. Eighty-six years on, the anniversary is both a memorial to the people who died and a reminder of one city's choice to answer destruction with forgiveness.
