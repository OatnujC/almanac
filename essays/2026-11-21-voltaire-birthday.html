Minor occasion · 21 November 2026

Voltaire’s birthday

Voltaire’s conventional birthday is 21 November 1694, making today the 332nd anniversary. Born François-Marie Arouet in Paris, he became one of the best-known writers of the French Enlightenment. There is a small but important qualification to the date: Oxford’s Dictionary of National Biography says the twenty-first follows the baptismal notice, while Voltaire himself claimed a different birth date. An almanac should preserve that uncertainty rather than conceal it. Today is a literary anniversary based on the accepted date, not an official worldwide observance.

For British readers, Voltaire’s time in England provides a particularly interesting connection. The Voltaire Foundation describes his encounter with English writing, including Swift’s Gulliver’s Travels and Addison’s Spectator. England gave him material for a book first published in English in London in 1733 as Letters Concerning the English Nation. The connection runs both ways: a French writer learned from English culture, then helped turn that experience into something readers elsewhere could discuss. Ideas moved through books, languages and personal encounters, rather than staying neatly inside national borders.

His best-known fiction, Candide, appeared in 1759. The Folger Shakespeare Library’s account begins with a young man learning from Dr Pangloss that everything leads towards the best outcome. Experience soon puts that comforting teaching under pressure. The story tests a philosophical explanation against the world’s cruelty and absurdity, using comedy to make the argument difficult to ignore. Reading it today does not require agreeing with every judgement made by its author. It asks the reader to notice the distance between an attractive theory and the lives that theory claims to explain.

The book’s early publication history is revealing too. Oxford scholar Nicholas Cronk describes a title page presenting it as a translation from the German of a fictitious Dr Ralph, without naming Voltaire, a publisher or a place of publication. Numerous printings followed during 1759, including an early London edition issued by John Nourse. Such details remind us that a famous classic was once a new and potentially troublesome work moving through a particular publishing world. Its wit reached readers through printers and booksellers as well as through the author’s pen.

Voltaire’s public activity also extended beyond fiction. The Voltaire Foundation recalls his campaign to clear the name of Jean Calas, a case that helped bring forth the Treatise on Tolerance in 1763. This belongs to his reputation as a writer who intervened in public affairs. It is better approached through the specific case and the work it produced than through a convenient quotation passed around without a source. His continuing importance rests on texts, arguments and actions that can be examined, rather than the reassuring idea that a celebrated thinker must always have been right.

In Britain today, the Oxford-based Voltaire Foundation continues scholarly work on his writing, while libraries and collections around the world preserve his books and their histories. Those are ongoing forms of attention, not a claim about a birthday festival taking place this afternoon. A good personal observance would be to read a chapter of Candide or explore the story of the English Letters. The birthday provides the prompt; the interest lies in encountering the writing itself. More than three centuries after the accepted birth date, Voltaire still offers a lively example of how literature can travel, provoke and ask awkward questions.
