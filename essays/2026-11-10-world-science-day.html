Major occasion · Tuesday 10 November 2026

World Science Day for Peace and Development

Today is World Science Day for Peace and Development, a United Nations day held every 10 November and led by UNESCO, the UN's agency for education, science and culture. Its aim is simple and rather lovely: to remind us that science belongs to everyone, that it shapes our daily lives, and that it can be a force for peace as well as progress. The 2026 theme is "Common ground through science diplomacy", a focus on how scientists can keep talking and working together across borders even when governments find that hard.

Where it came from

The day grew out of the World Conference on Science, held in Budapest in 1999 by UNESCO and the International Council for Science (now part of the International Science Council). The conference produced a declaration on science and the use of scientific knowledge, a sort of charter for science in the twenty-first century, which argued that science should serve all of humanity, that its benefits should be shared fairly, and that the public should be part of the conversation about where research is heading.

To keep that commitment alive, UNESCO's General Conference proclaimed World Science Day for Peace and Development in 2001. It was first celebrated on 10 November 2002 and has been held on the same date every year since. There is no famous scientific event behind the date; it is a fixed observance chosen by UNESCO, so it never moves.

What the day is for

UNESCO sets out a handful of goals for the day. It wants to strengthen public awareness of the role science plays in building peaceful and sustainable societies. It wants to encourage national and international solidarity so that knowledge is shared rather than hoarded. It wants countries to renew their commitment to using science for the benefit of society. And it wants to draw attention to the challenges science faces, and to raise support for scientific work.

Behind those goals is a worry as old as modern science itself. The same discoveries that cure disease and grow more food can also build weapons or damage the environment. The day's emphasis on "peace and development" is a reminder that the direction science takes is a choice societies make.

Science diplomacy

The 2026 theme looks at a quieter side of science: its knack for building bridges. Problems like climate change, pandemics, the loss of wildlife and new technologies do not respect borders, and no single country can solve them alone. Scientists often keep working together even when political relations are strained, and those working relationships can open doors for wider dialogue.

There are some well-known examples. CERN, the particle physics laboratory near Geneva, was set up in the 1950s partly with UNESCO's encouragement, to help a Europe still recovering from war pool its talent and resources; scientists from many nations, including some whose governments were at odds, have worked side by side there ever since. More recently SESAME, a particle accelerator in Jordan established under UNESCO's auspices, has brought together researchers from across the Middle East, including countries with tense or no formal relations. The International Science Council is encouraging its members to hold talks, panels, workshops and other events on science diplomacy on or around today.

How it is marked

World Science Day is not a public holiday anywhere; it is a day for events. Around the world, universities, museums, schools and research institutes use it for open days, public lectures, classroom discussions about how science touches everyday life, visits from scientists, and science fairs. UNESCO and its national commissions organise discussions, and some governments have used the day to announce new science policies or funding.

It is also a good excuse to do something small yourself: visit a science museum, read about a discovery you never quite understood, or ask a scientist friend what they are working on. The point of the day is that science is not just for laboratories; it is for curiosity, conversation and the common good.

A busy season for science days

World Science Day sits among several related observances. UNESCO also marks International Day of Women and Girls in Science each February, and the UN often declares international years for scientific themes, such as the International Year of Quantum Science and Technology in 2025. Together they reflect the same idea at the heart of today: that knowledge grows best when it is shared.
