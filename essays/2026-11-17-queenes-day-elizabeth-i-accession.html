Queene’s Day: Elizabeth I’s Accession Day

Long before Bonfire Night became the great English November blaze, there was another. For around two centuries, 17 November was Queen Elizabeth’s Day, or Queene’s Day, when church bells rang across England in thanks for the accession of Elizabeth I. Today marks 468 years since she came to the throne.

Why this date

Elizabeth became queen on 17 November 1558, the day her half-sister, Mary I, died. Mary had restored Catholicism in England; Elizabeth would restore the Protestant Church of England. That religious turn is the key to everything that followed, because the anniversary was never just a royal birthday party. It was a Protestant thanksgiving for a Protestant queen.

From bells to a ‘holy day’

In 1568, the tenth anniversary of the reign, parishes rang their bells to mark the day. Historians used to see the celebrations as a spontaneous, popular cult of the queen, but a recent study in the journal History, which traces the day through revisions of the Church of England’s prayer-book calendar, argues that it began and stayed a religious occasion, shaped by the bishops and the religious politics of the Elizabethan state. Either way, the timing is telling. Celebrations spread quickly from about 1570, after the Northern Rebellion of 1569 and the Pope’s excommunication of Elizabeth in February 1570, and probably after the St Bartholomew’s Day massacre of French Protestants in 1572. Ringing bells for the queen became a way of showing which side you were on. Some churchwardens’ accounts and town records began calling it ‘the Queen’s holy day’.

In 1576 Edmund Grindal, Archbishop of Canterbury, issued an official ‘Form of Prayer with Thanksgiving’ for the day, used until 1602. Ministers and congregations recited responses giving thanks for the queen’s deliverance from her enemies, and the prayers likened Elizabeth to the reforming kings of the Old Testament, such as Josiah and Hezekiah.

The Accession Day tilts

At court the day became a dazzling show. From at least 1581 there were Accession Day tilts at Whitehall: jousting matches wrapped in costume, poetry and pageantry. A German visitor, Lupold von Wedel, described the tilt of 1584. The queen and her ladies watched from the windows of a long room overlooking the tiltyard, while thousands of spectators paid 12 pence each for a place in the stands. Knights arrived with servants dressed as ‘savages’ or wearing horse manes, in carriages pulled by horses dressed as elephants or that seemed to move by themselves. Before each pair rode, a servant climbed the stairs to the queen and delivered verses or a comic speech, then presented a costly gift. The jousting ran until five in the afternoon, when the Earl of Leicester, Master of the Horse, called a halt. The tilts inspired verse by Edmund Spenser and Philip Sidney, and a famous Nicholas Hilliard miniature shows George Clifford, Earl of Cumberland, in his tilt costume as the Knight of Pendragon Castle.

Long after the queen

Elizabeth died in 1603, but her day did not. Through the 17th and into the 18th century she was remembered as a Protestant heroine, and 17 November became a day of Protestant rejoicing and anti-Catholic feeling, with sermons, processions and the burning of the Pope and the Devil in effigy. After the Great Fire of London in 1666, which many blamed on Catholics, the London celebrations turned rowdy and political. They peaked during the Exclusion Crisis of 1679–81, when political clubs paid for huge processions and bonfires to stir up feeling against the Catholic heir, James, Duke of York. Over time Bonfire Night on 5 November, with its own anti-Catholic origins, absorbed most of that energy, and Queene’s Day faded.

A Devon revival

Almost everywhere, that is. In 2005 the village of Berry Pomeroy in south Devon brought the tradition back. Its version begins with evensong at St Mary’s Church and ends with a bonfire in the next-door field, on which an effigy of the Devil, rather than the Pope, is burned. It is a small, local affair, but it means that somewhere in England Elizabeth’s accession is still being marked with fire, as it was more than 400 years ago.
