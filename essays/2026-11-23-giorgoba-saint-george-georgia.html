Major occasion · 23 November 2026

Giorgoba: St George’s Day in Georgia

Georgia keeps one of its best-loved saints' days on 23 November. Giorgoba honours St George, and its November observance is a public holiday. In 2026 the date falls on a Monday. The day belongs to both the religious calendar and the country's shared public life: churches keep the feast, while the holiday gives people time for family and community. The Georgian honorary consulate's public-holiday list confirms the date under the country's labour legislation.

There are two principal Georgian celebrations of St George, and the distinction explains why an English reader may find November surprising. The spring feast falls on 6 May in the civil calendar, corresponding to 23 April in the Julian calendar. The autumn feast is 23 November, corresponding to 10 November in the Julian calendar. Georgian Public Broadcaster identifies the latter with the saint's torture on the wheel. These are separate commemorations within the same tradition, rather than rival guesses about one festival's date.

The wheel belongs to the Christian account of George's suffering under persecution. It is best described as church tradition, rather than as an incident whose every detail can be independently reconstructed. The meaning for worshippers is the saint's steadfastness in faith despite violence and demands to renounce it. This careful distinction allows the story to retain its religious force without treating devotional narrative and modern historical evidence as interchangeable kinds of knowledge.

An academic study of warrior saints in Georgian medieval art shows that the two George feasts were already present in medieval Georgian church calendars. It also notes the special national importance acquired by the November commemoration. That long history helps explain why Giorgoba feels rooted rather than newly created. Saints' days can become meeting places for personal devotion, local identity and a country's understanding of itself. George is a particularly strong example of those layers sitting together in Georgian culture.

Worship is central to the observance. Georgian reporting describes festive liturgies and prayers in churches across the country. Around that religious centre, families and friends may gather, and the name Giorgi gives the feast an especially personal resonance for those who bear it. Customs vary, so there is no need to imagine that every household eats one prescribed meal or follows an identical programme. A national day off establishes common time; what people do with that time remains shaped by belief, family and place.

Contemporary celebrations also include music and hospitality. A published 2026 listing for the Giorgobistve venue in Tbilisi explicitly invites people to gather on 23 November for Giorgoba with music, friends and food. That is a specific example of how the feast reaches beyond a church service, rather than evidence for a universal Georgian custom. It shows how an old religious date can continue to make space for an ordinary social evening. The sacred and the sociable need not be separated into entirely different days.

For people in the UK, the comparison with England's April St George's Day is useful only as a starting point. Georgia's November feast has its own calendar, devotional emphasis and public-holiday status. A reader here might learn about Georgian Christian art, listen to Georgian music or ask a Georgian friend what Giorgoba means in their family. Those are invitations to take an interest, not claims that a particular British event is scheduled today. The pleasure of an almanac is finding that a familiar saint can open a door into a different country's year.
