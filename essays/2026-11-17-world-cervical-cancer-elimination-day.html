Major occasion · 17 November 2026

World Cervical Cancer Elimination Day

Today is only the second time the world has marked World Cervical Cancer Elimination Day, but it carries one of the boldest ambitions in public health: to make cervical cancer the first cancer ever eliminated as a public health problem. The day is a newcomer to the calendar, and its arrival has even moved a much older observance out of the way.

Why 17 November?

The date is an anniversary. On 17 November 2020 the World Health Organization launched its Global Strategy to Accelerate the Elimination of Cervical Cancer. Earlier that year the World Health Assembly, the yearly meeting of the world’s health ministers, had passed a resolution backing the plan, which WHO described as the first time 194 countries had committed to eliminating a cancer. The launch was marked with a global day of action, and monuments from Niagara Falls to the Dubai Frame were lit up in teal, the colour of cervical cancer awareness.

For a few years 17 November was kept informally as a day of action. Then, in May 2025, the 78th World Health Assembly adopted resolution WHA78.8, formally designating 17 November each year as World Cervical Cancer Elimination Day. South Africa and Nigeria led the member states pushing for it. The first official observance was on 17 November 2025.

There was one complication. 17 November had for years been World Prematurity Day, the purple-themed day for babies born too soon, which began in 2008. When WHO brought World Prematurity Day into its official calendar, it gave it a new date so the two causes would not compete. From 2026, World Prematurity Day is kept on 15 November, and 17 November belongs to the cervical cancer campaign.

What ‘elimination’ means

Eliminating a cancer sounds impossible, and in the sense of wiping it out completely, it is. WHO’s definition is more practical: a country has eliminated cervical cancer as a public health problem when it keeps the rate below four new cases per 100,000 women per year. That is far below today’s rates in most of the world.

It is possible because cervical cancer is unusual. Almost all cases are caused by long-lasting infection with certain types of human papillomavirus (HPV), a very common virus passed on through sexual contact. That gives three clear points of attack: a vaccine that prevents the infection, screening tests that find the virus or early cell changes years before cancer develops, and simple treatments for those pre-cancers. As WHO’s strategy put it, the disease is preventable, and curable if caught early, yet it is still the fourth most common cancer in women worldwide.

The 90–70–90 targets

The strategy turns this into three targets that every country is meant to hit by 2030. First, 90% of girls fully vaccinated against HPV by the age of 15. Second, 70% of women screened with a high-performance test by 35 and again by 45. Third, 90% of women found to have cervical disease treated, meaning 90% of those with pre-cancer treated and 90% of those with invasive cancer properly managed. Hitting all three, WHO estimated at the launch, could cut new cases by more than 40% and prevent 5 million deaths by 2050.

The case is economic as well as medical. WHO estimated at the launch that every dollar invested would return about US$3.20 to the economy through women staying in work, rising to about US$26 once the wider benefits to families and communities are counted.

A question of fairness

The burden is very unequal. At the launch WHO reported about 570,000 new cases and 311,000 deaths a year (2018 figures), projected to rise to 700,000 cases and 400,000 deaths by 2030 without more action. In low- and middle-income countries incidence was nearly twice as high, and death rates three times as high, as in rich countries, largely because vaccination, screening and treatment are harder to reach. WHO Assistant Director-General Dr Princess Nothemba Simelela called the fight against cervical cancer a fight for women’s rights, describing the toll as the result of decades of neglect.

That is why so much of the day’s campaigning is about access: cheaper HPV tests, the WHO-backed move to a single-dose HPV vaccine schedule, which makes school programmes far easier to run, and ‘screen-and-treat’ approaches where a woman can be tested and treated in one visit rather than having to come back.

Closer to home

The UK is one of the countries often mentioned as on track. Its school HPV programme began for girls in 2008 and was extended to boys in 2019, and the NHS cervical screening programme now uses HPV testing as its first test. NHS England has set itself the goal of eliminating cervical cancer by 2040. Since 2023 the routine school programme has used a single dose. Australia, an early adopter of HPV vaccination, aims to get there even sooner, by 2035.

How it is marked

Expect teal lighting on landmarks, health ministries announcing vaccination drives or new screening programmes, and charities urging people to book their screening. WHO uses the day to publish progress updates, and this year’s message is that countries are expanding screening and treatment and bringing elimination closer. For most people the practical takeaway is modest: if you are due a smear test, book it, and if you have children of the right age, the school HPV jab is part of what this day is about.

It is a young observance, but its premise is quietly historic. Never before has the world agreed on a date, a definition and a deadline for getting rid of a cancer.
