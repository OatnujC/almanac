Minor occasion · 17 November 2026

Polytechneio: the Athens Polytechnic Uprising

If you were in central Athens today you would find the metro stations around Syntagma shut from the afternoon, thousands of police on the streets and the gates of an old university building on Patission Street piled with carnations. 17 November is Polytechneio, the anniversary of the Athens Polytechnic uprising of 1973, and this year is the 53rd.

Greece under the colonels

On 21 April 1967 a group of army officers seized power in Greece. The regime, usually called the junta or ‘the colonels’, banned political parties, censored the press and sent opponents into internal exile, put them on trial in emergency courts or tortured them. Through 1973 opposition grew bolder, much of it led by students. In February students gathered at the National Technical University of Athens (NTUA), the Polytechnic, and were attacked by police inside the campus. Days later they occupied the Athens Law School, and did so again in March. Each protest was put down by force. On 4 November a memorial service for the statesman George Papandreou turned into another demonstration.

Three days in November

On Wednesday 14 November 1973 a general meeting of student unions rejected the government’s rules for student elections and decided to occupy the Polytechnic, on the corner of Patission and Stournari Streets, about a hundred metres from the National Archaeological Museum. Crowds of supporters gathered outside. The students set up a makeshift radio station that called on the people of Athens to join them, broadcasting slogans for bread, education and freedom. Over the next two days workers, farmers and ordinary Athenians rallied to them, and the sit-in grew into an open revolt against the regime. On the evening of 16 November police fired shots, street fights broke out across central Athens, and a curfew was declared.

Tanks appeared after midnight. A makeshift hospital inside the Polytechnic took in the wounded and the dead. The student radio pleaded with the soldiers not to become murderers: ‘Soldiers, we are your brothers.’ The army gave those inside twenty minutes to leave. Then, at around 3am on Saturday 17 November, a tank drove through the iron main gate, which still had people clinging to it. Shots were fired, plain-clothes police made arrests, and by about 3.20am the Polytechnic was empty. Several demonstrators were killed and many more arrested and tortured for months in military prisons; the full death toll during and after the uprising has been studied and argued over ever since.

The regime first claimed that nothing had happened. That story collapsed thanks largely to the photojournalist Aristotle Sarrikostas, whose pictures captured the tank coming through the gate, and a 35-second film shot by a Dutch cameraman. The uprising did not bring the junta down at once; in fact a harder-line faction took over within days. But it badly damaged the regime, and after the Cyprus disaster of July 1974 the dictatorship collapsed and democracy returned.

How it is marked today

Polytechneio is a holiday for all schools and universities in Greece, and the NTUA holds three days of commemoration from 15 to 17 November. The Polytechnic’s gates are opened, and students, schoolchildren, unions and families file in to lay flowers on a memorial by the sculptor Memos Makris and on the twisted old iron gate the tank smashed, now kept as a monument in the courtyard. Schools hold assemblies where pupils read the uprising’s slogans and poems.

The day ends with a march. Student associations gather at the Propylaea of the University of Athens and walk to the United States Embassy, a route chosen because many Greeks saw Washington as having backed the junta. In Thessaloniki and other cities there are parallel rallies. The marches are mostly peaceful but sometimes end in clashes, which is why Athens police deploy several thousand officers each year; for today they announced more than 5,000, with drones and a helicopter.

The memory is not frozen. Some Greeks honour the uprising as a pure act of courage, others criticise how its legacy has been claimed by political parties. But its slogans, especially ‘Bread, Education, Freedom’, still appear on banners every November, and for many Greek families 17 November is when they explain to their children what it was like to live without democracy.
