Minor occasion · Monday 9 November 2026

Amavasya (new moon day, Diwali week)

Amavasya is the Hindu name for the new moon day, the dark night of each lunar month when the moon cannot be seen. It comes round roughly every 29 and a half days, so it is hardly rare. But this month’s Amavasya is a special one. It is the new moon of Diwali, the night on which Lakshmi Puja was celebrated on Sunday 8 November, and in India it ran on into the morning of Monday 9 November. That overlap gives Diwali week an unusual gap this year, and it also makes Monday 9 November a Somvati Amavasya, a new moon on a Monday, which many Hindus regard as especially auspicious.

What the word means

Amavasya comes from Sanskrit. It is usually explained as coming from words meaning “together” and “to dwell”: on this day the sun and moon “dwell together”, sitting at the same longitude in the sky, so the side of the moon facing us is unlit. It is the opposite of Purnima, the full moon. In some regional calendars the month ends with Amavasya (the amanta system, common in western and southern India); in others it ends with Purnima (the purnimanta system, common in the north). That is why the same days of Diwali can be described as falling at the end of Ashvin in one region and in the dark half of Kartika in another. The days are the same; only the month’s label differs.

Why the dates move: lunar days

The Hindu calendar counts lunar days called tithis. A tithi is the time the moon takes to gain 12 degrees on the sun, so there are 30 in a lunar month. Because the moon’s speed varies, a tithi can be shorter or longer than a solar day, and it can begin at any hour. So a festival is not fixed to a date on the wall calendar; it is tied to a tithi, and the rules then say which part of the day the tithi must cover.

That is exactly what happened this year. According to the widely used Drik Panchang calculations for New Delhi, the Diwali Amavasya began at 11.27am on Sunday 8 November and ended at 12.31pm on Monday 9 November, Indian time. Lakshmi Puja is performed in the early evening while Amavasya is in force, so the main night of Diwali was Sunday. But the Amavasya was still running at sunrise on Monday, and by the usual rule a day belongs to the tithi in force at sunrise. So Monday is listed as Amavasya too.

The gap in Diwali week

In most years the five days of Diwali fall on five consecutive dates: Dhanteras, Naraka Chaturdashi, Lakshmi Puja, Govardhan Puja and Bhai Dooj. In 2026 they do not. Govardhan Puja, the day of the Annakut “mountain of food” offered to Krishna, is kept on the first day of the bright fortnight, Pratipada, when it holds at sunrise. Pratipada began at 12.31pm on Monday 9 November in New Delhi, so it first meets a sunrise on Tuesday. In India, then, Govardhan Puja falls on Tuesday 10 November, which is also the Gujarati New Year, and Bhai Dooj, the festival of brothers and sisters, on Wednesday 11 November.

Because the moment the tithi changes is fixed for the whole world, but sunrise is not, some calendars made for places outside India give these days a day earlier. Panchang timings also vary by city. If you keep these festivals, it is worth checking your own temple’s or community’s calendar rather than relying on a single date.

A day for the ancestors

Amavasya has its own character apart from Diwali. It is particularly associated with the ancestors, the pitrs. Many Hindus make offerings of water and black sesame seeds for the dead on Amavasya, perform shraddha rites, and give food and money to those in need. Bathing in a sacred river, where possible, is a traditional observance. The most important of these ancestral new moons is Mahalaya Amavasya, which closes the fortnight of Pitru Paksha in the autumn, a few weeks before Diwali.

Some people fast on Amavasya. Others regard it as a less auspicious time to begin new ventures, because the moon is dark, while at the same time it is the night chosen for some of the year’s most important worship: Lakshmi Puja at Diwali, and in Bengal, Odisha and Assam, the midnight worship of the goddess Kali on the same night.

Somvati Amavasya

When the new moon falls on a Monday, it is called Somvati Amavasya. Monday, Somvar, is associated with the moon (Soma) and with Shiva, so the combination is held to be especially powerful. It does not happen often, because the lunar month and the seven-day week drift against each other; it comes round only a few times a year. On Somvati Amavasya, many people bathe in rivers such as the Ganges at dawn, worship Shiva, and give to charity. In some traditions married women fast and pray for their husbands’ wellbeing, and walk around a peepal tree, tying thread around it.

This year the Somvati Amavasya also falls in the middle of the Diwali festival, which is unusual. After the noise and lights of Sunday night, it gives the week a quieter day: a morning bath, remembering the ancestors, giving food away, and getting ready for Govardhan Puja and Bhai Dooj later in the week.

A note on the sky

The astronomical new moon, the exact instant when the sun and moon line up, and the Amavasya tithi are related but not identical, and neither is the same as the first visible crescent that some other calendars use. Festival dates follow the traditional rules for tithis, not the astronomers’ tables alone. That is why careful almanacs give times as well as dates, and why a single Diwali can spread itself, as it has this year, across six days of the Gregorian calendar.
