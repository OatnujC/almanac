Major occasion · 16 November 2026

The First Salute: Statia Day at 250

Today a tiny volcanic island in the north-eastern Caribbean, barely 21 square kilometres in size and home to around 3,300 people, finds itself at the centre of one of the more charming footnotes of world history. Sint Eustatius, known to everyone who lives there simply as Statia, celebrates Statia Day, and this year it is no ordinary Statia Day: it is exactly 250 years since the guns of the island's Fort Oranje fired what has become known as the First Salute to the United States of America.

What happened on 16 November 1776

In July 1776, thirteen British colonies in North America declared their independence. Few people at the time expected the rebellion to last, and no European power had any wish to be seen recognising it. On 16 November that year, the American brig Andrew Doria, a small warship of 14 guns commanded by Captain Isaiah Robinson, sailed into the anchorage below Fort Oranje flying the Continental Colours, the striped flag the rebels were then using. She had come to buy weapons and gunpowder, which the island sold in great quantities.

As was the custom when entering a foreign port, the Andrew Doria fired a salute. The question for the island's governor, Johannes de Graaff, was whether to reply. Answering would mean acknowledging the flag of a people at war with Britain, a powerful neighbour. De Graaff ordered the fort's cannon to return the greeting. According to the usual account, Robinson fired thirteen guns, one for each colony, and the fort replied with eleven, the smaller number then customary when answering a flag that was not of a fully recognised sovereign state. Once the ship was docked, the governor received the captain with honours, and the Americans on the island celebrated. To the Americans, this was the first time an official of a foreign power had formally saluted their flag.

It was only a few months after the Declaration of Independence, and the gesture carried weight far beyond its few rounds of gunpowder. The Andrew Doria is also said to have brought a copy of the Declaration of Independence to the governor. The Dutch government back home, under heavy British pressure, quickly distanced itself from what had happened, and de Graaff was summoned to explain himself. But the salute had been fired, and the story stuck.

Why a small island mattered so much

In the 18th century Statia was nicknamed the Golden Rock. Declared a duty-free port in 1756, it had become one of the busiest trading places in the Americas, its waterfront lined with warehouses where goods from across the Atlantic world changed hands. That wealth had a dark side: the island was also a transit point in the transatlantic and inter-island slave trade, and its plantations were worked by enslaved Africans, a history that Statians today remember alongside the story of the salute.

During the American Revolution the island was one of the main sources of arms and ammunition for the rebels. Britain was furious. Trade between Statia and the Americans was one of the main reasons Britain declared war on the Dutch Republic in 1780, starting the Fourth Anglo-Dutch War. In February 1781 Admiral George Rodney arrived with a fleet and 3,000 troops; the governor, who did not yet know war had been declared, surrendered after firing two shots for honour's sake. Rodney seized the island's goods and property and singled out its Jewish merchants for expulsion, actions that Edmund Burke condemned in Parliament. The French recaptured the island later that year, and it returned to the Dutch in 1784, but its glory days as a trading hub never came back. Its population fell from more than 8,000 in 1790 to under a thousand by the mid-20th century.

How the salute has been remembered

The United States has never quite forgotten the gesture. In 1939 President Franklin D. Roosevelt visited the island aboard the USS Houston and presented a bronze plaque, still mounted at Fort Oranje, recording that here the sovereignty of the United States was first formally acknowledged to a national vessel by a foreign official. In 1991 President George H. W. Bush described it as the first salute ever given by a foreign power to the flag of the United States. The historian Barbara Tuchman took the event as the starting point and title for her 1988 book The First Salute. Strictly speaking, historians have pointed out that there were other early salutes and acknowledgements, and the Dutch Republic did not formally recognise the United States until 1782, but the Statia salute has the strongest popular claim to being first.

Statia Day itself is the island's annual holiday, held each 16 November in honour of the salute. Since 10 October 2010, when the Netherlands Antilles was dissolved, Statia has been a special municipality of the Netherlands, so the day is also a celebration of the island's identity within the Dutch kingdom. The island adopted its own flag and coat of arms in 2004. The arms show the Golden Rock for the past, Fort Oranje for the present and an angelfish for the future, under the motto Superba et Confidens, proud and confident.

Today's 250th anniversary

Because the United States is marking 250 years of independence in 2026, Statia has been planning a celebration to match. A week of free evening events on Fort Oranjestraat, under the theme "The Statia Spirit Perseveres, Saluting 250 Years", began on 10 November with storytelling under the stars, followed by a gospel night, a youth night, a culture night and an all-white evening on the rock, with a light and music show over the bay last night. This morning at 7am local time (11am in the UK) the official commemoration takes place at Fort Oranje, with seats filling from before dawn. The Royal House of the Netherlands says King Willem-Alexander is attending, together with the Dutch Prime Minister and the State Secretary for Kingdom Relations. Tonight the island parties on Fort Oranjestraat with the Barbadian soca band Edwin Yearwood and Krosfyah and local acts, closing with fireworks.

It is a lovely thought that, two and a half centuries on, the world still remembers that a few cannon fired from a small rock in the Caribbean were the first to say hello to a brand-new country. As the island's tourism foundation puts it, for residents and visitors this will be a Statia Day unlike any in living memory.
