Major occasion · 18 November 2026

Morocco’s Independence Day

Today is Independence Day in Morocco, a national public holiday known in Arabic as Eid al-Istiqlal and in French as the Fête de l’Indépendance. Streets and balconies go red and green with the national flag, official ceremonies include a salute to the flag, and families gather. The date commemorates an announcement Sultan Mohammed V made on 18 November 1955, two days after he returned from more than two years in exile. This year marks the 71st anniversary of that speech.

A date with more than one meaning

18 November is not the day Morocco’s independence was recognised in law. France formally recognised Moroccan independence on 2 March 1956, more than three months later, and Spain followed with a joint declaration on 7 April. The holiday remembers the moment the sultan, newly home, declared the end of the protectorate era and the beginning of freedom. The date already belonged to the monarchy: Mohammed V, then known as Sultan Mohammed Ben Youssef, had ascended the throne on 18 November 1927. For years that anniversary was kept as Throne Day. After his return it became the date of Independence Day as well.

The protectorate

Morocco had been a sovereign kingdom for centuries before European powers divided it. On 30 March 1912 Sultan Moulay Abdelhafid signed the Treaty of Fez, which established a French protectorate. A Franco-Spanish convention later that year defined Spain’s zones of influence. France administered the centre of the country through a resident-general. Spain administered the north from Tetouan and held territories in the south. Tangier was placed under a special international regime. The sultan and some local institutions remained, but real power over administration, security and political life sat with the French and Spanish authorities.

Resistance never disappeared. It flared in different regions and at different times: in the Middle Atlas, in the Rif, where fighters led by Abdelkrim El Khattabi defeated Spanish forces at the Battle of Annual in 1921, and later in the south. Political organisation grew alongside the fighting. On 11 January 1944 Moroccan nationalists issued the Manifesto of Independence, a demand for full sovereignty that Morocco still commemorates as a separate holiday. The sultan increasingly aligned himself with the nationalist cause, which made him intolerable to the residency in Rabat.

Exile and return

On 20 August 1953 the French authorities deposed Mohammed V and sent him into exile, first to Corsica and then to Madagascar, replacing him with a more compliant relative. The move backfired. Deposing the sultan turned him into a national symbol, and the years that followed are remembered in Morocco as the Revolution of the King and the People, marked every 20 August. Armed resistance and political pressure mounted until France agreed to bring him home.

Mohammed V landed in Morocco on 16 November 1955 to large crowds. Two days later, on 18 November, he gave his first throne speech since the exile and announced the end of the protectorate and trusteeship system. The period is linked to a famous phrase attributed to him: that the country had returned from the lesser jihad, the struggle for liberation, to the greater jihad, the harder work of building the nation. Official accounts differ on whether he said this on the day he landed or in the 18 November speech, so it is best treated as a saying of the return rather than a line pinned to a single hour. The meaning is clear enough. Winning freedom, he was saying, was only the start.

Independence, and what followed

The legal end of the French protectorate came with the Moroccan–French declaration of 2 March 1956. Spain’s joint declaration of 7 April followed, and Mohammed V visited Tetouan to mark the reunion of the north with the rest of the country. Even then, not every territory under Spanish control returned at once. Tarfaya was recovered in 1958 and Sidi Ifni in 1969. Later recoveries in the south are commemorated on other dates in the national calendar, including the Green March of 6 November 1975. Independence, in the official Moroccan telling, was a process rather than a single signature.

Mohammed V died in February 1961 and was succeeded by his son, Hassan II, and later by the present king, Mohammed VI. The holiday itself is kept simply. Schools and most businesses close. Officials hold flag-raising ceremonies in the provinces. In Rabat, wreaths are traditionally laid at Mohammed V’s mausoleum. Families spend the day together, and the red-green flag appears on houses, shops and public buildings. The history beneath the flags is the point: a sultan sent away, a country that refused to accept the substitution, and a speech, delivered on the anniversary of his own accession, that told Moroccans their protectorate era was over.
