Elizabeth and Philip’s wedding: 79 years on

Seventy-nine years ago today, on 20 November 1947, Princess Elizabeth married Lieutenant Philip Mountbatten at Westminster Abbey. She was not yet queen; her accession would come in 1952. The wedding belongs to the history of postwar Britain, when a national occasion could carry feelings far beyond the lives of the two people at its centre. Westminster Abbey describes the day as one of rejoicing after years of war. Its anniversary now offers a way to revisit that moment through records rather than through the assumption that the later reign had already begun.

The Royal Family’s archive gives the sequence clearly. Elizabeth marked her twenty-first birthday in April 1947 during the royal visit to South Africa. The engagement was announced on 9 July, and the marriage followed on 20 November. The order of service preserved by Westminster Abbey gives the day as Thursday and the time as 11.30am. That contemporary document is a useful anchor. It names the bride as Princess Elizabeth and the bridegroom as a Royal Navy lieutenant, placing the ceremony within the titles and circumstances of its own year.

The setting carried a longer family history. Elizabeth had attended her father George VI’s coronation at the Abbey in 1937. The same church would host her own coronation in 1953. Looking at those events together shows how a building can gather personal, religious and national meanings over a lifetime. For the wedding, however, the essential ceremony concerned marriage vows. Later anniversary services would return to that promise and give thanks for the years that followed, connecting a private commitment with an unusually public life.

The material details are well preserved. Norman Hartnell designed an ivory silk dress decorated with hand-embroidered pearls. The bouquet included myrtle from a plant associated with Queen Victoria’s wedding tradition, and the ring was made from Welsh gold. The Royal Family’s account also records ingredients given by Australian Girl Guides for the official wedding cake. Such objects connect the central ceremony with craftspeople, places and gifts beyond the Abbey. They help explain how the occasion became something people could feel involved in, even at a distance.

The following day Elizabeth’s bouquet was returned to the Abbey and laid on the Grave of the Unknown Warrior, continuing a tradition begun by her mother. Anniversaries later brought their own acts of remembrance and celebration. Westminster Abbey records services for the couple’s Silver, Golden and Diamond anniversaries, while its bells marked the seventieth anniversary in 2017 with a full peal. These are documented historical commemorations, not announcements of a special event for today. No unverified programme is needed to give the 2026 anniversary its place in the calendar.

For JT, a gentle way to mark the date is to look at the original order of service or photographs and notice the distinction between what people knew in 1947 and what later generations know. The princess in those records had not yet become the long-reigning monarch of familiar memory. The date can also prompt family stories about postwar weddings and the hopes attached to them. Across Britain and beyond, the surviving records make a public event accessible at a smaller scale: a morning service, particular objects, a bouquet returned, and promises whose significance unfolded over time.
