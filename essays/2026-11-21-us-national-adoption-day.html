Minor occasion · 21 November 2026

National Adoption Day in the United States

National Adoption Day in the United States falls on Saturday 21 November in 2026. It brings courts, organisations and communities together to raise awareness of children waiting to be adopted from foster care and to mark adoptions being finalised. It is an American occasion, even though the questions it raises about belonging and lasting family relationships reach much further. The day is best understood through children’s needs and experiences, rather than simply as a celebration of adults becoming parents.

The national coalition dates the initiative to 2000. Its founding partners included the Dave Thomas Foundation for Adoption, the Congressional Coalition on Adoption Institute, the Alliance for Children’s Rights and Children’s Action Network. The annual pattern is on or around the Saturday before Thanksgiving, which brings it to the twenty-first this year. Individual communities sometimes choose nearby dates. That flexibility is important: the national day provides a shared focus, while the practical business of courts, local organisations and participating families determines the programme in each place.

One confirmed example for today is Los Angeles. The organisers announce that the Superior Court, county child-welfare department, legal organisations and volunteer attorneys will come together at the Edmund D. Edelman Children’s Courthouse. Their programme anticipates finalising 101 children’s adoptions by 71 families through private hearings. The listing also describes refreshments, books, toys and other activities for children. These are announced arrangements, rather than a report that every hearing has already concluded. The private nature of the event deserves respect even when the day has a public message.

The legal hearing and the surrounding celebration do different jobs. One completes a formal step; the other gives families and children a moment to recognise it together. Neither can contain the whole history or future of a person’s life. A careful account should leave room for an adopted person’s relationships, questions and feelings, including those that may be complicated. A public occasion can recognise a significant change without asking anyone to feel only happiness or to set aside connections that remain important to them.

Britain has its own adoption calendar. Adoption England places National Adoption Week 2026 on 19–25 October, so it should not be confused with today’s US day. Its programme emphasised the voices of people with lived experience, the needs of children and outcomes for adopted people, adoptive parents and birth families. That is useful context for a British reader: learning from an American occasion need not mean importing its date or treating different systems as interchangeable. The UK discussion also makes clear that adoption concerns more than one group of people.

Around the United States, the coalition’s event listings show how a national campaign becomes local, with programmes in different cities and courts. Readers elsewhere can mark the day by listening to adopted people’s accounts or learning about the work of organisations in their own country. Those are suggestions for attention, not advice about an individual adoption. The strongest idea in the day is the importance of lasting care and belonging for children. Keeping their voices and needs central makes the anniversary more thoughtful than a collection of cheerful courtroom photographs, and gives the public celebration a purpose beyond the day itself.
