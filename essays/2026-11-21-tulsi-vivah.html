Major occasion · 21 November 2026

Tulsi Vivah

Tulsi Vivah is a wedding celebration with a living plant at its heart. Tulsi, the sacred basil revered in Hindu worship, is honoured as a goddess and ceremonially married to Vishnu, often represented by a Shaligram stone. The occasion belongs to the bright half of the lunar month of Kartika, near the observance of Vishnu’s awakening. It carries the warmth of a wedding into devotional life: a relationship expressed through care, offerings and the gathering of a household or temple community.

For London, Drik Panchang places Tulasi Vivah on Saturday 21 November 2026, the day of Shukla Dwadashi. Its calculation puts the lunar day’s beginning at 1.01am and its end at 11.26pm, both London times, which are GMT on this date. MyPanchang independently lists Tulsi Vivah and Tulsi Kalyanam for London today. These details establish why it belongs in this run’s almanac. They are a calendar reference, rather than a universal instruction about when every family must perform its ceremony.

The festival is closely connected with Devutthana or Prabodhini Ekadashi, already covered in yesterday’s almanac. The two deserve separate entries because the awakening observance and the ceremonial marriage have distinct meanings, even when a community celebrates them together. Traditions can begin Tulsi weddings at Ekadashi and continue them during the following days. Location, the lunar day and inherited custom all matter. A fixed Gregorian date copied from an earlier year would lose the very calendar relationship that gives this festival its place.

An example from the living tradition comes from Sandipani’s ShriHari Mandir in Porbandar. Its own description of an earlier Tulsi wedding explains the close of Chaturmas, the devotional period understood as Vishnu’s four months of rest, and the resumption of auspicious ceremonies. The temple describes the marriage of Bhagavan Shaligram and Devi Tulsi in terms of her devotion and surrender. This is religious language describing the community’s faith. It should not be confused with a claim that the divine wedding is a datable event in secular history.

Present-day celebrations also show why one should leave room for variation. Reporting from Varanasi published this morning describes Tulsi marriages performed with Friday’s awakening observance, including worship at the Badrinarayan temple within the Kashi Vishwanath complex. The account mentions decoration, offerings, recitation and the distribution of prasad. Those ceremonies were held yesterday, while London’s Dwadashi calendar names today. Both facts can stand together: a broad religious festival is lived through particular local calendars, temples and families, rather than one globally uniform timetable.

For Hindu households and communities in Britain, the appropriate guide is their own temple or family tradition. For other readers, a good way into the day is to notice the scale of its central image. A plant that needs daily attention becomes the focus of an elaborate act of honour. The festival joins the domestic and the divine, giving ordinary tending a ceremonial expression. One need not invent a single founding year or a single mandatory ritual to appreciate its history as a continuing devotional practice. Today’s London date is definite; the ways believers give it meaning are more various.
