Minor occasion · 22 November 2026

Blackbeard’s final battle (1718)

On 22 November 1718, the pirate known as Blackbeard was killed in a battle near Ocracoke Island, in what is now North Carolina. Today is the conventional 308th anniversary of that encounter. It is a historical anniversary rather than a public observance celebrating piracy. The name invites stories of treasure and theatrical menace, but the surviving history leads into a harder world of merchant shipping, imperial rivalry, slavery and violence.

There is a calendar detail worth keeping in view. The familiar date belongs to the calendar then used in Britain and its American colonies, before their change to the Gregorian calendar in 1752. Histories generally retain 22 November for the battle rather than move its modern anniversary by converting the date. This entry follows that conventional dating, also used by the US National Park Service. Historical anniversaries do not always work like modern appointments entered into one worldwide calendar.

Blackbeard is associated with the names Edward Teach and Edward Thatch. The National Park Service’s fuller account cautions that his precise birthplace and birth date are uncertain. It also treats his possible earlier service as a privateer with appropriate qualification. A privateer operated under state authority to attack enemy shipping; a pirate acted outside that permission. The distinction matters in a period when wars and their aftermath could change a sailor’s legal position as well as his prospects of employment.

His best-known ship, Queen Anne’s Revenge, had previously been the French slave ship La Concorde. North Carolina’s cultural resources department documents that earlier history through records of voyages and contemporary reports. Renaming the vessel did not erase the suffering already connected with it. The ship ran aground and was abandoned near Beaufort in June 1718, several months before Blackbeard’s last battle. It was therefore not the vessel on which he met his death at Ocracoke.

By the autumn of 1718, Blackbeard’s position in North Carolina had become entangled with colonial politics and the terms of a royal pardon. Virginia’s governor Alexander Spotswood sent an expedition against him. Royal Navy lieutenant Robert Maynard approached Ocracoke with men aboard smaller vessels suited to the shallow waters. The fighting involved gunfire and a boarding action, and ended with Blackbeard’s death. The coastal geography was part of the encounter, not just scenery behind it.

Today, the history is interpreted through the National Park Service and North Carolina’s archaeology and heritage work. Their accounts offer a more useful starting point than assuming that every familiar pirate image can be traced to Blackbeard himself. The earlier life of Queen Anne’s Revenge, in particular, puts objects and records beside the legend. In Britain there is no general commemoration of the death; the connection lies in Royal Navy and colonial history, and in the stories later told about both.

A good way to approach the anniversary is to ask what a source can actually support. An uncertain childhood should remain uncertain. A violent encounter need not be embroidered with invented last words. A recovered ship can tell a story wider than its most famous captain. Blackbeard’s reputation has travelled much further than the short span of his documented pirate career. Today’s date offers a chance to bring that reputation back into contact with the people, places and evidence from which the history has to be made.
