Minor occasion · 22 November 2026

Ravi Pradosh Vrat (Pradosham)

For devotees of Shiva, Pradosh Vrat, also called Pradosham, is a recurring opportunity for prayer near the close of the day. Indian calendars place this month’s bright-fortnight observance on Sunday 22 November 2026. A Sunday Pradosh is commonly called Ravi Pradosh. It is a smaller recurring religious observance rather than a once-a-year national festival, and it brings a different rhythm to a calendar otherwise filled with fixed November anniversaries.

The date is governed by the lunar calendar. Pradosham is associated with Trayodashi, the thirteenth lunar day of a fortnight, and particularly with its overlap with the evening period around sunset. There are observances in both the waxing and waning halves of the lunar month. A lunar day is not identical to a civil day beginning at midnight, which is why a date cannot safely be worked out by counting forward from a new moon on a wall calendar.

The 2026 annual list from Drik Panchang gives 22 November for New Delhi. Published Indian calendar material independently places a Pradosham on that date. The place attached to a calculation matters: sunset and lunar-day boundaries can produce differences between locations. This entry therefore identifies the Indian dating rather than declaring one universal timetable. Readers elsewhere should follow the calendar and practice of their own temple or tradition.

There is also a confirmed British connection for today. London Sri Mahalakshmi Temple’s published programme lists Sukla Pradosham on 22 November 2026 at 5.30pm UK time. That is a specific local service time, not a time that should be applied to every temple. It shows how a lunar observance can have a clear place in the practical life of a London congregation, with its own arrangements for communal worship.

Abhishekam is ritual bathing of the sacred image. The Hindu Temple Society of New Mexico describes its own Pradosham worship as including bathing of the Shiva lingam, sacred chanting, and offerings such as bilva leaves, flowers and fruit. The New York Ganesh Temple also maintains an explanation of the observance. These temple accounts are useful because they describe a living practice from within worshipping communities, rather than reducing it to a list of supposed benefits detached from their religious setting.

Some devotees keep a vrat, a religious observance that can include fasting, while others take part through worship according to their family or temple custom. There is no need to prescribe one uniform household rule in an almanac. Nor should devotional beliefs about blessings be presented as guaranteed physical outcomes. The meaningful historical continuity lies in the recurring dedication of time to Shiva, expressed through ritual, prayer and the shared life of a congregation.

For an interested reader, the most helpful way to understand Pradosham may be to notice its scale. It is not dependent on a large public spectacle. Twice in a lunar month, an evening can become a point of return, drawing attention away from the unfinished business of the day and towards worship. Today’s London listing makes that rhythm visible close to home, while calendars across regions remind us that a religious date is often the meeting of astronomy, place and inherited practice.
