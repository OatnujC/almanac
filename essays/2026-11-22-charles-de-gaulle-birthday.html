Minor occasion · 22 November 2026

Charles de Gaulle born (1890)

Charles de Gaulle was born in Lille on 22 November 1890. Today is the 136th anniversary of that birth, an opportunity to begin his story with a family house and a civil record rather than a uniform or a presidential portrait. It is a historical birthday anniversary, distinct from the anniversary of his death earlier in November and from the national commemorations associated with the Second World War.

His birthplace was his maternal grandparents’ home at 9 rue Princesse. The Fondation Charles de Gaulle identifies it as a house that had also contained a tulle-making business. That detail gives the beginning of the story a useful domestic scale. Before de Gaulle became a figure associated with armies, radio appeals and constitutions, he belonged to an extended family with a particular address, a household and a place in the working life of Lille.

The Nord departmental archives provide another close view through the registration of his birth. On 23 November, his father Henri went to Lille’s town hall with witnesses to declare the birth of Charles, André, Joseph, Marie on the previous day. The difference between the day of birth and the day of registration is small but important. It is exactly the sort of distinction that helps an anniversary rest on evidence rather than on a date copied without context.

The Élysée’s biographical timeline follows his military education and service, including his capture during the First World War and the development of his thinking about military organisation between the wars. In 1940, after France’s defeat, he went to London and called for the struggle to continue. His BBC appeal of 18 June became foundational to Free France. The Élysée notes a useful point about recordings: the famous 18 June appeal was not recorded, whereas the later appeal of 22 June was.

That London connection gives his life a particular resonance for British readers. The British capital became a base from which he sought to sustain a French voice in the war. Later, his role in the Fifth Republic and his presidency made him central to France’s post-war political story. The birthday can introduce those later chapters, but it does not require retelling every episode. Its distinctive focus is the distance between a child’s beginnings and the public life that followed.

In France, the birthplace is preserved as a museum. The Nord department describes a restoration designed to return the house to the appearance de Gaulle would have known, including domestic rooms and his cradle. Such a museum offers a different sort of historical encounter from a battlefield memorial. Visitors look at spaces associated with childhood and family habits, asking how an individual was formed before later events made him famous. This entry does not assume a special anniversary event or opening arrangement today.

There is no UK-wide observance of de Gaulle’s birthday. A reader in Britain or elsewhere can mark it through the surviving records and museum accounts, taking the opportunity to distinguish documented childhood from hindsight about destiny. Nothing in a birth certificate predicts a public career. That is part of its value. On a date crowded with large events, it reminds us that even the most imposing historical figure began as someone whose arrival had to be reported at the local town hall.
