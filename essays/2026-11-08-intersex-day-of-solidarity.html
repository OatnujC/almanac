Intersex Day of Solidarity — remembering a life, standing with others

Intersex Day of Solidarity, also called Intersex Day of Remembrance, is observed each year on 8 November. It marks the birthday of Herculine Barbin, a nineteenth-century French intersex person whose memoir is one of the earliest first-person accounts of intersex life. The day is a chance to remember one life that was long talked about more than listened to, and to stand with intersex people today, whose rights to bodily autonomy and equal treatment are still contested in many places.

What intersex means

Intersex is an umbrella term for people born with variations in sex characteristics, such as chromosomes, gonads, hormones or genitals, that do not fit typical definitions of male or female bodies. There are many different variations. Some are apparent at birth, some appear at puberty, and some are discovered only later in life, or never. Estimates of how common intersex traits are vary widely, from well under 1 per cent to about 1.7 per cent of births, depending on which conditions are counted. Being intersex is about bodies; intersex people have the same range of gender identities and sexual orientations as anyone else.

Why 8 November: Herculine Barbin

Herculine Adélaïde Barbin was born on 8 November 1838 in Saint-Jean-d’Angély in western France. She was assigned female at birth, named Alexina by her family, and despite poverty won a charity place at a convent school run by Ursuline nuns. She trained as a teacher and in 1857 began working at a girls’ school, where she fell in love with another teacher, Sara. Barbin’s health was poor, and she suffered severe pain. After she confided in the Bishop of La Rochelle, a doctor examined her in 1860 and concluded that, in the medical language of the time, she was a “male pseudohermaphrodite”.

A court then ruled that Barbin was legally male. She had to leave her job and her lover, change her name to Abel Barbin and move to Paris, where she lived in poverty. There she wrote her memoirs, using female pronouns for her life before the court’s decision and male pronouns afterwards, and describing herself as punished and “disinherited”. In February 1868 she was found dead in her room, having taken her own life; the memoirs lay beside her bed.

A doctor passed the manuscript to the forensic physician Auguste Ambroise Tardieu, who published extracts in 1872 in a medical book on mistaken sex. A century later, the philosopher Michel Foucault came across the memoir while researching in French archives and republished it, together with medical reports, legal documents, newspaper articles and a short story based on her life, as Herculine Barbin: Being the Recently Discovered Memoirs of a Nineteenth-century French Hermaphrodite. The book made her famous among scholars of gender. Some intersex writers have pointed out that, even then, others often interpreted her life for their own arguments; her own words describe herself as female, though an exceptional one. Remembering her on her birthday is in part a way of putting her own voice back at the centre.

How the day began

The observance appears to have first been held on 8 November 2005, as Intersex Solidarity Day. Joëlle-Circé Laramée, then Canadian spokeswoman for Organisation Intersex International, invited anyone concerned with intersex human rights to commemorate the date, whether by marking Barbin’s life or by discussing the harm done by non-consensual medical treatment and by rigid ideas about sex and gender. It has been marked mostly in Europe. In 2012 the New South Wales Parliament in Australia formally acknowledged the day, and in 2016 OII Europe chose it to launch a visibility website with information in 23 European languages.

It is distinct from Intersex Awareness Day on 26 October, which remembers the first public demonstration by intersex people in North America, outside a conference of the American Academy of Pediatrics in Boston on 26 October 1996. Some countries, including Australia and South Africa, treat the two dates and the days between as “14 days of intersex”: one day for raising public understanding, the other for remembrance and solidarity.

What the day is about now

A central concern of intersex campaigners is medical intervention on infants and young children: surgery or hormone treatment to make a child’s body look more typically male or female, carried out before the child is old enough to consent and often when there is no medical need. Many intersex adults describe lasting physical and psychological harm. United Nations human rights bodies, the World Health Organization and the Council of Europe have criticised such non-consensual “normalising” interventions. In April 2015 Malta became the first country to ban them, and in 2024 the UN Human Rights Council adopted its first resolution on the rights of intersex people.

Other issues include discrimination, legal recognition, access to one’s own medical records, and the stigma that still surrounds bodies that do not fit neat categories. On 8 November, intersex organisations and allies hold talks, publish stories and share information, and many simply take a moment to remember Herculine Barbin, who wrote her own story when almost no one was willing to hear it.
