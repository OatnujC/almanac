Major occasion · 21 November 2026

Venice’s Feast of the Madonna della Salute

On 21 November, Venice turns towards the Basilica of Santa Maria della Salute. The Feast of the Madonna della Salute joins a pilgrimage, candles, prayer and the livelier pleasures of a local festival. “Salute” means health, and the occasion remembers the city’s appeal to the Virgin Mary during the plague of 1630–31. It remains a living Venetian observance, rather than simply a picturesque anniversary. People cross the water towards a church whose very existence records a promise made in a time of fear.

The State Archives of Venice traces the public vow to the plague year of 1630. The city promised a church in honour of Mary and an enduring act of gratitude. The basilica’s own history records the laying of the first stone on 1 April 1631 and its consecration in 1687. Baldassare Longhena’s design gave Venice one of its most recognisable buildings. Its beauty has a specific purpose behind it: the architecture was intended to carry a city’s prayer and thanksgiving into the lives of later generations.

The annual celebration falls on 21 November, also the Christian feast of Mary’s Presentation. It is not necessary to pretend that every stage of the plague or the church’s construction happened on that one day. The date brings together the religious calendar and a civic tradition of pilgrimage. The diocesan liturgical text preserves the link between the Senate’s vow, the building and the people returning to ask Mary’s protection. That continuity is central to the feast, even for a visitor encountering it for the first time.

The temporary crossing is its most distinctive practical feature. A votive bridge connects the Santa Maria del Giglio side of the Grand Canal with the basilica, making the pilgrimage itself part of the city’s changing landscape. Venice’s council confirms that the 2026 bridge opened on Thursday 19 November and is due to remain open until Sunday 22 November. Today, Saturday the twenty-first, is the heart of the celebration. The council’s current announcement, rather than a recycled visitor timetable, is the appropriate guide to this year’s arrangements.

For 2026, the council announces that the basilica is open from 4.40am to 9.20pm UK time today. The solemn Mass is scheduled for 9am UK time, with other services through the day. Those are converted from Venice’s local hours, one hour ahead of Britain in November. The city also describes the custom of buying a candle, the presence of stalls and the traditional dish castradina. Religious devotion and convivial city life sit alongside each other; reducing the feast to either alone would miss something of its character.

This is a local Venetian festival, not a UK public observance. From Britain, a reader can mark it by exploring the basilica’s history, following the announced broadcast or learning about the public-health records preserved in the city’s archives. Such attention can reveal more than a photograph of a famous dome. It shows how a community gives form to memory: through a building that lasts, a bridge rebuilt for the occasion and a journey people choose to repeat. Nearly four centuries after the plague, those acts still connect today’s city with the promise that helped shape its waterfront.
