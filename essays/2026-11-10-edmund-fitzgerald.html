Minor occasion · Tuesday 10 November 2026

Wreck of the Edmund Fitzgerald

Fifty-one years ago tonight, on 10 November 1975, the American freighter SS Edmund Fitzgerald sank in a ferocious storm on Lake Superior, the largest of North America's Great Lakes. All 29 men aboard were lost, and no bodies were ever recovered. It remains the most famous shipwreck in Great Lakes history, partly because of a haunting song, and each year on this date it is remembered with the ringing of bells and the lighting of a lighthouse.

The Queen of the Lakes

The Edmund Fitzgerald was launched on 7 June 1958, and at 729 feet long she was then the largest ship on the Great Lakes, earning the title "Queen of the Lakes". She was built for an insurance company in Milwaukee and named after its chairman. For seventeen years she carried taconite, pellets of iron ore, from the mines near Duluth in Minnesota to steelworks in Detroit, Toledo and other ports, setting seasonal haulage records six times. Boat watchers loved her, not least because one of her captains liked to play music over the ship's loudspeakers as she passed through the rivers and locks.

The last voyage

On the afternoon of 9 November 1975 she left Superior, Wisconsin, fully loaded with taconite and bound for a steel mill near Detroit, under Captain Ernest McSorley. The next day she ran into a severe late-autumn storm, a classic "gale of November", with winds approaching hurricane force and waves reported as high as 35 feet. Late in the afternoon the Fitzgerald reported that she was in difficulty, with damage and a list. Another freighter, the Arthur M. Anderson, was following her and stayed in radio contact.

At about 7:10 pm, Captain McSorley told the Anderson, "We are holding our own." Shortly afterwards, the Fitzgerald vanished from the radar screen. She sank suddenly in Canadian waters about 530 feet deep, around 17 miles from the shelter of Whitefish Bay, near the twin towns of Sault Ste. Marie, a distance she could have covered in just over an hour. There was no distress call. A US Navy aircraft located the wreck a few days later, broken in two on the lake bed.

Nobody knows for certain why she sank. Investigators and later expeditions have suggested that she may have taken on water through damaged hatches, suffered structural failure, been damaged on a shoal, or been overwhelmed by giant waves, or some combination of these.

The song

The sinking might have remained a regional tragedy but for the Canadian singer-songwriter Gordon Lightfoot. After reading an article about the wreck in Newsweek, he wrote "The Wreck of the Edmund Fitzgerald", released in 1976. Its slow, rolling melody and storytelling lyrics, beginning "The legend lives on from the Chippewa on down", made it a hit, reaching number one in Canada and number two in the United States. Lightfoot considered it his finest work. In later years he changed one line in live performances to reflect newer findings about how the ship went down.

Changes on the lakes

The loss led to real improvements in Great Lakes safety, including mandatory survival suits, depth finders and positioning systems on ships, greater freeboard (the height of the deck above the water) and more frequent inspections.

How it is remembered

On 10 November each year, memorial services around the Great Lakes ring a bell for the lost crew. The Great Lakes Maritime Academy now rings its bell 29 times for the men lost, once for all lives lost at sea, and once for Gordon Lightfoot, who died in 2023. In 1995 the ship's bell was recovered from the wreck and is displayed at the Great Lakes Shipwreck Museum at Whitefish Point in Michigan, where an annual ceremony is held. And at Split Rock Lighthouse on the Minnesota shore of Lake Superior, the beacon is lit on the evening of 10 November in memory of the Fitzgerald and her crew. It is a small, quiet anniversary, but for people around the lakes it is still very much alive.
