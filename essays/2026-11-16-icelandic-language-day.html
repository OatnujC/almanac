Minor occasion · 16 November 2026

Icelandic Language Day

Every 16 November Iceland throws a small party for its own language. Dagur íslenskrar tungu, the Day of the Icelandic Tongue, is one of the country's official flag days, and it falls on the birthday of the poet Jónas Hallgrímsson, born on this day in 1807 and still one of the nation's best-loved writers.

How the day began

In the autumn of 1995, the then Minister of Education, Science and Culture, Björn Bjarnason, proposed that one day a year be set aside to celebrate Icelandic, and the government agreed that it should be 16 November, Jónas's birthday. The first Icelandic Language Day was held in 1996. Its purpose, in the government's words, is to remind people of the importance of the Icelandic language and to rejoice in its history, present and future. Responsibility for the day has rested with the Ministry of Culture and Business Affairs since 2021.

The central event is the presentation of the Jónas Hallgrímsson Award, given each year to a person who has made an outstanding contribution to the Icelandic language, along with special recognitions for work on its behalf. Schools, libraries and cultural institutions hold readings, competitions and events, and the celebration now spreads across much of the week. Icelandic communities abroad mark it too; Iceland's government has described an Icelandic Language Day celebration at the Scandinavian Cultural Centre in Winnipeg, home to one of the largest communities of Icelandic descent in the world.

Why Icelanders treasure their language

Icelandic is spoken by only a few hundred thousand people, yet it is famous among linguists for how little its written form has changed over the centuries. Modern Icelanders can read the medieval sagas, written down around 800 years ago, with relatively little difficulty. That continuity is a deep source of national pride and was central to Iceland's 19th-century independence movement, which looked back to the saga age as proof of a distinct Icelandic nation.

Icelanders also have a long tradition of creating new words from native roots rather than borrowing foreign ones. Where English borrows a Greek or Latin word, Icelandic often builds its own. That habit makes the language feel alive and inventive, and it is a big part of what the day celebrates. Today the worry is less about Danish influence, as it was in Jónas's day, than about English, which reaches Icelandic children through phones, games and streaming, so the day has also become a moment to talk about keeping Icelandic strong in a digital world.

Who was Jónas Hallgrímsson?

Jónas Hallgrímsson was born at Hraun in Öxnadalur, in the north of Iceland. His father, a curate, drowned when Jónas was a boy, and he was sent to live with an aunt. He won a place at the school at Bessastaðir, worked as a clerk in Reykjavík, and in 1832 sailed to Copenhagen to study at the university. He began in law but switched to literature and natural sciences, and spent years on research expeditions around Iceland, keeping diaries and reports on its geology and nature.

In 1835 he and three fellow Icelandic students in Copenhagen founded Fjölnir, a patriotic journal that promoted Icelandic culture and the cause of independence. Many of his poems first appeared in its pages. He is regarded as one of the founding figures of Icelandic Romanticism, and his poems about the landscape and its people are among the best-known in the language. He also introduced foreign metres, such as the pentameter, to Icelandic poetry. One of his most famous poems, Ísland, laments that the old parliament at Þingvellir no longer meets and that the forefathers' fame lies forgotten.

Jónas was also a prolific word-maker. Translating foreign scientific works into Icelandic, he coined many words that now sound as if they had always existed. The government's history of the day quotes the scholar Guðrún Kvaran on how his new words seem familiar and part of the basic vocabulary. A favourite example is reikistjarna, planet, built from the verb að reika, to wander, and stjarna, star: a wandering star, which is exactly what the ancient Greek word planet meant too.

A strange postscript

Jónas died young. In May 1845 he slipped on the stairs to his room in Copenhagen, broke his leg and died of blood poisoning a few days later, aged 37. He was buried in Copenhagen, and there his story might have ended, but in 1946 his remains were dug up and brought home in an episode Icelanders call beinamálið, the case of the bones. The campaigner behind it, Sigurjón Pétursson, wanted to bury the poet in Öxnadalur, but the government insisted he belonged at the national burial ground at Þingvellir. After a week in which the coffin stood in a church in the north, it was driven back south and buried at Þingvellir on 16 November, his birthday. The affair was satirised by Halldór Laxness in his 1948 novel The Atom Station and by Milan Kundera in Ignorance.

So today, as Icelanders hand out awards, read poems aloud and perhaps invent a new word or two, they are honouring both a language and the man who did so much to keep it fresh.
