Minor occasion · Sunday 8 November 2026

Synaxis of the Archangel Michael — a gathering of the heavenly host

On 8 November many Orthodox churches keep the Synaxis of the Archangel Michael and the Other Bodiless Powers of Heaven: a feast honouring Michael, Gabriel, Raphael and the whole company of angels. “Synaxis” is a Greek word for a gathering or assembly. The day is not only Michael’s, though he is named as chief commander of the heavenly hosts; it is a single celebration of the angelic world as the Church understands it.

Two dates, one feast

The civil date depends on which calendar a church follows. Churches that use the revised Julian or “new” calendar, including the Orthodox Church in America, the Church of Greece and the Ecumenical Patriarchate, keep the synaxis on 8 November, which in 2026 was a Sunday. Churches that still follow the older Julian calendar, including the Russian, Serbian and Georgian Orthodox Churches and Mount Athos, also keep it on “8 November”, but by their reckoning that day falls thirteen days later on the everyday calendar: on 21 November 2026. So the same feast is celebrated twice in a typical November, by different communities.

Why 8 November

The Orthodox Church in America’s account explains the date symbolically. In ancient times the year was often counted from March, which made November the ninth month, and Christian tradition counted nine ranks of angels. The eighth day was chosen because the Church Fathers called the Last Judgement the “Eighth Day”: after the seven days of this age, the Son of Man will come in glory, “and all the holy angels with him”, as Matthew’s Gospel puts it.

Orthodox tradition connects the feast with the Council of Laodicea, a regional church council in Asia Minor. Its 35th canon condemned the worship of angels as gods or rulers of the world, while affirming their proper honour; the concern was that some Christians in the region had drifted into a cult of angels. The tradition places the council early in the fourth century, a few years before the First Ecumenical Council at Nicaea in 325, while many modern historians date it to around 363 or 364. Either way, the feast reflects an early effort to give angels a proper place in Christian worship: honoured as servants and messengers of God, never worshipped as divine.

Nine ranks of angels

The idea of nine ranks of angels was set out in a famous work called The Celestial Hierarchy, written around the year 500 under the name of Dionysius the Areopagite. It arranges the angels in three groups of three. The highest are the Seraphim, Cherubim and Thrones; the middle, the Dominions, Powers and Authorities; and the lowest, the Principalities, Archangels and Angels, the last being closest to human beings. All of them are called “angels” in a general sense, from the Greek word for messenger.

Michael and the other archangels

Michael’s name in Hebrew means “Who is like God?”, a question that is also a challenge. In the Book of Daniel he is a great prince who protects God’s people, and in the Book of Revelation he leads the angels in a war in heaven and casts down the dragon. In Orthodox icons he is usually shown in armour with a spear or a fiery sword, trampling the devil. He is honoured as a protector and a defender against evil, and many churches, monasteries and cities are dedicated to him; in Moscow, the Cathedral of the Archangel in the Kremlin is the burial place of many of Russia’s grand princes and tsars.

The feast also remembers Gabriel, “the strength of God”, who announced the birth of Christ to Mary; Raphael, “the healing of God”, who in the Book of Tobit guides the young Tobias and cures his father’s blindness; and, in Orthodox tradition, further archangels named as Uriel, Selaphiel, Jehudiel, Barachiel and Jeremiel. Each has a traditional appearance in icons: Raphael holding a vessel of medicine, Uriel a sword and a flame, Selaphiel with hands folded in prayer.

How it is kept

In Orthodox churches the feast is celebrated with vespers on the eve and the Divine Liturgy on the day, with special hymns to the archangels. People named Michael, Gabriel, Raphael and their equivalents, Mikhail, Mihai, Michalis, Gavriil, celebrate their name day, which in much of Eastern Europe and Greece matters at least as much as a birthday. In Serbia the feast of Archangel Michael, known as Aranđelovdan, is one of the most common family patron-saint days, or slava, so many households mark it with a special loaf, candle and family meal.

Michaelmas: the Western cousin

Western churches honour Michael on a different date. In the Catholic and Anglican calendars, 29 September is the feast of St Michael and All Angels, known in England as Michaelmas. It gave its name to one of the traditional English quarter days, when rents fell due, and to the autumn term at the universities of Oxford and Cambridge and in the law courts. Michaelmas goose was a traditional dish, and there is an old saying that the blackberries should not be picked after Michaelmas, because the devil, cast out of heaven, spoils them. The Orthodox November feast and the Western September one share a subject but have separate histories.

Whatever the date, the feast invites a particular kind of attention: to the idea, ancient in Judaism, Christianity and Islam alike, that the visible world is not the whole of creation, and that the unseen is busy with care and protection.
