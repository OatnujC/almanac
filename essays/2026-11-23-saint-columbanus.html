Minor occasion · 23 November 2026

St Columbanus’s Day

St Columbanus, also called Columban, is remembered on 23 November. His life began in Ireland and carried him across much of western Europe before his death at Bobbio in Italy in 615. Benedict XVI links his death to 23 November, though some older accounts give 21 November; the Roman feast is securely kept on the 23rd. The Roman calendar keeps his memory today, and the Irish bishops' national liturgical texts also place him on 23 November. This is a religious commemoration rather than a general public holiday, and its local prominence varies between churches and communities.

The broad outline of his life is a story of movement undertaken for religious reasons. Born in Leinster around 543, he studied and entered monastic life, eventually joining the community at Bangor under Comgall. Prayer, study and a demanding discipline shaped him there. The approximate birth date is worth retaining as approximate: the sources do not give us the kind of precise personal record available for a modern public figure. The Roman commemoration on 23 November is the secure calendar anchor here, even where historical accounts differ over the death date.

Around the age of fifty, Columbanus left Ireland with twelve companions. Benedict XVI describes this departure through the Irish idea of pilgrimage for Christ: leaving familiar surroundings as part of a religious vocation. It was not travel for novelty or a tour with a planned return. Such a journey carried the uncertainty of finding new patrons, negotiating unfamiliar political circumstances and establishing a durable community. Seen that way, the monastery was something built through relationships as well as through stone and timber.

His continental career eventually brought him across the Alps to northern Italy. At Bobbio, land provided by the Lombard king in 612 or 613 enabled the foundation of a new monastery. He died there only a few years later. The Vatican account describes Bobbio becoming a major centre of culture. A life devoted to prayer could therefore have consequences well beyond one person's spiritual discipline: monasteries became places where learning and writing acquired an institutional home and could be passed on.

Columbanus was also a writer who addressed rulers and church leaders. His surviving reputation includes strong demands for conversion and moral seriousness. It would be too easy to turn him into a modern symbol of effortless international harmony. His world contained disputes, divided churches and political conflict, and he was not a person inclined to avoid difficult arguments. The European reach of his life is meaningful precisely because it involved work across those divisions rather than an absence of them.

The feast continues beyond the places he knew. The Missionary Society of St Columban takes him as its patron; a published feast-day greeting from its Oceania region addresses priests, lay missionaries, co-workers and companions in several countries. The Irish bishops' proper prayers describe missionary zeal joined to monastic life. Those are different expressions of the same remembered example: contemplation and outward activity belong together. The modern missionary network is part of his legacy, though its organisation and circumstances are very different from a seventh-century monastery.

For UK readers, Bangor in present-day Northern Ireland is a particularly close point of connection. A way to mark the date is to learn about that monastic setting or follow the route from Ireland towards Bobbio on a map. Believers may keep the feast through prayer; others can appreciate the history of learning, travel and community-building. No particular local 2026 event is assumed here. The day offers a quieter invitation: think about what people carry when they leave home, and how something rooted in one place can take a new form far away.
