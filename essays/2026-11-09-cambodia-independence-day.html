Independence Day (Cambodia)

Every year on 9 November, Cambodia celebrates Independence Day, the anniversary of the day in 1953 when King Norodom Sihanouk declared the country’s full independence from France. It is a public holiday, and the centre of the national celebration is the Independence Monument in Phnom Penh, a tall, lotus-shaped tower that has become one of the city’s best-known landmarks. In 2026 the 73rd anniversary falls on a Monday.

Why 9 November

Independence Day is a fixed civic anniversary, so it does not move like Cambodia’s lunar festivals. It commemorates a declaration rather than a battle. By the end of October 1953, France had already agreed to hand over control of Cambodia’s courts, police and, finally, its defence. Sihanouk then travelled to Phnom Penh and declared independence on 9 November 1953. The Geneva Conference of 1954, which dealt with the end of the wider war in Indochina, confirmed Cambodia’s independence internationally.

Ninety years of French rule

In the mid-nineteenth century Cambodia was a weakened kingdom squeezed between two powerful neighbours, Siam (now Thailand) to the west and Vietnam to the east. Siam had taken its western provinces, including the region around Angkor. When France established a colony in southern Vietnam in 1862, King Norodom, Sihanouk’s great-grandfather, looked to France as a counterweight, and on 11 August 1863 he signed a treaty placing Cambodia under French protection.

The monarchy remained, but real power passed steadily to a French Resident-General in Phnom Penh. In 1887 Cambodia became part of French Indochina, alongside the territories that are now Vietnam and Laos. Over the decades France built roads, a railway and plantations, but Cambodians paid heavy taxes and had little say in how they were governed. There were rebellions in the 1880s and protests over taxes in 1916. Senior posts were filled mostly by French officials, with Vietnamese clerks below them; Cambodians themselves were largely shut out.

A young king and a crusade

When King Monivong died in 1941, the French chose his 18-year-old grandson, Norodom Sihanouk, as king, assuming he would be easy to manage. The Second World War intervened. Japan occupied French Indochina, and in March 1945 it removed the French administration and pressed Sihanouk to declare independence. The French returned after Japan’s surrender, and in 1946 Cambodia became an autonomous state within the French Union. In 1949 a new treaty called it “independent” within that union, but in practice France kept control of most important matters.

Sihanouk decided to push harder. In 1953 he went to France and wrote to President Vincent Auriol asking for full independence; the request was turned down. He then took his case to Canada and the United States, giving interviews in which he argued that only real independence would keep Cambodians from turning to the communist-led independence movement fighting in neighbouring Vietnam. He returned home in June 1953, held rallies and raised a volunteer militia that attracted about 130,000 recruits. The campaign became known as his Royal Crusade for Independence. France, already fighting a costly war in Vietnam, gave way step by step over the summer and autumn. Because of this, Cambodians widely honour Sihanouk as the “father of independence”.

A complicated legacy

Sihanouk dominated Cambodian politics for decades. He abdicated in 1955 to enter politics directly, led the country through the 1960s, and was overthrown in a coup in 1970. He then allied himself with the Khmer Rouge, whose rule from 1975 to 1979 led to the deaths of a very large share of the population, a decision that badly damaged his reputation. He became king again in 1993 and abdicated in 2004 in favour of his son, Norodom Sihamoni, who is king today. Sihanouk died in 2012. Historians describe a mixed record, but his role in winning independence is not seriously disputed, and that is the part of his life Independence Day celebrates.

The Independence Monument

The Independence Monument stands on a roundabout where Norodom and Sihanouk Boulevards meet in central Phnom Penh. It was built in 1958 and designed by the Cambodian architect Vann Molyvann, a leading figure in the modernist style known as New Khmer Architecture. About 37 metres tall, it takes the form of a lotus-shaped stupa, borrowing from the temples of the Angkor era such as Banteay Srei. Behind it stands a memorial to Sihanouk, built after his death.

How it is celebrated

The main ceremony takes place at the monument in the morning. The King, as commander-in-chief of the armed forces, presides over the raising of the national flag and lights a ceremonial flame inside the monument, with senior officials and representatives of state bodies in attendance. Roads around the monument are closed, and the ceremony is broadcast on national television and radio. Government buildings are decorated with flags, slogans and lights, and in the evening there are often fireworks over the river in front of the Royal Palace, where the Tonlé Sap meets the Mekong. Smaller celebrations take place in provincial towns.

For many families it is simply a day off, a chance to visit the riverside, picnic and enjoy the decorations. In Cambodian communities abroad, including in Britain, the date is a moment of national pride, often marked by gatherings at temples and community centres.

A note on November

Independence Day sits in a busy season. The Water Festival, Bon Om Touk, with its famous boat races on the river in Phnom Penh, is also usually held in late October or November, but it follows the lunar calendar and so moves from year to year. The two are easy to confuse: Independence Day always falls on 9 November, while the Water Festival follows the moon.
