Major occasion · 19 November 2026

International Men’s Day

International Men’s Day, held every year on 19 November, is a day for thinking about men and boys: celebrating the good they do, and paying honest attention to the problems that hit them hardest, from poor health and suicide to homelessness, violence and abuse. It is not an official United Nations day, as International Women’s Day is, but it has grown from a small Caribbean initiative into an occasion marked in dozens of countries, and in Britain it now draws hundreds of organisations each year.

Why 19 November

The modern day was founded in 1999 by Dr Jerome Teelucksingh, a history lecturer at the University of the West Indies in Trinidad and Tobago. He chose 19 November for two reasons. It was his father’s birthday, and it was the date in 1989 when Trinidad and Tobago’s men’s football team had united the country with its attempt to qualify for the World Cup. Teelucksingh has always presented the day as more than a gendered celebration: a day when any issue affecting men and boys can be raised. In his words, its grassroots activists “are striving for gender equality and patiently attempt to remove the negative images and the stigma associated with men in our society.”

An idea older than the date

People had been asking for a men’s day for decades before that. In 1969 The New York Times reported that “many men have been agitating privately to make February 23 International Men’s Day, the equivalent of March 8, which is International Women’s Day.” In the Soviet Union, 23 February was Red Army and Navy Day, and in some places it was treated informally as a male counterpart to Women’s Day; several former Soviet countries, including Belarus, Ukraine, Moldova, Russia and Georgia, have since adopted 19 November instead.

The first organised attempt came in the early 1990s, when Professor Thomas Oaster, who directed the Missouri Center for Men’s Studies at the University of Missouri–Kansas City, invited groups in the United States, Australia and Malta to hold small events in February. The event was inaugurated on 7 February 1992. It went well for a couple of years, but a poorly attended attempt in 1995 led Oaster to drop it. Only Malta kept going, holding a February men’s day every year from 1994. Australians re-established the day on 19 November in 2003, and in 2009 Malta’s Association for Men’s Rights voted to move to 19 November too, so that the world would mark it together. The International Men’s Day organisation also credits Uma Challa, an Indian men’s advocate, with pioneering the day in India in 2007; it describes India’s celebration as the biggest of any country.

Six pillars

The day’s organisers set out its objectives as six “pillars”: to promote positive male role models, including ordinary working men living decent, honest lives, and not only film stars and sportsmen; to celebrate men’s contributions to society, community, family, marriage, child care and the environment; to focus on men’s health and wellbeing, social, emotional, physical and spiritual; to highlight discrimination against men in social services, attitudes and law; to improve gender relations and promote gender equality; and to create a safer world in which people can reach their full potential. Global coordinators have also suggested yearly themes, from peace in 2002 and men’s health in 2003 to “Stop Male Suicide” in 2016 and “Zero Male Suicide” in 2023.

The day in Britain

In the UK the day was first marked in 2008 by a student group at the University of Kent called the Bishopden Boys, who held a football tournament, comedy and live music on campus and raised money for Orchid, a charity for male cancers. Since 2010 there has been an organised national platform, now run by the charity the Association of Male Health and Wellbeing. The growth has been steady: more than 25 organisations held events in 2014, including two in the House of Commons; in 2015 the MP Philip Davies led a debate on men’s issues in Westminster on 19 November; by 2018 there were more than 200 events; and in 2022 an estimated 600 organisations took part. The UK organisers now say more than 800 organisations get involved each year, and the day has been endorsed by prime ministers including Theresa May.

For 2026, which falls on a Thursday, the UK theme is “Supporting Men and Boys”, built on three pillars that are used every year: making a positive difference to the wellbeing and lives of men and boys; raising awareness or funds for charities that support them; and promoting a positive conversation about men, manhood and masculinity. The organisers deliberately keep it broad, encouraging workplaces, schools, charities and community groups to adapt it. Their suggestions range from “It’s OK to talk” mental health messages and reminders to get checked by a GP, to events about male victims of domestic abuse, support for new dads, and simply reaching out to mates who are finding things tough. They also stress that the UK day takes a gender-inclusive approach, which means it wants issues affecting women and girls resolved too, and that it includes all men, including gay, bisexual and trans men.

How it is marked around the world

The forms the day takes are as varied as the countries that keep it. Organisers list seminars and conferences, classroom activities, men’s health events, Movember fundraisers, radio and television programmes, parliamentary speeches, church services, awards ceremonies, concerts and art displays. In Australia, the group Dads4Kids held celebrations at the New South Wales state parliament in 2008 and at the federal parliament in Canberra in 2013. In Ghana, the Ghana Fatherhood Initiative Foundation marked its first day in 2009 by donating books to schools in Accra and urging fathers to read to their children.

Whatever the setting, the underlying idea is the one Teelucksingh started with: a day to look at men and boys with fairness, to say thank you where it is due, and to take seriously the problems that too often go unspoken.
