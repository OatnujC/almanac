Minor occasion · 22 November 2026

Stir-up Sunday

Some calendar customs begin in a church and end up beside a mixing bowl. Stir-up Sunday is one of Britain’s most homely examples. On the Sunday before Advent, families traditionally prepare a Christmas pudding, turning dried fruit, spice and a little patience into something intended for a later feast. In 2026 that Sunday is 22 November. The date moves each year because it follows the church calendar rather than a fixed day in the month.

The name began with a prayer, not a recipe. The Book of Common Prayer’s collect associated with this Sunday opens with the words ‘Stir up’. Its subject is the will of the faithful and the doing of good works. The kitchen interpretation is a cheerful second life for the phrase. It links spiritual preparation for Christmas with the practical preparation of food, while leaving room for people who enjoy the custom without attending a service.

Advent begins on 29 November this year, so the Sunday immediately before it is today. In modern church calendars the same Sunday is also celebrated as Christ the King, but that does not erase the older nickname or the pudding tradition. Different layers of the calendar can coexist. One concerns the end of the liturgical year; another preserves a remembered phrase; another gives a household an enjoyable reason to gather around the table.

The National Trust traces Christmas pudding back through older forms of plum pottage, a dish that could contain meat alongside dried fruit. By the nineteenth century, stirring a sweeter pudding on the Sunday before Advent had become a tradition. This is a useful correction to the idea that everything on a Christmas table has always looked the same. Seasonal food changes with ingredients, taste and domestic habits, even when it comes to feel reassuringly ancient.

The custom remains alive in contemporary Britain. For this year’s Stir-up Sunday, the Church of England has published a pudding film with baker Jasmine Mitchell. Its recipe includes several kinds of dried fruit and offers fruit juice as an alternative to brandy. That small choice illustrates how a tradition can be adaptable. A household can keep the sense of shared preparation while making something that suits the people who will actually eat it.

For many families, the pleasure is as much in the making as in the eventual pudding. Measuring, mixing and taking turns make a task into an occasion. A pudding prepared in November also introduces a different pace into the approach to Christmas: something is being done now for a meal still weeks away. It is possible to enjoy that anticipation without believing that every family follows an identical ritual or that a homemade pudding is a test of a proper Christmas.

The tradition’s strongest documented home is Britain, though Christmas pudding belongs to festive tables in other places with British cultural connections too. There is no single international Stir-up Sunday ceremony. For someone abroad, the idea travels easily enough: set aside a little time for a dish that connects people and memories. For someone in the UK today, it might simply mean finding a bowl, choosing a trusted recipe and inviting another pair of hands. The original prayer concerns action as well as intention; the kitchen version gives those hands something warm and generous to do.
