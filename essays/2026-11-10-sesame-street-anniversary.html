Minor occasion · Tuesday 10 November 2026

Sesame Street’s birthday

On 10 November 1969, American public television stations broadcast the very first episode of Sesame Street. Fifty-seven years later it is still going, one of the longest-running and most influential television shows in the world. Generations of children learned their letters and numbers from Big Bird, Bert and Ernie, Oscar the Grouch and Cookie Monster, and today is the show's birthday.

A dinner party idea

The show began with a conversation in 1966 between Joan Ganz Cooney, a television producer, and Lloyd Morrisett, an educational psychologist and vice president of the Carnegie Corporation. In the late 1960s, almost every American household had a television, and young children were watching many hours a week, much of it programmes critics considered violent or simply commercial. Cooney and Morrisett asked a bold question: could television be used to help young children, especially from poorer families, get ready for school? In their famous phrase, the aim was to "master the addictive qualities of television and do something good with them".

After two years of research, a new organisation, the Children's Television Workshop, received a combined grant of 8 million dollars from the Carnegie Corporation, the Ford Foundation and the US government to make the show. Sesame Street was announced at a press conference in May 1969 and premiered that November.

A show built on research

What made Sesame Street different was its method. It was the first children's programme to be planned around a written curriculum with measurable educational goals, and the first whose effects on children were formally studied. Producers, writers, educators and researchers worked together, testing segments on real children to see what held their attention and what they actually learned. This way of working became known as the CTW model.

The show also borrowed shamelessly from commercial television. Short, punchy segments worked like advertisements, which is why episodes were famously "brought to you by" a letter and a number of the day. The puppeteer Jim Henson created the Muppets for the show, mixing them with a human cast on a realistic city street, a deliberate choice so that children in cities would see a neighbourhood like their own.

Growing up with the audience

Within ten years, nine million American children under six were watching every day, and studies showed it was making a real difference. Over time the show tackled harder subjects too. When Will Lee, the actor who played the shopkeeper Mr Hooper, died in 1982, the writers chose to tell children honestly that Mr Hooper had died, in an episode that is still remembered as one of the most moving moments in children's television. Later storylines took on marriage, birth, divorce, disability, and military families.

The show has had to change with the times. It moved from a fast "magazine" format to longer stories, and the Muppet Elmo became so popular that he got his own segment, Elmo's World. In 2016 first-run episodes moved to the American cable channel HBO, later to its streaming service, and on 10 November 2025, the show's 56th birthday, new episodes began appearing on Netflix, while PBS continued to show it in the United States.

Around the world, and in Britain

Sesame Street travelled far. By its 40th anniversary in 2009 it was being shown in more than 140 countries, with around twenty independently made local versions featuring their own characters, languages and street settings. It has won more Emmy Awards than any other children's programme.

Britain had a more complicated relationship with it. In the early 1970s the BBC declined to buy the show, with its head of children's programmes criticising its approach, though some ITV regions did broadcast it. Decades later, a British co-production, The Furchester Hotel, starring Elmo and a family of monsters running a seaside hotel, ran on CBeebies from 2014. Many British children of the 1970s and 1980s still remember the theme song and the sunny question it asks: can you tell me how to get to Sesame Street?
