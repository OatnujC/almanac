Minor occasion · Friday 13 November 2026

Robert Louis Stevenson born (1850)

Robert Louis Stevenson was born at 8 Howard Place in Edinburgh on 13 November 1850, 176 years ago today. He grew into one of the great storytellers of the 19th century, the author of Treasure Island, Kidnapped, Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde and A Child’s Garden of Verses, and today he is still one of the most translated authors in the world.

A lighthouse family

He was christened Robert Lewis Balfour Stevenson. His father, Thomas, was a leading lighthouse engineer, and lighthouses were the family business: his grandfather Robert Stevenson and his uncles Alan and David built many of the lights around Scotland’s coast. His mother, Margaret Balfour, came from a family of ministers and gentry. At about 18 he changed the spelling of Lewis to Louis, and in 1873 he dropped Balfour.

He was a sickly only child, prone to coughs and fevers, and spent long stretches in bed, cared for by his devoted nurse, Alison Cunningham, known as Cummy. She read him the Bible and John Bunyan and told him stories of the Covenanters, and he later dedicated A Child’s Garden of Verses to her. Its poem “The Land of Counterpane” recalls those days of playing with toy soldiers on his bedcovers. At 16 his father paid to print his first publication, a history of the Pentland Rising of 1666.

He went to the University of Edinburgh to study engineering but showed little interest, and in 1871 he told his father he wanted to be a writer. They agreed he would study law instead, and he qualified for the Scottish bar in 1875, but he never practised. His father even put up a brass plate reading “R.L. Stevenson, Advocate”.

Travels and love

Stevenson spent much of his twenties travelling for his health and his writing. A canoe trip through Belgium and France became his first travel book, An Inland Voyage, and a walk with a donkey called Modestine became Travels with a Donkey in the Cévennes, one of the first books to present hiking and camping as fun. The route is now a popular long-distance footpath in France, the Chemin de Stevenson.

In 1876, at an artists’ colony at Grez-sur-Loing, he met Fanny Van de Grift Osbourne, an American who was separated from her husband. In 1879, against the advice of his friends, he crossed the Atlantic and the whole of America to join her in California, and arrived close to death. They married in May 1880 and spent their honeymoon in an abandoned mining camp in Napa Valley.

The great books

Back in Britain, the Stevensons settled in 1884 at Bournemouth, in a house he named Skerryvore after a lighthouse built by his uncle. Though he was often bedridden, describing himself as living “like a weevil in a biscuit”, it was there and in the years around it that he wrote his most famous work. Treasure Island appeared in 1883; his friend the poet W. E. Henley, who had a wooden leg, is often thought to have inspired Long John Silver. Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde and Kidnapped followed in 1886.

Tusitala

After his father died in 1887, Stevenson sailed for the Pacific in search of a healthier climate. He visited Hawaii, where he became friends with King Kalākaua, and the Gilbert Islands, Tahiti and Samoa, and in 1890 he settled at Vailima on the Samoan island of Upolu. The Samoans called him Tusitala, the teller of tales. He grew alarmed at the way Britain, Germany and the United States were meddling in Samoa, wrote letters to The Times and a book on the subject, and his late fiction, such as The Beach of Falesá, turned towards a darker realism.

He died suddenly at Vailima on 3 December 1894, aged 44, probably of a brain haemorrhage. Samoans carried him to the top of Mount Vaea and buried him overlooking the sea. His grave bears his own poem, “Requiem”, ending with the lines “Home is the sailor, home from the sea, / And the hunter home from the hill.”

Today he is remembered in Edinburgh at the Writers’ Museum and with a bronze memorial in St Giles’ Cathedral, and there are Stevenson museums in Monterey, California, at Saranac Lake in New York and at his house at Vailima. In 1994 the Royal Bank of Scotland issued a commemorative £1 note bearing his face for the centenary of his death.
