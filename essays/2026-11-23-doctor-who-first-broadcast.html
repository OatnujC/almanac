Minor occasion · 23 November 2026

Doctor Who first broadcast: 63 years on

On Saturday 23 November 1963, a strange police box entered British television history. The first episode of Doctor Who was broadcast that evening, beginning the story later known as An Unearthly Child. Today is the programme's 63rd anniversary. This is a cultural anniversary, rather than a British public holiday: a date for remembering how a small television adventure grew into a shared imaginative world for audiences in the UK and far beyond it.

The opening story starts with an everyday kind of curiosity. Teachers Ian Chesterton and Barbara Wright are concerned about their pupil Susan Foreman. Following her home brings them to her mysterious grandfather, the Doctor, and to the TARDIS. The programme's official story guide describes them becoming unwilling passengers in his time ship. The contrast is the source of the wonder: a school, a pupil and a London junkyard become the threshold of something much larger than the characters expected.

William Hartnell played the first Doctor. Waris Hussein directed the opening adventure, and Verity Lambert was its producer. The British Film Institute's account identifies Hartnell and Hussein, while Hussein's own diaries, published by Radio Times, preserve the production world around Lambert, script editor David Whitaker and drama chief Sydney Newman. Remembering those people makes the anniversary more than a salute to a fictional traveller. Television is made through the decisions and cooperation of many people, often under pressures that an audience never sees.

There is a small timing detail worth handling carefully. Official anniversary material gives the first broadcast's scheduled slot as 5.15pm. Other historical accounts place the actual start a little later. For this almanac the secure point is the evening and the date: 23 November 1963, in UK time. An exact-to-the-second claim adds little unless the surviving transmission record is being examined. The beginning is remarkable enough without making a rounded schedule time carry more precision than it was meant to have.

The programme's anniversary tradition has developed through special stories and the return of familiar characters. The official Doctor Who anniversary history notes that The Five Doctors was shown in the United States on 23 November 1983 before its UK transmission. Its account also looks at other anniversary productions. These examples show how the date became part of the programme's relationship with its audience. They do not establish that a new episode or a particular event is scheduled for 23 November 2026, and none is promised here.

One way to mark the date in Britain is to return to an early story and notice its pace, performances and assumptions about the future. A viewer coming from modern television may find that encounter as revealing as the fictional journey itself. Another is to read about the makers, including Lambert and Hussein, whose work turned the premise into something that could actually be broadcast. These are suggested ways of observing a historical anniversary, rather than reports of a national celebration happening today.

The international anniversary history offers a gentle reminder that a programme can start in one broadcasting culture and come to belong, emotionally, to people in many others. A date in a BBC schedule becomes a date in a fan's personal calendar. Sixty-three years after the beginning, the original invitation still has its charm: follow a question a little further than seems sensible, step through an unlikely door, and discover that the familiar world contains more room than you thought.
