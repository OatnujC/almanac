Major occasion · 15 November 2026

Shichi-Go-San (Japan)

If you walk past a Shinto shrine in Japan around this time of year, you are likely to see very small children in very grand clothes: little girls in brightly patterned kimono with padded vests, boys of five in pleated hakama trousers and haori jackets, solemn toddlers clutching long paper bags, and proud parents and grandparents with cameras. This is Shichi-Go-San, literally "seven-five-three", a traditional rite of passage that celebrates children reaching three particular ages. Its fixed date is 15 November, which this year falls on a Sunday, so today is likely to be one of the busiest days of the season.

What it is

Shichi-Go-San is for girls aged three and seven and boys aged five (and in some families boys aged three too). Families take the children to a shrine, where a priest may perform a short purification and prayer, to give thanks that the children have grown safely so far and to ask for long, healthy lives ahead. It is not a national public holiday, so although its date is 15 November many families choose the nearest weekend, and in practice shrine visits spread across October and November.

Why these ages, and why this date

The ages 3, 5 and 7 fit East Asian numerology, which treats odd numbers as lucky. The custom is often traced to the Heian period, when court nobles marked their children's passage into middle childhood; some historians instead place its roots in the Muromachi period, when infant mortality was high and simply surviving the early years was worth celebrating. The observance is said to have been set to the fifteenth day of the month in the Kamakura period, and the tradition that it belongs specifically to 15 November is usually credited to the fifth Tokugawa shogun, Tsunayoshi, who held rites for his son Tokumatsu on that day in the late 1600s.

Traditionally the age counted was kazoedoshi, the old East Asian reckoning in which a baby is one year old at birth and everyone gains a year on New Year's Day. Some families still follow it, so a girl may celebrate her "three" at what Western counting would call two, and her "seven" at six; many others now use ordinary birthdays.

Three old ceremonies

Shichi-Go-San gathers up three separate ceremonies that the aristocracy and then the samurai class performed for centuries. For three-year-olds there was kamioki, "leaving the hair": until then, children's heads were often kept shaved, and from three they were allowed to grow their hair. For five-year-old boys there was hakamagi, when a boy first put on the formal hakama, rather like the old Western custom of "breeching" a small boy. For seven-year-old girls there was obitoki, when a girl stopped tying her kimono with simple cords and wore a proper obi sash for the first time, a step towards adulthood. In the Kamakura period obitoki was held at nine, for boys as well as girls; by the Edo period it had settled at seven, for girls.

By the Meiji era the celebration had spread from the samurai to ordinary townspeople and had taken its modern shape, centred on the shrine visit. The haircutting custom has long since faded, but the clothing has stayed at the heart of the day: for many children Shichi-Go-San is the first time they wear a kimono or hakama.

Thousand-year sweets

The day's emblem is chitose ame, "thousand-year candy": long, thin sticks of red and white sugar candy, symbolising healthy growth and a long life. They come in paper bags printed with a crane and a turtle, both traditional Japanese symbols of longevity, and the candy is wrapped in a thin edible starch film called oblaat. Pictures of toddlers trailing bags almost as long as themselves are a classic Shichi-Go-San image.

How it is marked today

The modern celebration has changed surprisingly little since the Meiji period, but photography has become a central part of it. Studios do a busy trade in formal portraits, often with kimono rental included, and many families book their photos weeks before or after the shrine visit, when it is quieter. Some children wear Western formal dress instead of kimono. Large shrines such as Meiji Jingu in Tokyo see steady crowds of families, and afterwards there is often a celebratory family meal.

The festival has a cosy place in Japanese popular culture too: it turns up in long-running cartoons such as Crayon Shin-chan and Chibi Maruko-chan, where the families' outings to the shrine are played for gentle comedy. Underneath the fuss and the photographs lies the same simple thought as centuries ago, when reaching the age of seven was far from certain: gratitude that the children are growing up, and a wish that they keep doing so for a thousand years.
