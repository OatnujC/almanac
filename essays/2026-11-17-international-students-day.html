Major occasion · 17 November 2026

International Students’ Day and the Day of Struggle for Freedom and Democracy

Some dates are remembered for one thing. 17 November is remembered in Prague for two, half a century apart, and both began with students. Today is International Students’ Day, an observance kept by student unions and universities around the world. In the Czech Republic and Slovakia it is also a public holiday, officially called the Day of Struggle for Freedom and Democracy, which joins a night of Nazi terror in 1939 to the first evening of the Velvet Revolution in 1989.

An occupied country

The story starts with the dismantling of Czechoslovakia. After the Munich Agreement of autumn 1938, Britain, France and Italy let Hitler take the border regions known as the Sudetenland. On 15 March 1939 German troops marched into what was left, and the Czech lands became the Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia, nominally autonomous but in practice run from Berlin. Slovakia had been declared a separate client state of Nazi Germany the day before.

In those first months most Czechs resisted quietly. Concerts of Bedřich Smetana’s patriotic cycle Má vlast (My Country) turned into moments of spontaneous anthem-singing, until the authorities banned patriotic songs in public. On 30 September 1939, the first anniversary of Munich, Prague staged a boycott of public transport: almost everybody walked, and only uninformed Germans rode the trams. Leaflets then called for a mass demonstration on the most emotive date in the national calendar, 28 October, Czechoslovak Independence Day.

Jan Opletal

On 28 October 1939 crowds gathered in Wenceslas Square despite a BBC broadcast from the exiled diplomat Jan Masaryk urging people not to take risks. They sang the anthem in Czech and Slovak and called for the return of the free republic. German police opened fire. A 22-year-old worker, Václav Sedláček, was killed, and a popular medical student at Charles University, Jan Opletal, was badly wounded. He died of his injuries on 11 November.

Opletal’s funeral on 15 November drew around 3,000 students. It began peacefully, but groups started singing patriotic songs and shouting slogans, and the driver of Karl Hermann Frank, the Protectorate’s powerful State Secretary, was beaten. Some historians suspect the occupiers had allowed the procession in the expectation of trouble, to give themselves a pretext for a crackdown. Whether or not that is true, the crackdown came fast.

The night of 16–17 November 1939

On the night of 16 November the Gestapo raided student halls of residence in Prague and Brno, dragging hundreds of students from their beds and homes. More than 1,200 were sent to Sachsenhausen concentration camp, near Berlin. At dawn on 17 November nine men accused of leading the protests were shot without trial: eight students, Jaroslav Klíma, Jan Weinert, Josef Adamec, Jan Černý, Marek Frauwirth, Bedřich Koula, Václav Šafránek and František Skorkovský, and one lecturer, Josef Matoušek. All Czech universities and colleges were closed, at first for three years and in the end until the war was over, affecting nearly 20,000 students and teachers. The next day the Protectorate’s puppet president, Emil Hácha, appealed to Czechs to stop what he called senseless resistance.

From London to the world

Those events would probably have stayed a Czech tragedy were it not for exiles in Britain. Czechoslovak soldiers based in England, among them former Prague students who had escaped, persuaded student organisations from fourteen countries, including Britain, France, the Netherlands, Greece and Poland, to adopt 17 November as an annual International Students’ Day. It was proclaimed in London in 1941 through the International Students’ Council, and that year many British universities paused their lectures to read the proclamation aloud in honour of the executed students. It is one of the few international days born in the middle of the Second World War, and its first purpose was simple: not to let the occupiers erase what had happened.

1989: the anniversary that toppled a regime

After the war 17 November became a national day in Czechoslovakia. Under communism the regime took over the anniversary for its own messages, and after the Warsaw Pact invasion of 1968 the ceremonies became formulaic. Then came the 50th anniversary, in 1989. On 17 November, eight days after the Berlin Wall fell, independent student leaders and the official Socialist Union of Youth organised a commemorative march. About 15,000 people gathered at the Albertov campus of Charles University. One speaker, Josef Šárka, had been at Opletal’s funeral in 1939, and told the crowd he was glad they were fighting for the same thing his generation had.

The students then left the agreed route and headed towards Wenceslas Square, singing the anthem. On Národní třída riot police boxed them in and beat them with truncheons. A rumour spread that a student had been killed; it was false, and the motionless ‘student’ turned out to be an undercover agent, but it hardly mattered. That night students and theatre actors agreed to strike. Within days hundreds of thousands were filling Wenceslas Square, and by the end of the year the Communist Party had given up its monopoly on power and the dissident playwright Václav Havel was president. Because it was so peaceful, it became known as the Velvet Revolution.

How it is marked today

In Czechia and Slovakia 17 November is a public holiday, and in Prague people leave flowers and candles at the memorial plaque on Národní třída, where the 1989 march was stopped, and gather at Albertov and outside the Hlávka hall of residence, one of the buildings raided in 1939. There are concerts, debates and, in recent years, open-air festivals celebrating democracy. Universities hold ceremonies for the nine men shot in 1939.

Elsewhere the day has drifted into something gentler. Many universities use it, sometimes on a nearby date, to celebrate their international students, with food fairs, flag displays and cultural evenings, and student unions use it to campaign on fees, access and academic freedom. The European Students’ Union and national unions often mark it, and some countries hold their own versions on other dates. If you see your local campus filled with stalls and flags this week, that is the cheerful modern face of a day that began with nine executions.

There is a neat symmetry to it. In 1939 students who wanted a free country were shot and their universities shut. Fifty years later to the day, their successors marched in their memory and started the chain of events that delivered the very freedom the first group had demanded.
