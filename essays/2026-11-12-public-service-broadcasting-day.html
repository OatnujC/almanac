Public Service Broadcasting Day (India)

In India, 12 November is Public Service Broadcasting Day, a day to celebrate radio and television as a service to the public rather than a business or a mouthpiece. It commemorates a single, very human broadcast: on 12 November 1947, Mahatma Gandhi went to the studios of All India Radio in Delhi, the national broadcaster now known as Akashvani, and spoke to hundreds of thousands of refugees from the Partition of India who were living in a vast tented camp at Kurukshetra. It was his only visit to the broadcaster’s studios, and, according to Akashvani, his first and last live broadcast with them.

A country on the move

Three months earlier, in August 1947, British India had been partitioned into India and Pakistan. The division set off one of the largest and most violent migrations in history, as millions of Hindus and Sikhs moved east and millions of Muslims moved west, amid terrible killings. Many of those who fled into India from the west ended up in camps. One of the largest was at Kurukshetra, in what is now Haryana. The Tribune reports that around 300,000 people were living in its huge tented camp at the time, and that the camp was finally closed in April 1950.

Gandhi, then 78, was in Delhi, trying to calm communal violence and holding prayer meetings each evening. He wanted to go to Kurukshetra in person but was tied up with meetings of the Congress Working Committee. As he explained in the broadcast itself, the industrialist Ghanshyam Das Birla suggested he speak to the refugees by radio instead.

The broadcast

The text of the talk is preserved in The Collected Works of Mahatma Gandhi. Gandhi spoke in Hindustani. As he was led into the studio, he joked that the staff could regard him as an almost uncivilised person and tutor him about his talk. He began: My suffering brothers and sisters. He said he did not know whether only they, or others too, were listening, and that this was only his second experience of the radio, the first having been in London years before, when he attended the Round Table Conference in 1931.

He told the refugees that he had been distressed to hear that more than two lakh, or 200,000, of them had arrived at Kurukshetra, with more pouring in, and that to suffer with the afflicted and try to relieve their suffering had been his life’s work. The talk was not just comfort. Gandhi was honest about the hardship, acknowledging that many were still without shelter as the cold Punjab winter came on, but he also urged the refugees to cooperate with the camp authorities and with each other, to keep discipline and to take the sanitation of the camp into their own hands. His service, he said, was not only for them or for India but for the world.

There was one especially poignant line. The broadcast fell on Diwali, the festival of lights. Today is Diwali, Gandhi said. But there can be no lighting of chirags, the small oil lamps lit for the festival, for you or for anyone. The best way to celebrate it, he suggested, would be through service to the refugees, and for them by living in the camp as brothers and treating everyone as their own.

From one talk to a national day

Public Service Broadcasting Day was first marked in 2001, and it has been kept every 12 November since. The choice of Gandhi’s visit as its anchor says something about what Indian public broadcasting likes to think it is for: reaching people in trouble, in their own language, with a message of calm and responsibility. Akashvani reports that Gandhi described the radio as a shakti, a miraculous power of God.

All India Radio itself had taken that name in 1936 and later adopted the name Akashvani, meaning a voice from the sky. Along with the television service Doordarshan, it is part of Prasar Bharati, India’s public service broadcaster. Akashvani still broadcasts in a remarkable number of Indian languages and dialects, and in many rural areas radio remains a lifeline for news, farming advice and entertainment.

How it is marked

On the day, Akashvani Delhi holds a special programme at its headquarters, and the broadcaster carries programmes about Gandhi and the role of public service media. The broadcaster has restored the studio in Delhi where Gandhi made his address, and recordings of the talk are often played. Newspapers, such as The Tribune, use the anniversary to revisit the history of the Kurukshetra camp and the refugees who rebuilt their lives across north India.

For British readers, the idea will feel familiar. The BBC grew out of the same belief that broadcasting should inform, educate and entertain the whole public. India’s version of the day has a particular emotional weight, though, because it is tied to a moment when a frail old man, unable to reach the people he wanted to comfort, used the radio to sit with them in their grief instead.
