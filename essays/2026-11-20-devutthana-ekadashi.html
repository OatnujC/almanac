Major occasion · 20 November 2026

Devutthana Ekadashi (Prabodhini Ekadashi)

Devutthana Ekadashi, also called Prabodhini or Dev Uthani Ekadashi, celebrates the awakening of Vishnu from his period of divine rest. It marks the close of Chaturmas, the four holy months associated with the monsoon season. For many Hindu communities it is a point of renewal, when a period of particular vows and disciplines comes to an end. In 2026 Drik Panchang’s calendar for London places the observance on Friday 20 November. Other traditions keep it tomorrow, so there is an important qualification to that date.

The festival belongs to Kartika Shukla Ekadashi: the eleventh lunar day of the bright, or waxing, half of Kartika. A lunar day is not the same thing as a midnight-to-midnight civil day. Drik Panchang gives the relevant London interval as 1.45am on 20 November to 1.01am on 21 November, in UK time. Rules for choosing the fasting day involve more than the mere presence of a lunar date. Location and religious tradition both matter, which is why a calendar should identify whose calculation it is using.

BAPS’s 2026 calendar distinguishes the Smarta Ekadashi on 20 November from its Prabodhini Ekadashi observance on 21 November. It places the beginning of Tulsi Vivah on that later date too. This is not a reason to treat either community’s calendar as a mistake. A family’s temple or established tradition is the appropriate guide for its observance. Today’s almanac records the London date supplied by Drik Panchang while making the alternative explicit, rather than presenting one date as universal for all Hindus everywhere.

The sacred stories give this seasonal turning point its meaning. BAPS describes accounts in which Vishnu returns after a four-month rest, or after guarding King Bali’s door. These are religious narratives, conveying a divine pattern of rest, return and renewed activity. The organisation’s account also reads awakening as an inward invitation: a devotee should become more aware of the self and of God. The calendar’s change is therefore connected with spiritual attention, not simply the resumption of a busier social season.

Practices vary, but fasting, prayer, worship and offerings are important. In the Swaminarayan tradition BAPS describes a waterless fast and an offering of fresh vegetables called a hatadi, later distributed as prasad. Its historical report from Neasden in London records an elaborate vegetable offering in 2006, demonstrating how the observance has been carried into British temple life. That report illustrates a tradition; it is not a notice of a 2026 event. Devotees should use current local temple information for this year’s arrangements.

The date also has particular historical significance within the Swaminarayan community. BAPS connects it with Swaminarayan’s initiation in 1800 and his appointment as leader of the fellowship in 1801, remembered according to the religious calendar. Thus a single festival can hold a wider Hindu account of Vishnu’s awakening and a community’s own history of spiritual leadership. For a visitor or reader in Britain, that layering is worth noticing. The occasion is best understood through the people who keep it, with enough care to respect both their shared stories and their different dates.
