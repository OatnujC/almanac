Minor occasion · 21 November 2026

Bloody Sunday in Dublin, 1920

On 21 November 1920, Dublin experienced a day of killings remembered as Bloody Sunday. It belongs to the history of Ireland’s War of Independence, and its best-known scene is Croke Park, where fourteen people died following the entry of Crown forces during a Gaelic football match. Today is the 106th anniversary. This is a historical remembrance, not a festive observance. To approach it well is to keep the people who died in view while examining the political violence that brought the day about.

The morning began with an IRA operation planned by Michael Collins against the British intelligence network in Dublin. Croke Park’s historical account records fourteen deaths in those shootings, including intelligence officers and members of the Auxiliary Force. Descriptions of the victims’ exact roles require care; the whole group should not casually be reduced to one convenient label. The operation formed part of the struggle over intelligence and control during the war. Its place in the day’s sequence matters, but it does not remove the responsibility for what happened to spectators later.

That afternoon Dublin and Tipperary met at Croke Park for a challenge match, with proceeds intended for the Republican Prisoners Dependents Fund. A large crowd assembled despite fears of reprisals. Crown forces entered the ground and opened fire. Fourteen people, including the Tipperary player Michael Hogan, died, and many more were injured. The GAA’s account names each of those killed, restoring individuality to a number often repeated in summaries. The youngest victims were children; others had come to watch an afternoon’s sport.

University College Cork’s mapping project places the Croke Park killings within the wider escalation of the war in November 1920. Maps can help make the geography understandable, but they should be read alongside testimony and individual histories. The National Museum of Ireland’s chronology also recalls the deaths of three prisoners at Dublin Castle that evening. The whole day therefore reaches beyond the stadium, even though Croke Park has become its most recognisable place of remembrance. Following the sequence avoids turning a complex day into one isolated image.

Remembrance has continued through the institutions that preserve this history. The Hogan Stand was named for Michael Hogan in 1925. The GAA Museum offers historical interpretation and commemorative tours, and its centenary programme gathered lectures and other material for later readers. In 2019 the GAA also unveiled headstones for victims who had lain in unmarked graves. That work gives remembrance a practical form: researching a life, identifying a burial and making sure a name can be found. These examples have their own dates; they are not being presented as events taking place today.

For readers in Britain, the anniversary is an invitation to learn carefully about a part of the shared and contested history of these islands. It must also be distinguished from other events called Bloody Sunday, particularly the later killings in Derry. Reading the Croke Park accounts or the university’s maps offers a grounded way to begin. No UK ceremony or 2026 stadium programme is assumed here. The date’s enduring purpose is remembrance informed by evidence: people went to a match, violence entered an ordinary gathering, and families were left to live with the consequences long after the ground fell quiet.
