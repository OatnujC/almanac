Minor occasion · 15 November 2026

Day of the Imprisoned Writer

Every year on 15 November, PEN International and its centres around the world mark the Day of the Imprisoned Writer. It is a day to remember writers, poets, journalists and publishers who are in prison, under threat or missing because of what they have written, to campaign for named individuals, and to commemorate writers killed in the previous year. The idea is simple and old-fashioned in the best sense: that letters, appeals and public attention can make it harder for governments to forget the people they have locked away.

Where it comes from

PEN was founded in London in 1921 as a fellowship of writers, and defending writers under attack became one of its central purposes. Its Writers in Prison Committee monitors cases around the world and mobilises PEN's network on behalf of people persecuted for their work by governments, armed groups or other powerful actors. According to PEN International, the idea of a dedicated day was first proposed by the American playwright Arthur Miller, then PEN's international president, in the late 1960s. The Writers in Prison Committee officially launched the Day of the Imprisoned Writer in 1981, and it has been held on 15 November ever since.

The date itself is not tied to a particular anniversary; its power comes from repetition, with PEN centres in more than 100 countries acting together on the same day. In recent years the campaign has run from 15 November to 10 December, Human Rights Day, the anniversary of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, whose Article 19 protects freedom of opinion and expression.

How it works

Each year PEN picks a handful of emblematic cases, usually one from each region of the world, to stand for the broader struggle. Supporters are asked to write appeals to the authorities holding them, send messages of solidarity to the writers themselves, share their names, and report back on any responses. PEN centres organise readings, debates, performances and letter-writing evenings. Over the years the featured writers have included some very well-known names, such as the Chinese writer Liu Xiaobo, later awarded the Nobel Peace Prize while in prison, the Turkish novelist Orhan Pamuk, who faced prosecution over his comments about history, and the Turkish-Armenian editor Hrant Dink, who was murdered in 2007, as well as many writers whose names would otherwise be little known outside their own countries.

This year's five

PEN International's 2026 campaign, titled "Free Them All", asks people to stand with five writers:

Pham Doan Trang, an author and journalist in Vietnam, imprisoned since October 2020 and serving a nine-year sentence for allegedly "spreading propaganda against the state". A UN working group found her detention arbitrary in 2021.

Henry Constantín Ferreiro, a Cuban journalist, writer, photographer and director of La Hora de Cuba, detained since 16 September 2026 on charges of "disobedience" and held in pre-trial detention in a maximum-security prison in Camagüey.

Ahmed Mansoor, an Emirati poet, blogger and human rights defender, imprisoned since March 2017 and serving sentences totalling 25 years on national security charges, reportedly in harsh isolation.

Vacłaŭ Areška, a Belarusian editor, political scientist and trade union activist, arrested in April 2022 during a crackdown on independent unions and sentenced in January 2023 on charges including taking part in an "extremist" group.

Innocent Bahati, a Rwandan poet known for critical writing on social issues, missing since 7 February 2021; PEN continues to demand that the authorities account for his whereabouts.

Introducing the campaign, Ma Thida, chair of PEN International's Writers in Prison Committee and herself a former prisoner in Myanmar, said that writers are targeted "because words travel. A poem can cross a border its author cannot."

The wider picture

The day lands against a worrying background. PEN America's Freedom to Write Index, published in the run-up to this year's day, found that a record 401 writers were detained or imprisoned in 44 countries in 2025, the highest number of countries it has recorded. PEN America is using the day to encourage people simply to read the work of imprisoned writers, a quiet act of solidarity that anyone can join. Whether through a letter, a reading or a name said out loud, the day's message is that silence is exactly what jailers hope for, and that noise helps.
