Minor occasion · 20 November 2026

The retreat from Fort Lee: 250 years on

Two hundred and fifty years ago today, on 20 November 1776, British troops crossed the Hudson River and forced the Americans to abandon Fort Lee in New Jersey. It was another serious reverse for George Washington’s army during the campaign for New York. Yet the soldiers escaped rather than being trapped in their position. The anniversary belongs to a difficult kind of military history: an army losing ground while trying to preserve the ability to fight again. Survival mattered more than holding one more fort.

The position was part of a paired defence. Fort Lee stood on the high ground west of the Hudson, opposite Fort Washington on Manhattan. American planners hoped artillery and obstructions in the river could make passage costly for British ships. Fort Lee was initially called Fort Constitution and was renamed in honour of General Charles Lee. Its strategic importance came from the river below it. Control of the Hudson could shape communications and movement between the colonies, so its banks became a focus of the 1776 campaign.

The British had already taken Fort Washington on 16 November. Four days later, General William Howe ordered Charles Cornwallis to take troops across the Hudson north of Fort Lee. The National Park Service’s account describes Cornwallis’s early-morning operation and the capture of the abandoned fort and substantial supplies. The American position could no longer be treated as secure. Retreat meant accepting the loss of equipment and a prepared base in order to avoid the much worse possibility of losing the force defending it.

Washington’s army then moved across New Jersey, crossing rivers and continuing south towards the Delaware. The National Park Service traces the route through New Bridge Landing, Newark, New Brunswick, Princeton and Trenton. Cornwallis pursued, but failed to destroy the army. The later American counterstroke at Trenton in December is often the better-remembered episode. Its possibility depended on soldiers and commanders getting through the earlier crisis. The November retreat is therefore part of the same story, even though it offers none of the immediate satisfaction of a victory.

Fort Lee Historic Park now preserves the setting through a reconstructed encampment, visitor displays and living-history activities. The park’s recurring Retreat Day programme is held on the weekend nearest the historical date and describes music, cooking and artillery demonstrations. Its current 2026 page still asks readers to watch for updates, so this essay does not assign an unconfirmed event time to today. The distinction is useful: 20 November is the actual 250th anniversary, while public interpretation can be arranged on a nearby day.

For a British reader, the anniversary offers a view of the American Revolution before its outcome was known. British military success was real, but it did not guarantee that the rebellion would end. The park’s later phrase Retreat to Victory should not erase the uncertainty experienced at the time. One way to mark the date from the UK is to follow the retreat on a map and compare the commanders’ immediate aims with what happened later. The story becomes more human when the famous eventual result is set aside for a moment, and the problem is simply how to get an army safely away.
