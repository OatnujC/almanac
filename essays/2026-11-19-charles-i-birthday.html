The birthday of Charles I

Charles I was born on 19 November 1600 at Dunfermline Palace in Fife. His 426th birth anniversary is an opportunity to consider a life whose consequences reached far beyond the royal household. He became king of England, Scotland and Ireland, fought with his parliaments and many of his subjects, and was eventually tried and executed. The date belongs in an almanac as a historical birthday, rather than as a national celebration. Remembering a birth need not mean approving of the reign that followed.

Charles was the second son of James VI of Scotland and Anne of Denmark. When James also inherited the English throne in 1603, the family’s position changed dramatically. Charles was not initially expected to succeed him: his elder brother Henry was heir. Henry’s death in 1612 placed Charles in that position, and he became king in 1625. The Royal Collection’s account describes an educated court in which Charles developed a lasting interest in art, an interest that would become one of the most striking features of his kingship.

Political power was much harder for him to manage. The central quarrel concerned the authority of the Crown and Parliament, particularly the right to raise money and the obligation to hear grievances. Parliament’s history records Charles’s forced loan of 1626 and the imprisonment of some who refused to pay. In 1628 he assented to the Petition of Right, which challenged taxation without parliamentary consent and arbitrary imprisonment. These were practical conflicts over money and liberty, as well as arguments about the principles of government.

In 1629 Charles dissolved Parliament and governed without it for eleven years. Attempts to raise revenue, including the extension of ship money inland, deepened opposition. Religion brought further strain, especially in Scotland, where resistance to royal religious changes developed into armed conflict. The expense of the Bishops’ Wars helped force Charles to summon Parliament again in 1640. The subsequent civil wars involved all three kingdoms in different ways. Reducing them to a simple personal disagreement between an English king and English MPs misses much of their scale.

The end came through a startling public trial. Charles appeared in Westminster Hall in January 1649, charged with tyranny and treason. He challenged the court’s authority to judge him. Parliament’s account preserves a vivid detail: John Bradshaw, who presided, wore a protective, bullet-proof hat. Charles was condemned and executed at Whitehall on 30 January. The trial remains a powerful episode to revisit because the question of who could hold a monarch accountable was being acted out before spectators, with irreversible consequences.

Alongside that political history sits his remarkable collection. Charles acquired works by major European artists and supported painters including Anthony van Dyck. After his execution, his possessions were sold and many pictures travelled across Europe. Charles II recovered part of the collection after the Restoration in 1660, but other works remained abroad. Today the birthday can be marked quietly through museum collections, portraits and Parliament’s historical records, all accessible to readers in Britain and beyond. Looking from an imposing royal portrait to the documents of the king’s trial reveals an extraordinary distance between the image of unquestioned authority and the reality of a kingdom at war.
