Minor occasion · 19 November 2026

Garifuna Settlement Day

Early on the morning of 19 November, people in the Belizean town of Dangriga gather at the water’s edge to watch dugout canoes come in from the sea. On board are men, women and children in costume, carrying cassava, plantain and sugar cane, singing to the beat of drums. This is Yurumein, the re-enactment that opens Garifuna Settlement Day, one of the largest cultural celebrations in Belize and a national public and bank holiday.

Who the Garifuna are

The Garifuna, or Garinagu, are a people of mixed African and Indigenous Caribbean ancestry who traditionally speak Garifuna, an Arawakan language. Their story begins on the Caribbean island of Saint Vincent, which they knew as Yurumein. In the 18th century, after Britain took control of the island in 1763, the people the British called “Black Caribs” fought a series of Carib Wars to keep their land, led above all by their chief, Joseph Chatoyer. After their defeat in 1796, the British decided to deport them. In 1797 several thousand were shipped away; when they landed on the island of Roatán, off Honduras, on 12 April 1797, only about 2,500 had survived the journey. From there they spread along the Caribbean coast of Central America, north into what is now Belize and Guatemala and south to Nicaragua.

Why 19 November

Garinagu began arriving in Belize, then the British settlement that became British Honduras, as early as 1802, when a group of about 150 settled at Yarborough in what is now Belize City. The celebration on 19 November commemorates the arrival of the largest group. According to an account by Abraham Ramos, son of the day’s founder, published in the Belizean newspaper Amandala, Alejo Beni and several companions met the superintendent of the settlement, Major-General Edward Codd, on 31 March 1823 and won permission for a large group of Garinagu from Honduras to settle. They went back to fetch their people and returned on 19 November 1823, landing at what is now Dangriga.

You will sometimes see the year given as 1832. Belizean sources say this is a mix-up. The family of the day’s founder have long pointed out that Codd was superintendent from 1823 to 1829, so the permission could not have come in 1832, and Amandala adds that 19 November fell on a Wednesday in 1823, matching the family’s records, but not in 1832. Belize’s living heritage inventory, Amandala and the Garifuna historian Sebastian Cayetano all give 1823, and in 2023 Belize marked 200 years since that landing.

A holiday won by lobbying

The day was the work of Thomas Vincent Ramos, a Garifuna leader and Belizean patriot. In 1941 he, together with Mateo Avaloy and C. S. Benguche, wrote to the District Commissioner of Stann Creek asking for a public and bank holiday there. First known as Carib Disembarkment Day, it was granted to Stann Creek Town, now Dangriga, in 1943 and later extended to Punta Gorda. In the 1970s Garifuna leaders pressed for it to become national, and in 1977 it was granted across the whole country and renamed Garifuna Settlement Day. Ramos’s own tribute is part of the season, held each year on 13 November, the anniversary of his death.

How the day is celebrated

“19th Celebrations”, as Belizeans call it, starts with Yurumein. Participants paddle dories to shore re-enacting the journey from Saint Vincent to Roatán and then to Belize. On landing, one of Chatoyer’s captains asks the authorities for permission to settle and is refused twice; only at the third attempt are they allowed ashore. The voyagers include figures from history such as Chatoyer and Alejo Beni, along with an old man and an old woman, a pregnant woman, other adults and children. The procession is led by the black, white and yellow flag of the Garifuna nation, and moves, singing and dancing to the primero and segunda drums and the sisira, to church for a Mass of Thanksgiving.

The celebrations have grown into a month of events coordinated by the National Garifuna Council and its branches across the country, in places including Dangriga, Hopkins, Punta Gorda, Belize City, Orange Walk, San Ignacio and Libertad Village. There are drumming competitions such as the Battle of the Drums in Punta Gorda, concerts, and plenty of food, including hudut, a dish of fish and mashed plantain. Belize’s other communities join in too, and Belize’s heritage project notes that many Belizeans treat November and Settlement Day almost like a season of their own, waiting until it is over before turning to Christmas.

The wider world has taken note of the culture behind the day. In 2001 UNESCO proclaimed the language, dance and music of the Garifuna in Belize, Honduras and Nicaragua a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity. As the Garifuna historian Sebastian Cayetano put it on the bicentenary, “we are still going strong.”
