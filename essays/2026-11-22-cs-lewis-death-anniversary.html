Minor occasion · 22 November 2026

C. S. Lewis remembered (1963)

C. S. Lewis died on 22 November 1963, the same day that John F. Kennedy was assassinated. Today marks sixty-three years since the death of the writer whose work ranges from literary scholarship and Christian argument to the imagined country of Narnia. It is a literary and personal anniversary, not a general public holiday. For readers who first met him through a wardrobe and a snowy wood, it is a chance to discover the wider life behind the stories.

Clive Staples Lewis was born in Belfast on 29 November 1898. His birthday is therefore next week, not today. He studied and worked in England, serving in the British Army during the First World War and being wounded in 1918. Later, he became a Fellow and Tutor in English Literature at Oxford and then held the chair of Medieval and Renaissance Literature at Cambridge. The academic career was substantial in its own right, alongside the books that reached a much larger audience.

During the Second World War, Lewis made radio broadcasts on Christianity. Material from those talks later became part of Mere Christianity. The change of medium matters: these were ideas shaped for listeners as well as readers, and for people trying to think about belief during a period of enormous uncertainty. His popular religious writing belongs beside his scholarship and fiction, rather than being treated as an accidental sideline to the children’s books.

The seven Chronicles of Narnia brought him another kind of readership. The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe appeared in 1950, beginning a sequence that continues to introduce children to his imaginative world. His other works include The Screwtape Letters, The Great Divorce and science fiction beginning with Out of the Silent Planet. The range helps explain why two readers can both feel they know Lewis while having encountered almost entirely different shelves of his work.

In 1956 he married Joy Gresham, whose later death became part of the personal story dramatised in Shadowlands. Lewis himself was buried at Holy Trinity Church in Headington, Oxford. Westminster Abbey’s connection is a memorial rather than his burial place. On 22 November 2013, exactly fifty years after his death, a stone was unveiled in Poets’ Corner. Its dedication service remembered him as a scholar, a Christian apologist and a storyteller, holding those aspects of his life together.

That memorial offers a lasting British place of recognition, while the readership is international. The author’s official publishing site describes the continuing reach of the books and their adaptations. This does not make today an organised worldwide Lewis festival, and no special 2026 event is assumed here. Readers can mark the anniversary more simply: by revisiting a familiar chapter, trying a book outside the genre they already know, or discussing an argument without feeling obliged to agree with it.

Lewis’s death shares a date with a political event that dominated the news, but his remembrance need not depend on that coincidence. His work offers its own reasons to pause: the relationship between reason and imagination, the difficulty of grief, and the possibility that a story can make familiar things look strange again. Today gives those questions a place in the calendar. The enduring act of commemoration happens whenever a reader opens a book and begins a fresh conversation with it.
