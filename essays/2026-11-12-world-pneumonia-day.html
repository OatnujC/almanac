Major occasion · Thursday 12 November 2026

World Pneumonia Day

Today is World Pneumonia Day, a global day of action against a disease that most of us think of as something from the past, or something that only happens to the very old. In fact pneumonia remains the single biggest infectious killer of both children and adults on the planet. The Every Breath Counts Coalition, which now leads the day, cites Global Burden of Disease estimates that pneumonia killed around 2.5 million people in 2023, including about 610,000 children under the age of five. Most of those deaths could have been prevented or treated with tools that already exist.

What pneumonia is

Pneumonia is an infection of the lungs. As the World Health Organization explains, our lungs are made up of millions of tiny air sacs called alveoli, which fill with air when a healthy person breathes in. In pneumonia those sacs fill instead with pus and fluid, which makes breathing painful and cuts the amount of oxygen the body can take in. The name comes from the Greek word for lung.

It is not one germ but many. Viruses, bacteria and fungi can all cause it. In children the most common bacterial cause is Streptococcus pneumoniae, followed by Haemophilus influenzae type b, usually shortened to Hib, while respiratory syncytial virus, RSV, is the most common viral cause. The germs often live harmlessly in a child’s nose or throat and cause trouble only when they are breathed down into the lungs, or they spread through droplets from coughs and sneezes. In a small child, the warning signs that health workers are trained to look for are fast breathing and the lower chest drawing inwards when the child breathes in, rather than expanding.

Why a world day was needed

For a disease that kills so many, pneumonia has always attracted surprisingly little attention. It has no single famous pathogen, no dramatic outbreaks to make headlines and no celebrity cause attached to it. By the late 2000s health campaigners had started calling it the forgotten killer of children: it was claiming more young lives than AIDS, malaria and measles combined, yet it rarely made the news.

So on 12 November 2009 more than 100 organisations, banded together as the Global Coalition against Child Pneumonia, held the first World Pneumonia Day. Supporters included Save the Children, the GAVI vaccine alliance, the Sabin Vaccine Institute and the Global Health Council. There is no special historical meaning to the date itself; it was simply the day chosen for that first campaign, and it has been kept every 12 November since. On that first day, the World Health Organization and UNICEF published a Global Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of Pneumonia, which estimated that a million children’s lives a year could be saved if proven prevention and treatment were made widely available in the poorest countries.

Who is at risk

Pneumonia hits hardest at the two ends of life. For children, the WHO notes that deaths are highest in southern Asia and sub-Saharan Africa, and that malnutrition, being born with a weakened immune system, and conditions such as HIV and measles all raise the risk. Every Breath Counts says that around 60 per cent of child pneumonia deaths are linked to malnutrition. The air a child breathes matters too: homes where cooking and heating rely on wood, dung or other polluting fuels fill small lungs with smoke day after day.

At the other end of life, ageing populations mean that more and more pneumonia deaths are among older adults. The coalition points out that more than one in four pneumonia deaths among people over 70 can be put down to air pollution and smoking. The Canadian physician William Osler, writing over a century ago, called pneumonia the captain of the men of death, and for older people it is still very often the illness that ends a life weakened by something else.

What works

The frustrating, and also hopeful, thing about pneumonia is how much we already know about stopping it. Vaccines against Hib, pneumococcus, measles and whooping cough are, in the WHO’s words, the most effective way to prevent pneumonia in children. Exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months helps babies fight off infection and recover faster when they do fall ill. Cleaner cooking stoves and good hygiene in crowded homes reduce the number of children who get sick in the first place.

When a child does develop bacterial pneumonia, the treatment is cheap: the WHO’s first choice is amoxicillin in dispersible tablets that dissolve in water, which trained community health workers can give close to home. Yet the WHO estimates that only about a third of children with pneumonia receive the antibiotics they need. For the sickest patients, the missing ingredient is often medical oxygen, which is still in short supply in many hospitals around the world, along with the simple pulse oximeters that tell a nurse who needs it.

This year’s theme

The theme for 2026 is A New Era of Pneumonia Control. It reflects genuine progress: newer pneumococcal conjugate vaccines that protect against more strains, new ways to protect babies and older people from RSV, including vaccines and long-acting antibodies, and better tools for diagnosis. The Forum of International Respiratory Societies, whose members include the American College of Chest Physicians, is using the day to call on governments and donors to make sure those advances actually reach the communities at greatest risk, and to keep tackling the old drivers of the disease: malnutrition, dirty air, tobacco and gaps in immunisation.

How the day is marked

World Pneumonia Day is not a holiday so much as a coordinated push. Health ministries, charities and medical societies publish new figures and reports, hospitals run awareness events, and campaigners lobby for funding for vaccines, oxygen and clean air. In India, which the medical news site Medindia notes recorded the largest estimated number of under-five pneumonia deaths of any country in 2023, newspapers and broadcasters use the day to remind parents of the free pneumococcal vaccine offered through the national immunisation programme and of the extra danger winter smog brings.

In Britain, the day is a timely reminder as winter arrives. The NHS offers a pneumococcal vaccine to babies, to people aged 65 and over and to people with certain long-term health conditions, and it now offers RSV vaccination to some older adults and to pregnant women to protect their newborns. The simplest way to mark World Pneumonia Day may be to check that you, your children or an older relative are up to date with the jabs on offer, and to remember that every breath really does count.
