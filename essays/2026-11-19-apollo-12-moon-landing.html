Minor occasion · 19 November 2026

Apollo 12: the second Moon landing

Fifty-seven years ago today, on 19 November 1969, a second crew landed on the Moon. Charles “Pete” Conrad and Alan Bean brought Apollo 12’s lunar module, Intrepid, down in Oceanus Procellarum, the Ocean of Storms. Richard Gordon remained in lunar orbit in the command module Yankee Clipper. Apollo 11 had answered the enormous question of whether people could reach the surface and come home. Apollo 12 asked a more practical question: could they land close to a chosen destination and carry out a planned scientific visit?

The answer was yes. Intrepid touched down close to Surveyor 3, an uncrewed American spacecraft that had reached the Moon in 1967. NASA’s mission account puts the separation at about 535 feet, or roughly 163 metres. That was an extraordinary piece of navigation after a journey from Earth. A successful expedition could now be planned around a particular patch of lunar ground, rather than simply the safest broad area available. Precision would matter when later crews were sent to more demanding and scientifically interesting landscapes.

The journey had begun with a fright. Apollo 12 launched from Florida on 14 November into cloud and rain. Lightning caused two electrical disturbances during the first minute of flight, interrupting the spacecraft’s power systems and producing a bewildering collection of warnings. Controllers and crew recovered the systems, checked the spacecraft in Earth orbit and continued. NASA’s subsequent investigation found that the rocket and its exhaust could trigger lightning in an electrically charged cloud. The experience changed the rules used to judge suitable launch weather.

Once on the surface, Conrad and Bean had work to do. They deployed scientific equipment, examined the ground, collected samples and visited Surveyor 3. Bringing back pieces of the earlier spacecraft allowed engineers to study hardware that had spent more than two years exposed to the lunar environment. In that small meeting between a human expedition and an older robot, the Moon became something like a field laboratory: a place where earlier experiments could be revisited and their results carried home.

NASA’s landing tables record about 7.75 hours of surface excursions and 34.35 kilograms of returned samples. Those figures help restore the scale of the achievement. Two people, working in cumbersome suits in a place without breathable air, were doing geology, setting up instruments and documenting their surroundings. Meanwhile Gordon’s work in orbit was essential to their eventual reunion. The mission returned to Earth on 24 November. Its success depended on the crew as a team and on the much larger organisation supporting them.

Today is an anniversary rather than a public holiday, in Britain or elsewhere. NASA preserves the mission through technical records, photographs, histories and modern imagery of the landing site. Its Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter has photographed the area, allowing the old journey to be understood against a much sharper map. For a UK reader, exploring those records is a good way to mark the date. Apollo 12 rewards a little attention: beyond the famous first landing lies the equally absorbing story of people learning how to return, work carefully and bring back knowledge.
