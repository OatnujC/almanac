Major occasion · Friday 13 November 2026

Martyrdom of Fatimah al-Zahra (Iran)

Today is a public holiday in Iran, marking the death of Fatimah al-Zahra, the daughter of the Prophet Muhammad, the wife of Ali and the mother of Hasan and Husayn. For Shia Muslims she is one of the most revered figures in Islam, and the anniversary of her death is one of the most important days of mourning in the Shia year. In Iran’s official calendar it falls on 3 Jumada al-Thani 1448 in the Islamic lunar calendar, which this year matches Friday 13 November 2026, or 22 Aban 1405 in Iran’s solar calendar.

Why the date moves

The Islamic calendar is lunar, with twelve months of 29 or 30 days, so it is about eleven days shorter than the solar year. That means the anniversary drifts earlier through the Western calendar each year. In 2025 it fell on 24 November, and next year Iran expects it on 3 November. Because each Islamic month begins with the sighting of the new crescent moon, sources such as timeanddate.com list this year’s date as tentative, and Iranian calendars note that the observance can move by a day depending on the moon.

Two traditions, two seasons of mourning

Fatimah died in Medina in 632, only a few months after her father. Early Muslim history was largely remembered and passed on by word of mouth, and the sources disagree on exactly how long she outlived him. One well-known Shia narration says 75 days, another says 95. Part of the confusion may come from early Arabic script, which had no dots or vowel marks, so that the Arabic words for seventy and ninety could be written in the same way.

Rather than choose between the two accounts, Shia communities mourn on both, giving two seasons of remembrance: the first around 13 to 14 Jumada al-Awwal and the second around 3 to 5 Jumada al-Thani. Together they are known as the Fatimiyya. In Iran the second date, today, is the official public holiday. In recent years some communities have stretched each season into ten days of gatherings, much as the first ten days of Muharram are kept in memory of Husayn.

Why her death is mourned

Fatimah’s life was short and, in Shia tradition, deeply marked by suffering after her father’s death. Shia accounts describe a confrontation at her house during the dispute over who should lead the Muslim community after Muhammad, in which, they say, she was injured and lost the child she was carrying. Sunni historians tell the events of those months differently, and the episode remains one of the long-standing points of difference between the two traditions. In Shia memory, Fatimah stands for loyalty to Ali, courage in the face of injustice, and devotion to her family and faith.

Tradition also says that she asked Ali to bury her at night, privately, in the al-Baqi cemetery in Medina, and that the exact place of her grave was kept hidden. To this day it is not known for certain where she lies, which adds a particular poignancy to the mourning.

How it is marked today

In Iran, schools, offices and most businesses close for the day, and this year it lands on a Friday, which is already the weekend. Mosques and religious centres hold mourning gatherings with sermons recounting Fatimah’s life, elegies and recitations, and in many places there are processions and devotional theatre retelling the events of her last months.

The Fatimiyya is observed well beyond Iran, by Shia communities in Iraq, India, Pakistan, Azerbaijan, Tajikistan and Australia, and in Europe, including at the Islamic Centre of England in London. Many Muslims of all traditions, Sunni and Shia alike, honour Fatimah as an example of faith and family devotion, even where they do not keep this particular day.
