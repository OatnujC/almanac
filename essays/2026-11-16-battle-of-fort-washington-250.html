Minor occasion · 16 November 2026

Battle of Fort Washington, 250 years on

On the same day that cannon on St Eustatius were saluting the new American flag, things were going very badly for that flag in New York. Exactly 250 years ago today, on 16 November 1776, British and Hessian troops stormed Fort Washington, the last American stronghold on the island of Manhattan, and forced the surrender of almost its entire garrison. It was one of the worst defeats the Patriots suffered in the whole war.

The fort on the rock

Fort Washington stood on the highest ground on Manhattan, a ridge of Manhattan schist near the northern tip of the island, in what is now the Washington Heights neighbourhood. Together with Fort Lee, on the New Jersey Palisades across the Hudson, it was meant to stop British warships sailing up the river. Pennsylvania troops built it in the summer of 1776 as a five-bastioned earthwork, hauling soil up the hill because the rock was too bare for proper ditches, and sinking a barrier of boulder-filled hulks across the Hudson.

That autumn the campaign for New York went badly for George Washington. After defeats on Long Island and at White Plains, most of his army withdrew, but he left a garrison at the fort under Colonel Robert Magaw. Washington had doubts about holding it, but General Nathanael Greene argued it could and should be held, and Washington deferred to him. The garrison grew to nearly 3,000 men. Then, on 2 November, Magaw's adjutant, William Demont, deserted to the British with detailed plans of the defences.

The battle

General William Howe planned to attack from three sides while a fourth force made a feint. On 15 November he sent an officer under a flag of truce warning that if the fort did not surrender the garrison might be put to the sword; Magaw replied that he would defend it to the last extremity. Before dawn on the 16th the attack began. Hessian troops under Wilhelm von Knyphausen came from the north, Lord Percy from the south, and British troops crossed the Harlem River from the east, delayed for hours by the tide.

The southern and eastern defences fell fairly quickly. In the north, riflemen from Maryland and Virginia under Lieutenant Colonel Moses Rawlings held off the Hessians for a long time from behind rocks and fallen trees, until fouled weapons and sheer numbers wore them down. Washington, Greene and other generals had crossed to Manhattan that morning to see for themselves and were persuaded to leave as the British advanced. From Fort Lee, Washington sent a message asking Magaw to hold out until nightfall so the men might be evacuated, but by then the fort was surrounded. Magaw surrendered in the afternoon, and the American flag came down at about 4pm.

The cost was enormous. Wikipedia's account, drawing on the historian Richard Ketchum, gives 59 Americans killed, 96 wounded and 2,837 captured, against 84 killed and 374 wounded on the British and Hessian side. The prisoners suffered terribly; many were held in appalling conditions, including on prison ships, and only around 800 are said to have survived to be exchanged 18 months later. Three days after the battle the Americans abandoned Fort Lee, and Washington's army retreated across New Jersey into Pennsylvania. The revolution's fortunes only revived with his crossing of the Delaware on Christmas night and the victories at Trenton and Princeton.

Margaret Corbin

The battle is also remembered for one remarkable woman. Margaret Corbin had followed her husband John, an artilleryman, to the battlefield. When he was killed at his small cannon on a ridge now in Bennett Park, she took his place and kept firing until she was badly wounded in the arm, chest and jaw. She is regarded as the first known woman combatant of the American Revolution, and later became one of the first women to receive a soldier's pension from the US government. Fort Tryon Park has a Margaret Corbin Circle, and the park's conservancy has established an annual Margaret Corbin Day.

Remembering it at 250

Today the site of the fort lies in Bennett Park, just north of the George Washington Bridge, where stones mark the line of its walls and a tablet records its history. As a local news site, The Lighthouse, has noted, Washington Heights does not plaster its streets with Revolutionary signage, and many residents first learn about the battle through local events. For the 250th anniversary the Fort Tryon Park Conservancy held a free commemoration yesterday afternoon on the park's Café Lawn, with a battle re-enactment, 18th-century music, blacksmithing and appearances by George and Martha Washington. On the anniversary itself, this evening at 5:30pm New York time (10:30pm in the UK), the conservancy hosts an online talk with local historian Jane Gordon Julien about Margaret Corbin.

Seven years after the fall of the fort, on 25 November 1783, Washington rode back down through the area as the last British troops left New York. The defeat of 16 November 1776 had not been the end after all.
