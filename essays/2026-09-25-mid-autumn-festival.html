Major occasion · Friday 25 September 2026

The Mid-Autumn Festival — one moon, many homes

The Mid-Autumn Festival, sometimes called the Moon Festival or the Mooncake Festival, has a gentler centre of gravity than a night of fireworks or fright. Its central image is a family gathered beneath the moon, sharing something round and sweet. It is one of the most important festivals in Chinese culture, kept by Chinese communities around the world, including in Britain, and it has close relatives elsewhere in East and Southeast Asia. Harvest, gratitude and reunion meet in it. The sky supplies an image of completeness, while the people beneath it supply the inevitable awareness that not everyone can be home.

Why it fell on 25 September

In 2026 the festival fell on Friday 25 September. Its traditional date is the fifteenth day of the eighth month of the Chinese calendar. That calendar is lunisolar: months follow the moon, while extra “leap” months are added from time to time to keep the year in step with the seasons. That is why the festival moves between early September and early October in the Gregorian calendar, without wandering through every season. In the traditional reckoning, autumn ran through the seventh, eighth and ninth months, so the fifteenth of the eighth is the very middle of autumn. Hence the name: zhongqiu, “mid-autumn”.

The fifteenth of a lunar month is associated with a full moon, but the festival need not coincide with the precise astronomical full-moon instant. The Hong Kong Observatory pointed this out in its 2026 guidance: the festival was on 25 September, while the exact moment of full moon came later. That small difference makes the celebration no less lunar. A cultural calendar organises shared human time; astronomy measures a continuously moving celestial relationship. They are closely connected, not identical. Families do not have to postpone dinner until the moon reaches mathematical perfection.

Where it came from

The festival grew out of very old traditions of giving thanks for the autumn harvest and honouring the moon. Harvest celebrations at the autumn full moon are thought to go back to the Shang dynasty, more than three thousand years ago, and the term “mid-autumn” appears in the Rites of Zhou, a collection of ritual texts associated with the Western Zhou dynasty.

As a popular festival, though, it took shape much later. Moon-viewing became fashionable among scholars and officials during the Tang dynasty (618 to 907), when poets wrote endlessly about the autumn moon, and by the Song dynasty it was a festival people of many kinds enjoyed. One of the most famous Chinese poems of all, Su Shi’s Shuidiao Getou, was written at Mid-Autumn in 1076 when the poet was far from his younger brother. It ends with the wish that, though they are a thousand miles apart, they may live long and share the moon’s beauty together. That longing, a distant loved one looking at the same moon, is still at the emotional heart of the festival.

Chang’e and the moon

The festival’s best-known story concerns Chang’e. In one familiar version, ten suns once rose together and scorched the earth, until the great archer Hou Yi shot down nine of them. He was rewarded with an elixir of immortality. Chang’e drank it, whether to keep it from a thief, out of curiosity or for reasons the tellers dispute, and floated up to the moon, where she lives in a cold palace with a jade rabbit for company. Retellings differ about her motives and character, sometimes making her protective, sometimes more ambiguous. It is a legend, not a biography. Its emotional force lies in distance: a figure imagined above the earth, separated from the life and love she knew below.

The jade rabbit, pounding the elixir of life with a pestle, is another favourite, and people look for its shape in the shadows on the moon. China’s lunar exploration programme pays its own tribute: its probes are named Chang’e, and its rovers Yutu, the jade rabbit.

Mooncakes

Mooncakes give the festival an edible emblem. Their roundness suggests reunion and wholeness; sharing one, often cut into slices so everyone has a piece, turns that symbolism into a small domestic act. The best-known style abroad is Cantonese: a thin, glossy pastry around a dense filling of lotus-seed or bean paste, sometimes with one or more salted duck egg yolks in the centre to stand for the moon. There are many other styles. Suzhou mooncakes have flaky pastry and can be savoury, and Hong Kong popularised the chilled “snow skin” mooncake. Boxes of mooncakes are given as gifts to family, friends and business contacts in the weeks before the festival.

There is also a famous tale that rebels hid messages inside mooncakes to coordinate an uprising against Mongol rule near the end of the Yuan dynasty. It is an irresistible story, but it is a popular legend, not established proof that a pastry brought down a dynasty. The Yuan’s fall was a long and complicated process.

How it is celebrated

At its simplest the festival is a family dinner followed by moon-gazing. People eat mooncakes and seasonal fruit such as pomelos, drink tea or osmanthus wine, and look up at the sky when the weather allows. Children carry lanterns, from traditional paper rabbits and carp to cartoon characters and electric lights, and in some places lantern riddles are written on them to be solved. Some households set out offerings for the moon; others focus on visiting relatives.

The festival is a public holiday in mainland China and Taiwan. In Hong Kong and Macau the public holiday is the day after, because so much of the celebration happens late at night. Hong Kong is famous for its lantern carnivals, and in Tai Hang, near Causeway Bay, a fire dragon about 67 metres long, studded with burning incense sticks, winds through the streets for three nights around the festival, a tradition dating back to 1880.

Relatives across Asia

Several neighbouring cultures keep related but distinct festivals on the same lunar date. In Korea, Chuseok is a major three-day harvest holiday when families return to their home towns, honour their ancestors and eat songpyeon, half-moon-shaped rice cakes. In Vietnam, Tết Trung Thu is especially a children’s festival, with lantern processions, lion dances and stories of Cuội, the man in the moon who sits beneath a banyan tree. In Japan, the custom of tsukimi, or moon-viewing, involves offerings of rice dumplings and pampas grass. In Malaysia and Singapore the festival is popular, with lanterns and mooncakes everywhere, though it is not a public holiday.

In Britain

In Britain, Chinese families and community organisations carry the festival into another climate and another pattern of working life. Chinatowns in London, Manchester and elsewhere often hold lantern displays and performances, supermarkets stock mooncakes, and many families mark the day with a meal and a phone call home. A cloudy British sky may hide the moon, and relatives may be far away, but neither defeats the idea. As Su Shi knew nearly a thousand years ago, the festival offers a beautifully modest consolation: even when we cannot share a table, we can think of one another beneath the same moon.
