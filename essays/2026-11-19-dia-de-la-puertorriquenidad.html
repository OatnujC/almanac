Día de la Puertorriqueñidad (Discovery of Puerto Rico Day)

Thursday 19 November is a public holiday in Puerto Rico. For most of the last century it was known as the Día del Descubrimiento de Puerto Rico, Discovery of Puerto Rico Day, marking the day in 1493 when Christopher Columbus’s fleet reached the island. Since 2022 it has had a new official name, the Día de la Puertorriqueñidad, or Day of Puerto Ricanness, and the change says a lot about how the island now thinks about its own history.

A landing on the second voyage

Columbus set out on his second voyage to the Americas on 24 September 1493, with 17 ships and, by one count, more than 1,200 men. On 19 November 1493 the fleet reached an island that its Taíno inhabitants called Borikén, and anchored off the northwest coast. Columbus named it San Juan Bautista, after Saint John the Baptist.

For about 15 years after that, Spain largely left the island alone. Then in 1508 Juan Ponce de León, who had sailed with Columbus, was allowed to explore it. On the north coast he found a large bay that could shelter many ships, and nearby founded Caparra, the first European settlement. By 1521 the town had moved to the end of the harbour and taken the name Puerto Rico, “rich port”. Over time the names swapped places: the island became Puerto Rico, and the capital became San Juan. Puerto Rico has been a territory of the United States since 1898.

Where exactly did Columbus land?

Puerto Ricans have argued for generations about where the landing happened. The town of Aguada has long claimed it, and has a statue of Columbus in its main square. Neighbouring Aguadilla says the honour is its own. In 2022 a lawyer and researcher from Aguadilla, Israel Roldán González, presented a study at the Pontifical Catholic University of Puerto Rico arguing, with historical documents, maps, oceanography and archaeology, that Columbus came ashore at what is now Aguadilla. Part of his case rests on a court ruling about a stone house Ponce de León built in a district called Higüey, and the fact that the island’s only Higüey district is in Aguadilla.

There is even a debate about whether Columbus got there first. Puerto Rico’s newspaper El Nuevo Día notes that some historians believe Martín Alonso Pinzón, who captained La Pinta on Columbus’s first voyage, reached Borikén in late 1492 while Columbus was on Hispaniola.

From discovery to Puerto Ricanness

The holiday was created by Law 91 of 1938, which declared 19 November the Day of the Discovery of Puerto Rico. But, as El Nuevo Día puts it, whoever arrived first, calling it a “discovery” ignores the Indigenous societies who were already living on Borikén when the Europeans came. For that reason Law 117 of 2022 changed the holiday’s name to the Día de la Puertorriqueñidad.

The Taíno legacy is still woven into everyday Puerto Rican life, not least in words. El Nuevo Día’s classroom guide for this year’s holiday asks pupils to match Taíno words still in use: Borikén, the island’s original name; bohío, a round house; yuca, the root vegetable; cacique, a chief; hamaca, a hammock; canoa, a canoe; and areyto, a celebration with dance. Several of them, hammock and canoe among them, made their way into English too.

How the day is kept

19 November is an official holiday of the Commonwealth of Puerto Rico. This year it falls a week before Thanksgiving, which on the island marks the beginning of the Christmas holiday season. El Nuevo Día publishes classroom activities to help teachers use the day to explore the island’s history, and the newer name has given the day a broader purpose: not only to remember the arrival of the Spanish, but to celebrate everything, Taíno, African and Spanish, that has gone into being Puerto Rican.
