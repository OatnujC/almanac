Major occasion · 21 November 2026

The Presentation of Mary and Pro Orantibus Day

On 21 November, Christians in several traditions remember Mary’s dedication to God as a child. Catholics call the observance the Presentation of the Blessed Virgin Mary; Orthodox Christians know it as the Entry or Entrance of the Theotokos into the Temple. Theotokos means Mother of God. The names point towards the same devotional scene, though the feast has its own place and emphasis in each church. It offers a quieter kind of occasion for the almanac: a day concerned with readiness, prayer and a life understood as an offering.

The childhood story comes from Christian tradition and apocryphal writing, particularly the Protoevangelium of James, rather than the canonical Gospel accounts of Jesus’s life. In that tradition, Joachim and Anne bring the young Mary to the Temple in Jerusalem. This distinction is essential to a careful telling. The feast celebrates a theological understanding of Mary and her dedication; it does not establish an independently verified itinerary for her childhood. Respect for the religious meaning and clarity about the nature of the source belong together.

The Vatican connects the date with the dedication of the New Church of the Virgin Mary in Jerusalem, associated with the emperor Justinian. The calendar therefore preserves a church anniversary as well as a sacred narrative. Such layers are common in religious observances: a story, a place of worship and generations of prayer gradually become joined in one annual date. The Catholic liturgy draws attention to belonging to Jesus through doing the will of God, giving the day a meaning that extends beyond a childhood episode alone.

In Orthodox worship the feast is one of the great celebrations of the Mother of God. The Orthodox Church in America explains its central image as Mary entering the Temple in preparation to become a living temple herself. The Greek Orthodox Archdiocese describes the icon and the services through which the story is made present in church life. Hymns and images carry much of the teaching. Rather than simply illustrating a distant scene, they connect the entrance of a child with the Church’s understanding of the incarnation.

Calendar differences need care in Britain as elsewhere. Churches keeping this fixed feast on the civil date celebrate today. Communities following the Julian calendar for fixed feasts reach their 21 November on 4 December in the present calendar period. It is therefore misleading to say that every Orthodox parish is celebrating this morning. Anyone hoping to attend worship should consult the parish calendar. The feast also shares today’s date with Greece’s Armed Forces Day, whose official explanation invokes Mary’s protection, although that civic commemoration has its own purpose.

For Catholics, 21 November is also Pro Orantibus Day, established by Pius XII in 1953. It remembers women and men in contemplative religious communities and invites prayer for them. The Vatican describes their largely hidden lives as a contribution to the whole Church. This gives the date a very present human dimension alongside its ancient imagery. In the UK, one might mark it by learning about a contemplative community or joining the appropriate liturgy; these are possibilities, not a claim about a particular event. The shared thread is attention to lives of dedication whose work is not always publicly visible.
