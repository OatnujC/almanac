St Martin’s Day (Martinmas)

Today is St Martin's Day, or Martinmas, the feast of St Martin of Tours, one of the best-loved saints of medieval Europe. In Britain it is now barely remembered, but for centuries it was one of the great dates of the year, the moment the farming year ended and winter began. In Germany, the Netherlands and Belgium it is still very much alive, with children carrying paper lanterns through the dark streets and families sitting down to roast goose.

Who was Martin?

Martin was born in the early fourth century in Savaria, in what is now Szombathely in Hungary, and became a soldier in the Roman army. The story that made him famous happened at the gates of Amiens in northern France on a freezing winter's day. He met a beggar shivering with almost nothing on, took his sword, cut his military cloak in half and gave half to the man. That night, the legend goes, he dreamed that Christ appeared to him wearing the half cloak. Martin left the army, became a monk and founded one of the earliest monasteries in Gaul, and in 371 was made Bishop of Tours, very much against his will.

That reluctance is the origin of the goose. According to legend, Martin felt unworthy of becoming a bishop and hid in a goose pen, but the honking geese gave him away. Ever since, geese have paid the price on his feast day.

Why 11 November?

Martin died on 8 November 397, at the age of about 81, and was buried in Tours three days later, on 11 November. As the Diocese of Essen explains, that burial date became his feast day. He was one of the first saints to be honoured not because he was martyred but because of the way he lived. The Frankish kings made him their patron, his half of the cloak became a treasured relic carried into battle, and churches dedicated to him sprang up across France, the Low Countries and Germany. The word "chapel" itself comes from the little building, the capella, where his cape was kept.

The date also happened to fall at a natural turning point in the farming year. The harvest was in, and animals that could not be fed through the winter were slaughtered and salted, which is why Martinmas was a time for feasting on fresh meat. New wine was ready to taste. Rents and dues were often paid then, sometimes in poultry, and in many places farmhands and servants finished their year's contract on this day and looked for new work at hiring fairs. In the medieval church, St Martin's Day also opened a forty-day fast before Christmas, so the feast had a last-chance, carnival flavour about it.

How it is marked today

In Germany the lantern procession, the Martinszug or Laternelaufen, is the highlight. Young children make lanterns at nursery or school and walk through the streets at dusk singing songs such as "Ich geh' mit meiner Laterne" ("I walk with my lantern"), often led by a rider on a horse dressed as Martin in a red cloak. Sometimes the cloak-sharing scene is acted out, and many processions end with a bonfire. Children are given sweet bread shaped like a little man with a clay pipe, called a Weckmann, or small pretzels or geese. In 2026 the Rhineland's St Martin's traditions were added to Germany's national register of intangible cultural heritage.

In the Netherlands and Flanders, children go from door to door with lanterns on the evening of 11 November singing St Martin's songs and collecting sweets, a little like Halloween. In Portugal, São Martinho is a time for roasted chestnuts and new wine around a fire, and there is often a spell of mild weather around this time known as St Martin's summer. In Poznań in Poland, people eat a huge crescent pastry filled with white poppy seed, the rogal świętomarciński. And in German restaurants, Martinsgans, roast goose with red cabbage and dumplings, is on menus all week.

In Britain, traces survive in old words. "Martlemas beef" was meat salted for the winter, and in Scotland Martinmas was long one of the legal term days on which rents fell due and leases began or ended. And there is a pleasing coincidence: the saint of the soldier who laid down his sword shares his day with the anniversary of the Armistice.
