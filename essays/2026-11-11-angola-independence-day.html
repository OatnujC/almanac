Minor occasion · Wednesday 11 November 2026

Angola Independence Day

Today is Independence Day in Angola, the country's main national holiday. On 11 November 1975, in the capital Luanda, Agostinho Neto, leader of the MPLA liberation movement, proclaimed Angola's independence from Portugal, ending centuries of Portuguese colonial rule. This year marks the 51st anniversary.

Five centuries of Portuguese rule

Portuguese navigators reached the mouth of the Congo River in the 1480s, and Portugal established the settlement of Luanda in 1576. For centuries Angola was a centre of the Atlantic slave trade, with huge numbers of people shipped across the ocean, especially to Brazil. After slavery ended, Portugal extended its control into the interior, and in the twentieth century, under the dictatorship of António Salazar, it insisted that its African colonies were overseas provinces of Portugal itself, and refused to give them up even as Britain and France dismantled their empires.

The war for independence

Armed struggle began in 1961. Three main movements fought the Portuguese, and often each other. The US State Department's history summarises them: the MPLA, a Marxist movement based in Luanda and led by Agostinho Neto, a doctor and poet; the FNLA, led by Holden Roberto and based in the north, with close ties to Zaire's Mobutu Sese Seko; and UNITA, an offshoot of the FNLA led by Jonas Savimbi and supported mainly by the Ovimbundu, Angola's largest ethnic group.

The breakthrough came not in Angola but in Lisbon. On 25 April 1974, a military coup, the Carnation Revolution, toppled Portugal's authoritarian regime, and the new government wanted to be rid of its costly colonial wars. In January 1975 the three movements met Portuguese representatives and signed the Alvor Agreement, which granted Angola independence and set up a three-way power-sharing government.

Why 11 November

The power-sharing deal collapsed almost at once, and fighting broke out among the three movements, each backed by outside powers in the Cold War. By the summer of 1975 the Soviet-supported MPLA held Luanda. When Neto announced 11 November 1975 as the day of independence, Portugal decided to withdraw its troops on that day. Cuban forces arrived to back the MPLA, while South African troops pushed north in support of UNITA. In the event, Neto proclaimed the People's Republic of Angola in Luanda, while the FNLA and UNITA declared a rival state of their own. Angola was born free but at war.

A long road to peace

The civil war that followed lasted, on and off, for more than a quarter of a century, becoming one of the bloodiest Cold War proxy conflicts in Africa. It only ended in 2002, after Savimbi was killed. Since then Angola has rebuilt, helped by its oil and diamond wealth, though poverty and inequality remain serious problems.

How it is marked today

Independence Day is a public holiday, celebrated with official ceremonies in Luanda, speeches, the raising of the flag, parades, concerts and cultural shows. The state news agency publishes chronologies and reflections on the events of 1975, and families gather for meals, music and dancing, including Angola's own semba and kizomba. Neto, who died in 1979, is remembered as the founding father of the nation; his birthday, 17 September, is a separate holiday, National Heroes' Day.
