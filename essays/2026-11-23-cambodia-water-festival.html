Major occasion · 23 November 2026

Bon Om Touk: Cambodia’s Water Festival begins

Cambodia's Water Festival, Bon Om Touk, brings water, the moon and the turning of the seasons into the same celebration. Its three-day public holiday begins on Monday 23 November in 2026 and continues through Wednesday 25 November. The Royal Embassy of Cambodia in London confirms those dates. There is an important qualification this year: the main celebrations beside the Royal Palace in Phnom Penh have been suspended. The holiday itself remains in place, and provincial celebrations may go ahead according to local circumstances.

The National Museum of Cambodia explains the festival through the changing flow of the Tonle Sap River and the end of the rainy season. This river connects the great lake with the Mekong. During the wet season the Mekong can push water back towards the lake; later, as river levels fall, the water drains out again. The Mekong River Commission treats that reverse flow as a vital part of the basin's seasonal system. A festival date celebrates this annual rhythm; it does not mean the whole river changes direction at a fixed hour on the first holiday morning.

The calendar is lunar, so the Gregorian dates move from year to year. That is why last year's dates cannot safely be carried into a new almanac. The 2026 holiday is specifically listed as 23–25 November by Cambodian institutions, including the London embassy. The festival's association with the full-moon season also gives the water celebrations a wider setting of night-time light and gratitude. For this entry, those confirmed holiday dates are the anchor, rather than an assumption based on the astronomical full moon alone.

In a usual Phnom Penh festival, the riverfront becomes a place to watch long racing boats and the work of their crews. The National Museum describes illuminated floating boats, fireworks and concerts as part of the familiar celebrations. These are descriptions of the festival's customary form, not promises of events taking place in the capital today. Photographs from a previous crowded riverfront can be beautiful records, but they would give a misleading impression if presented as a guide to this year's programme.

The government news agency AKP reports that the suspension of the main capital festivities is intended to prioritise humanitarian assistance and other urgent national needs. Its account specifically mentions support for displaced people and border-related concerns. It also says that the three-day break for civil servants, employees and workers is unchanged. The distinction is practical as well as cultural: a family may still have a holiday even when the best-known public spectacle is absent. Provincial authorities and communities retain room to make their own arrangements within their capacity.

The festival's relationship with the river makes it more than a convenient long weekend. It asks people to pay attention to the natural system that supports settlement, farming and fishing. The seasonal movement of water is something a community lives with, rather than simply scenery behind a boat race. Watching a crew pull together also offers a visible image of shared effort. These are ways of understanding the celebration, without needing to turn a traditional account of its origins into a single, certain founding story.

In Britain, today's date is not a public holiday for Bon Om Touk. The Cambodian embassy's calendar nevertheless gives a direct London connection to the occasion. Readers here can mark it by learning the geography of the Tonle Sap, exploring Cambodian cultural accounts or keeping in touch with family who observe the holiday. In 2026 the most faithful description makes room for both continuity and restraint: the Water Festival season has arrived, the public holiday begins, and the familiar main Phnom Penh festivities are not taking place.
