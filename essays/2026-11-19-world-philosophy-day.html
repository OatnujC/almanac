Major occasion · 19 November 2026

World Philosophy Day

Once a year UNESCO, the United Nations’ agency for education, science and culture, sets aside a day to celebrate the oldest habit of the human mind: asking awkward questions and trying to answer them carefully. World Philosophy Day falls on the third Thursday of November, which in 2026 is 19 November. By marking it, UNESCO says, it “underlines the enduring value of philosophy for the development of human thought, for each culture and for each individual.”

Why a UN agency cares about philosophy

UNESCO has always seen philosophy as close to its core mission. Its constitution famously speaks of building “the defences of peace in the minds of men and women”, and philosophy, in UNESCO’s telling, is one of the best tools for doing that. The organisation describes it as both an inspiring discipline and an everyday practice that can transform societies: by introducing people to the variety of the world’s intellectual traditions it encourages dialogue between cultures, and by training people to think for themselves and weigh opposing opinions with reasons, it helps build a more tolerant and respectful society. UNESCO is careful to say that the philosophy it has in mind is not abstract speculation for its own sake but critical questioning that gives meaning to life and action.

From 2002 to a fixed Thursday

The day was first celebrated on 21 November 2002. In establishing it, UNESCO’s General Conference stressed the importance of philosophy especially for young people, calling it “a discipline that encourages critical and independent thought and is capable of working towards a better understanding of the world and promoting tolerance and peace.” It added that turning Philosophy Day into an official “World Philosophy Day” would give strong impetus to philosophy and, in particular, to its teaching around the world. That formal step came at the 33rd session of the General Conference in Paris in October 2005, which proclaimed that World Philosophy Day would be celebrated every year on the third Thursday of November. Because the date moves with the calendar, it can fall anywhere from 15 to 21 November; last year it was 20 November, and next year it will be 18 November.

Over the years UNESCO has welcomed leading thinkers to mark the occasion, among them the anthropologist Claude Lévi-Strauss, the economist and philosopher Amartya Sen and the French thinker Edgar Morin. The day is not only a Paris affair: universities, schools, philosophy cafés and cultural bodies around the world hold debates, lectures and workshops of their own.

The 2026 lecture: Living the Earth Together

This year’s guest is Souleymane Bachir Diagne, one of the most celebrated philosophers working today. Born in Saint-Louis, Senegal, in 1955, he studied in Paris at the Lycée Louis-le-Grand and the École Normale Supérieure, where he was taught by Louis Althusser and Jacques Derrida, and he wrote his doctorate on George Boole’s algebra of logic. He taught philosophy at Cheikh Anta Diop University in Dakar, advised Senegal’s President Abdou Diouf on education and culture in the 1990s, and later taught at Northwestern University and Columbia University in New York, where he is now professor emeritus. His work ranges across the history of logic and mathematics, philosophy in the Islamic world, identity, and African literatures and philosophies.

His lecture at UNESCO’s Paris headquarters on the evening of 19 November is called “Living the Earth Together”. UNESCO says it will explore the idea of the universal human, a concept, as the programme puts it, that is increasingly questioned today. According to the programme as reported by Evolving Humanities, Diagne will set the idea of a shared humanity against three recent ways of thinking about our place on the planet: the historian Dipesh Chakrabarty’s “planetary consciousness”, the philosopher Achille Mbembe’s “earthly community”, and the older notion of “planetization” from the Jesuit thinker Pierre Teilhard de Chardin. UNESCO frames the choice pointedly: at a time when ethnonationalist visions are gaining ground, it says, Diagne’s defence of universalism as the common work of humanity sits close to UNESCO’s own mission of building peace through dialogue.

Students at the centre

The 2026 edition puts young people firmly in the room. Students from three of UNESCO’s partner universities, the Université Paris-Est Créteil, the University of Caen Normandy and Université Côte d’Azur, spend the day in nine interactive workshops on four big themes: climate, artificial intelligence, migration and health. Topics include citizens’ assemblies, simulated trials of AI, futures literacy, narrative medicine and the mapping of vocabularies of peace. In the evening plenary, after Diagne’s lecture, there is a disputatio, a structured debate in the medieval tradition in which two opposing positions are tested by argument, on the question “Can Philosophy Save the World?”, followed by a round table on thinking and acting together in a world of many challenges, with one student from each university taking part. The day’s work is meant to produce a public Citizen Manifesto of student-led proposals, which UNESCO plans to publish on its website as a resource for policymakers in its member states.

UNESCO’s framing for the year is direct. In a world shaped by disinformation and the erosion of democratic values, it says, philosophy is one of its most vital tools for building resilience, an ethical compass to help break down silos, restore social bonds and rethink hospitality. For anyone not in Paris, the simplest way to mark the day is the oldest: pick a question that matters, and think it through with someone who disagrees.
