Minor occasion · 20 November 2026

Edwin Hubble’s birthday

Edwin Powell Hubble was born on 20 November 1889 in Marshfield, Missouri. Today is the 137th anniversary of the astronomer whose name now belongs to a space telescope familiar far beyond professional science. The birthday offers a reason to look back at the person behind that name. His observations helped establish that the Milky Way is one galaxy among many and that the universe is expanding. Those discoveries changed the scale on which people could understand their place in the cosmos.

Hubble’s route into astronomy was not entirely direct. He studied at the University of Chicago and then at Oxford as a Rhodes Scholar, spending time on law before returning to scientific work. That gives the anniversary a small British connection as well as an American one. His decisive astronomical setting was Mount Wilson Observatory in California, where the 100-inch Hooker telescope offered extraordinary observing power for its time. The equipment mattered, but so did the careful interpretation of faint marks and changing points of light recorded through it.

One of the central questions concerned the nature of objects then commonly described as spiral nebulae. Were they part of the Milky Way, or separate stellar systems far beyond it? In photographs of Andromeda, Hubble identified Cepheid variable stars. These brighten and fade in a regular way that can be used to estimate distance. The resulting measurement placed Andromeda outside the Milky Way. A faint patch of light became evidence for an enormous change in the understood size and population of the universe.

That work depended on an earlier discovery by Henrietta Swan Leavitt. She established the relationship between a Cepheid’s variation period and its intrinsic brightness. Comparing an estimate of true brightness with how bright an object appears provides a way to gauge its distance. NASA’s accounts explicitly connect Hubble’s result with Leavitt’s work. Remembering that connection improves the birthday story: science advances through linked observations and methods, even when public memory settles on a single famous surname or the instrument later named after it.

Hubble’s 1929 work also connected galaxy distances with their recession speeds, providing important observational support for an expanding universe. The relation is a foundation of modern cosmology, though the history includes many contributors and the measurements have been refined greatly since then. NASA’s histories describe the use of Cepheids as part of a continuing cosmic distance ladder. The interesting achievement was not simply seeing something beautiful through a large telescope. It was turning observations into measurements that could test a much larger claim about how the universe behaves.

Today the birthday is a historical anniversary rather than a public holiday. In Britain and elsewhere, exploring NASA’s images and explanations is an accessible way to mark it, without needing a telescope or a clear sky. A particularly satisfying starting point is the story of the variable star in Andromeda and the photographic evidence behind it. Looking at a modern galaxy image afterwards can feel different when its distance has a history. Hubble’s name then becomes more than a label on a spectacular picture: it recalls the patient work that helped turn a distant blur into another world of stars.
