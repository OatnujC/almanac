Minor occasion · 15 November 2026

Proclamation of the Republic (Brazil)

Today Brazil has a national public holiday, the Proclamação da República, marking the day in 1889 when the country stopped being an empire and became a republic. It is 137 years since a column of soldiers moved through the streets of Rio de Janeiro, the capital at the time, and brought down a monarchy that had ruled Brazil since independence in 1822, almost without a shot being fired.

An empire losing its supports

Brazil in the 1880s was a constitutional monarchy under Emperor Pedro II, a scholarly and widely respected ruler who had reigned for nearly half a century. But the institution he headed was steadily losing its friends. After the Paraguayan War of 1864 to 1870, many army officers felt undervalued and resented being commanded by civilian politicians; they were also influenced by the positivist ideas of the French philosopher Auguste Comte, which spread through the military schools and favoured a strong, modernising republic. A Republican Manifesto was published in 1870, and republican clubs multiplied in the following years.

The Church had clashed with the state in the 1870s over the imprisonment of two bishops. And the succession worried many people: Pedro II had no surviving sons, and his heir was his daughter Isabel, married to a Frenchman, Gaston, Count of Eu, and some feared the country would effectively be ruled by a foreigner.

The final blow came from slavery. In May 1888 Princess Isabel, acting as regent, signed the Golden Law (Lei Áurea), which abolished slavery outright, making Brazil the last country in the Americas to do so. The landowners who had held enslaved people were not compensated, and many turned against the crown, earning the nickname "Republicans of 13 May". As one senator who opposed abolition reportedly put it, Isabel had "redeemed a race but lost the throne".

15 November 1889

A coup had been planned for 20 November, but rumours spread on the 14th that the government was about to arrest leading figures, and the conspirators moved early. In the small hours of 15 November they persuaded Marshal Deodoro da Fonseca, an ailing and by most accounts monarchist old soldier, to lead the troops. His target at first seems to have been the prime minister, the Viscount of Ouro Preto, rather than the emperor; historians still debate exactly what Deodoro shouted that morning. When the government asked General Floriano Peixoto to use loyal troops against the rebels, he refused, reportedly saying that in Paraguay they had faced enemies, but here they were all Brazilians. The cabinet was deposed, and the only person wounded was the navy minister, the Baron of Ladário, who was shot while resisting arrest.

That afternoon the republic was proclaimed at Rio's city hall, and a provisional government was formed with Deodoro as its head. Pedro II, who had come down from his summer residence in Petrópolis, chose not to resist. Within days he and his family were sent into exile in Europe; he died in Paris two years later. The family was allowed to return to Brazil only in 1920.

A republic and its symbols

The new regime was not uncontested. There were monarchist revolts in the following months, naval revolts in the early 1890s, and the bloody Federalist Revolution of 1893 to 1895. Deodoro became Brazil's first president, but governed in an authoritarian way and resigned in 1891, handing power to Floriano Peixoto. The early republic's positivist inspiration can still be seen on Brazil's flag, adopted just days after the coup, with its starry blue globe and the motto "Ordem e Progresso", "Order and Progress", taken from Comte.

The question of monarchy did not entirely go away: in a referendum in 1993 Brazilians were asked whether they wanted a republic or a monarchy, and voted decisively to keep the republic.

How the day is marked

Republic Day is a quieter holiday than Independence Day on 7 September. There are official ceremonies and military parades in some places, and Brasília and state capitals hold flag-raising events, but for many Brazilians it is simply a welcome day off, and, falling on a Sunday this year, an ordinary weekend. Its partner date comes on 19 November, Brazil's Flag Day, which remembers the adoption of that green, gold and blue flag in the first week of the new republic.
