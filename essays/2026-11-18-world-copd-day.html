Minor occasion · 18 November 2026

World COPD Day

Today is World COPD Day, the 25th since the first was held in 2002, and it falls, as it always does, on the third Wednesday of November. The day is organised by the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease, known as GOLD, together with doctors, nurses and patient groups around the world. This year’s theme is “Early Diagnosis and Management for a Better Life.”

A common disease that is still missed

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease is a long-term lung condition in which the airways become narrowed and damaged, making it hard to breathe. It is often talked about as emphysema or chronic bronchitis, which describe two of the ways it can appear. The usual symptoms are breathlessness, a cough that may bring up phlegm, wheezing and tiredness. The illness develops slowly, often from mid-life onwards, and it can worsen suddenly in flare-ups that last a few days and may need extra treatment. There is no cure, but symptoms can be improved, and the course of the disease slowed, by stopping smoking, avoiding polluted air, taking inhaled medicines, having vaccinations against infections, and, for some people, oxygen therapy or pulmonary rehabilitation.

The scale of it is easy to underestimate if you only meet the disease in a family or a clinic. The World Health Organization, in a fact sheet updated in June 2026, calls COPD the third leading cause of death worldwide. It caused 3.4 million deaths in 2023, about 6 per cent of all deaths on the planet. It is also the seventh leading cause of poor health, measured in years of healthy life lost. Nearly 90 per cent of COPD deaths among people under 70 are in low- and middle-income countries. In high-income countries, tobacco smoking accounts for more than 70 per cent of cases. In poorer countries that share falls to 30 to 40 per cent, and smoke from cooking and heating with wood, dung, crop waste or coal is a major cause. Other risks include dusty or chemical work, frequent or severe lung infections in childhood, and a rare inherited condition, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, which can bring on COPD at a young age.

Why the 2026 theme is early diagnosis

GOLD’s point this year is that COPD is common, preventable and treatable, and yet it is widely under-diagnosed and often misdiagnosed, so people start treatment later than they should. The disease can begin earlier in life than many people think, and it is not only a smoker’s illness. There are also precursor states, sometimes called pre-COPD or PRISm, that offer a chance to act before the lungs are badly damaged. The standard test is spirometry, a simple breathing test that measures how well the lungs are working. In many countries it is still hard to get, and in many clinics it is not used routinely even when it is available. GOLD argues that earlier treatment, including help to stop smoking and the right inhalers, can preserve lung function, cut flare-ups and improve daily life.

How the day is marked

Since 2002, organisers in more than 50 countries have held World COPD Day events: public lung-function testing, talks, clinics, and campaigns aimed at GPs as well as at patients and families. This year a 24-hour online relay called the Big Baton Pass ran on 18 November, with a virtual baton handed from region to region so that people living with COPD, their families, clinicians and researchers could speak in turn. Six hours of the Americas’ stretch were hosted from the pulmonary rehabilitation programme at Temple University Hospital in Philadelphia. In a more visible gesture, Niagara Falls is lit orange for a quarter of an hour this evening, as other landmarks have been on previous World COPD Days.

Twenty-five years is a long time to be repeating a message, and GOLD itself has said that awareness has to turn into year-round action: more spirometry, cleaner air, less smoking, and treatment that starts before the disease has already taken a great deal of someone’s breath. The day is a reminder that a condition this common should not still be so often missed.
