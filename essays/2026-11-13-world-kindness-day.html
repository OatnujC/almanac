World Kindness Day

World Kindness Day falls on 13 November every year. It is a simple idea: a day to notice, encourage and do good deeds, large and small, and to remember the common thread of kindness that ties people together whatever their country, culture or religion. It is not an official United Nations day. It was created by a grassroots coalition of national kindness organisations, the World Kindness Movement, and has spread mainly through schools, councils, charities and community groups.

Why 13 November?

The date has a precise origin. The World Kindness Movement grew out of Japan. Its Small Kindness Movement had been encouraging everyday courtesy and consideration since the early 1960s, and in the 1990s it began inviting people from other countries who had started similar groups to conferences in Tokyo. At the 1997 meeting, on 20 September, seven countries signed a declaration to establish the World Kindness Movement.

The movement’s first full conference opened in Tokyo on 13 November 1998, and the delegates agreed there that World Kindness Day would be held on that date every year. The day also marked the 35th anniversary of Japan’s Small Kindness Movement, the group that had first brought them together. So every 13 November quietly honours both that first meeting and the Japanese organisation whose idea it was.

A movement grows

The World Kindness Movement was formally launched in Singapore on 18 November 2000, at its third conference, with members from Australia, Canada, Japan, South Korea, Scotland, Thailand, the United States, Italy and several Southeast Asian nations. Its stated mission is to inspire individuals towards greater kindness and to connect nations to create a kinder world, and it encourages people everywhere to set up their own national kindness movements.

In the years since, the movement has held general assemblies and council meetings around the world, from Parma and Sydney to London, Hong Kong, Kuala Lumpur and Seoul. National branches have been launched in places such as China, France and the United States, and some towns, including the Gold Coast in Australia and Salisbury in Maryland, have been named World Kindness Cities. In 2019 the movement registered as a non-governmental organisation under Swiss law.

Australia has been one of the day’s keenest adopters. In 2010 the New South Wales parents’ federation asked for World Kindness Day to go on the state school calendar, and in 2012 it was placed on Australia’s national school calendar, reaching more than 9,000 schools. Singapore first marked the day in 2009 by giving away 45,000 yellow flowers. In the United Kingdom the day is championed by Kindness Day UK, co-founded by David Jamilly and Louise Burfitt-Dons.

How it is marked

There is no single ritual. Schools run kindness challenges and assemblies, workplaces encourage staff to volunteer or write thank-you notes, and councils sign declarations of support. Over the years people have held mass hugs, handed out kindness cards that ask the recipient to pass on a good deed, staged a global flash mob across 15 countries, and put on concerts in Canada. In Slovenia a volunteering group has run a project of random acts of kindness on the day.

The spirit of the day is deliberately modest. The movement describes kindness as a fundamental part of being human that bridges the divides of race, religion, politics, gender and place, and it asks people to look beyond themselves and their own borders and to see one another as citizens of the same world. In recent years the idea has also found allies in education: UNESCO’s Mahatma Gandhi Institute of Education for Peace has worked with the movement on a campaign arguing that kindness matters to the world’s development goals, and the movement added 20,000 kindness stories to the institute’s online storyboard.

A fitting coincidence

This year World Kindness Day shares its date with BBC Children in Need, the UK’s biggest night of charity television, which feels like a happy accident. If you want to mark it, the suggestions are refreshingly ordinary: a message to someone you have been meaning to thank, a coffee bought for a stranger, an hour given to a neighbour, a little patience on a busy commute. The point is not grand gestures, but noticing other people and doing something to make their day a bit better.
