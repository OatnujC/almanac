Minor occasion · 18 November 2026

Mickey Mouse’s Birthday

Today is Mickey Mouse’s official birthday, and Minnie’s too. On 18 November 1928 the cartoon Steamboat Willie opened at New York’s Colony Theatre, on a bill with the now-forgotten feature Gang War, and a whistling mouse at the wheel of a steamboat became a star. This year he turns 98.

Not quite the first appearance

Mickey had already been on a screen. After Walt Disney lost the rights to his earlier character, Oswald the Lucky Rabbit, he and the animator Ub Iwerks created a new mouse. The first short, Plane Crazy, was previewed in the spring of 1928, and a second, The Gallopin’ Gaucho, followed, but neither found a distributor. Both were silent. What changed everything was sound.

Steamboat Willie was not the first cartoon with a soundtrack, but it was planned as a sound film from the start, rather than having music and effects tacked on afterwards. The gags depend on rhythm: pipes, whistles, a goat, a crate of pots and pans, and Mickey’s performance of the tune “Turkey in the Straw” on a menagerie of improvised instruments. Iwerks designed and animated the film under Disney’s direction. The result, as the Walt Disney Family Museum has put it, was a fresh, original cartoon whose star’s personality, cheerful, clever and resourceful, was unmistakable from the first showing.

A debut that reviewers noticed

On the night of 18 November, audiences at the Colony responded at once. Mordaunt Hall of the New York Times called it “an ingenious piece of work with a good deal of fun.” Variety called it “a wow” and wrote that “giggles came so fast at the Colony they were stumbling over each other.” Most American cities would not see the film until 1929, but the character’s career had begun. Within two years there were Mickey Mouse storybooks, dolls and toys, a daily comic strip, and a spreading chain of Mickey Mouse Clubs that had started as a local cinema stunt in California. Rival studios began turning out mice that looked more than a little like him. Minnie was there from the beginning, in Steamboat Willie and in the two silent shorts that preceded it, which is why 18 November is kept as her birthday as well.

A birthday that wandered, then settled

The date was not always treated as official. In the 1930s, Disney’s then distributor, United Artists, promoted a “Mickey Mouse Birthday Week” around 28 September, and the studio went along with it as a way to book more cartoons into cinemas. The short The Birthday Party, in which Minnie and the gang throw Mickey a surprise party, was put into production around the time of his second anniversary but was not released until January 1931, which only added to the confusion. In the end the premiere date won. Historians and the Disney company now treat 18 November 1928 as the birthday, because that was the day the public first met the Mickey who would last.

A mouse in the public domain, and still everywhere

On 1 January 2024 the original Steamboat Willie version of Mickey, and the film itself, entered the public domain in the United States, 95 years after publication. Later designs and later films remain protected, and the name and the modern character are still tightly held as trademarks, but the whistling steamboat captain of 1928 can now be used by anyone in that first form. It was a reminder of how long he has been around, and of how completely a seven-minute cartoon remade popular animation.

People mark the day in the informal way a cultural birthday gets marked: by watching the film, which is easy to find, by visiting Disney archives and museums, and by noting, if they are so minded, that a character created after a business setback, drawn largely by one extraordinary animator, and built around a few synchronised sound gags, is still recognised almost anywhere in the world. Ninety-eight is a good age for a mouse who never much cared for acting his age.
