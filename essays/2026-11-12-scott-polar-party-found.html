Scott’s polar party found (1912)

On 12 November 1912, in the middle of the Antarctic ice shelf, a party of men from Captain Robert Falcon Scott’s expedition spotted something odd: a mound of snow that looked like a cairn, with a few feet of bamboo sticking out beside it. Someone brushed the snow away and the green flap of a tent ventilator appeared. Inside lay Scott and two of his companions, who had died there more than seven months earlier on their way back from the South Pole. It is one of the most famous and saddest days in the history of exploration.

The race for the Pole

Scott’s British Antarctic Expedition, usually known as the Terra Nova expedition after its ship, set out in 1910 with a serious scientific programme and one headline ambition: to be first to the South Pole. A five-man party, Scott, the doctor and naturalist Edward Wilson, Lieutenant Henry Bowers, Captain Lawrence Oates and Petty Officer Edgar Evans, made the final push on foot, hauling their own sledges. They reached the Pole on 17 January 1912, only to find that the Norwegian Roald Amundsen had beaten them there, arriving on 14 December 1911.

The return journey became a slow disaster. Evans died at the foot of the Beardmore Glacier in February. Oates, badly frostbitten and knowing he was slowing the others, walked out of the tent into a blizzard in March, and was never seen again. Scott, Wilson and Bowers struggled on until, around 21 March, they were pinned in their tent by a blizzard about 11 miles short of One Ton Depot, a large store of food and fuel. They never moved again. Scott’s last diary entry is dated 29 March 1912.

The search

Back at the expedition’s base at Cape Evans, the surviving men had to wait out the long, dark Antarctic winter knowing that the polar party must be dead, but not where or how. When spring came, the surgeon Edward Atkinson, who had taken charge, led a search party south with mules and dogs, following the route the polar party should have taken home.

Apsley Cherry-Garrard, one of the youngest members of the expedition, kept a diary on the search, which he later published in his classic book The Worst Journey in the World. His entry for 12 November begins, almost unbearably, with ordinary worries about a struggling mule and the cold. Then, nearly midday, he writes: We have found them, to say it has been a ghastly day cannot express it. It was the physicist Charles Wright who turned off course, having seen something that looked like a cairn with something dark beside it. As Thomas Williamson, another member of the party, recorded in words quoted by the Historical Association: I must own I shed a few tears.

Scott lay in the middle, with Wilson on one side and Bowers on the other. Wilson and Bowers seemed to have died quietly, as if in sleep. Around them were the records of the journey: diaries, letters, rolls of film, a weather log, and about thirty pounds of geological specimens they had dragged all the way from the Beardmore Glacier. Cherry-Garrard was moved that men in such a state had gone on pulling everything they had died to gain.

A grave which kings must envy

Atkinson gathered the men and read them the passage from Scott’s diary describing Oates’s death, because Scott had asked that it be known. He then read the burial service. The search party did not move the bodies. They left them in their sleeping bags, collapsed the tent over them and built a great cairn of snow above it, topped with a cross made from skis. Cherry-Garrard wrote that perhaps the burial lesson had never been read in a more magnificent cathedral, for it is a grave which kings must envy. The next day they went on south to look for Oates; his body was never found, and they built a cairn near the place where he died.

How the world found out

Because there was no way to send news from Antarctica, the world learned of the tragedy only when the Terra Nova reached New Zealand in February 1913. The reaction in Britain was extraordinary, and Scott’s diaries and letters, especially his closing message to the public, turned a failure into a national legend of courage and sacrifice. Before leaving Antarctica, the expedition raised a wooden cross on Observation Hill above their old hut, carved with the line from Tennyson’s poem Ulysses: To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield.

The anniversary is not a public occasion, but polar historians, museums and the Scott Polar Research Institute in Cambridge, founded as a memorial to the polar party, often recall it. Over the past century Scott’s reputation has swung between hero and bungler and back again. What has never changed is the power of that quiet scene on the ice, and of the men who walked out into the cold to bring their friends’ story home.
