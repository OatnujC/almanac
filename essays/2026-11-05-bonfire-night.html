Major occasion · Thursday 5 November 2026

Bonfire Night — when history became a night out

Bonfire Night has an unusually sharp historical starting point for something now associated with sparklers and chilly fingers. It remembers a particular conspiracy, against a particular king and Parliament, discovered on a particular night. Yet for many people in Britain its strongest associations are sensory rather than political: smoke in a coat, the heat of a fire against a cold face, toffee apples, and a crowd looking upward together while fireworks briefly light the sky. The distance between those two meanings is part of its story.

Why 5 November

The date is fixed at 5 November, which fell on a Thursday in 2026. Displays often take place on nearby evenings or weekends, but that practical flexibility does not move the anniversary itself. Unlike a harvest or moon festival, this is not a date worked out from the seasons or the heavens. It commemorates the planned destruction of the House of Lords at the State Opening of Parliament on Tuesday 5 November 1605, when King James I, members of his family and the country’s political, legal and religious leaders would have been assembled in one room.

England’s religious divisions

The Gunpowder Plot grew from the bitter religious divisions that followed the Reformation. Henry VIII had broken with Rome in the 1530s, and under Elizabeth I Catholics faced fines for not attending Church of England services, and priests could be executed. When James VI of Scotland became James I of England in 1603, many Catholics hoped he would be more tolerant; his mother, Mary, Queen of Scots, had been a Catholic. Those hopes were soon disappointed. A small group of Catholic gentlemen, led by the charismatic Robert Catesby, decided that only violence would change things. Their plan was to kill the King and much of the Protestant establishment in one blast, then raise a revolt in the Midlands and place James’s nine-year-old daughter, Princess Elizabeth, on the throne as a Catholic monarch.

The plot

The conspirators, who eventually numbered thirteen, met for the first time in May 1604. Among them was Guy Fawkes, a Yorkshireman, born in York in 1570 and raised a Protestant, who had converted to Catholicism and spent years fighting for Spain in the Low Countries, where he had learned about explosives. Using a contact who had a role at court, the plotters rented a lease on an undercroft, a ground-floor cellar, directly beneath the House of Lords. Over many months they moved in gunpowder, eventually 36 barrels, hidden under firewood. Parliament’s opening was postponed several times, partly because of plague, which gave them more time but also more chances to be found out.

The letter and the discovery

On 26 October 1605, Lord Monteagle, a Catholic peer, received an anonymous letter at his house in Hoxton warning him to stay away from Parliament, because it would “receive a terrible blow” and yet “not see who hurts them”. He took it straight to Robert Cecil, the King’s chief minister. The author has never been identified for certain; one of the plotters, Francis Tresham, Monteagle’s brother-in-law, is a favourite suspect. Historians still debate how much the authorities already knew and whether they let the plot run on to catch the plotters red-handed.

The cellars beneath Parliament were searched on 4 November. Late that night a second search found Fawkes, who gave his name as John Johnson, guarding the hidden barrels, with matches and touchwood in his pockets. He was arrested and taken to the Tower of London, where, on the King’s written authority, he was tortured. Over several days he gave up the names of his fellow conspirators.

The aftermath

The other plotters fled to the Midlands, hoping to raise support that never came. On 8 November, at Holbeche House in Staffordshire, the Sheriff of Worcestershire’s men surrounded them. Catesby and Thomas Percy were killed, reportedly by a single shot. The survivors were tried for treason in January 1606 and executed by hanging, drawing and quartering: four on 30 January in St Paul’s Churchyard, and four more, including Fawkes, on 31 January near the Houses of Parliament. Fawkes, weakened by torture, died from the fall from the gallows before the rest of the sentence could be carried out. The Jesuit superior Henry Garnet, who had learned of the plot through confession, was executed in May.

It is important to distinguish the conspirators from England’s Catholics generally. Their plan did not represent a whole religious population, and most Catholics were horrified. Nevertheless, the plot deepened suspicion and helped justify harsher laws. The original celebration was therefore not simply an innocent burst of relief after a narrowly avoided disaster. It was also bound up with state power, Protestant identity and hostility towards Catholicism. Remembering that uncomfortable background makes the history more truthful, without requiring today’s families to mean what a seventeenth-century crowd meant.

A celebration written into law

Bonfires and church bells marked the King’s escape soon after the plot was discovered. Commemoration was then made official. The Observance of 5th November Act 1605, passed in January 1606 (its date reflects the parliamentary session), required special thanksgiving services in every parish church each 5 November. For more than two centuries, “Gunpowder Treason Day” was one of the great public occasions of the English year, with church bells, sermons, bonfires and effigies, often of the Pope as well as of Fawkes. The Act was repealed only in 1859.

Over time the celebration took on a popular life of its own. Bonfires and effigies could express loyalty, local rivalry, satire or plain rowdiness. Children traditionally made a “guy” from old clothes stuffed with newspaper and wheeling it round the streets asking for “a penny for the guy” to buy fireworks, before burning it on the bonfire. The rhyme “Remember, remember, the fifth of November, gunpowder, treason and plot” became part of growing up in Britain. Later, firework manufacturers helped rebrand the occasion as Firework Night.

Lewes and other local traditions

Nowhere keeps the night quite like Lewes in East Sussex, sometimes called the bonfire capital of the world. Its bonfire societies march through the streets in costume, carrying flaming torches, and the town remembers not only the Gunpowder Plot but also seventeen Protestant martyrs burned there in the 1550s under Mary I; seventeen burning crosses are carried in their memory. Huge effigies, traditionally including Guy Fawkes and Pope Paul V (pope in 1605), are burned alongside topical figures of the year, which can make the night controversial. Tens of thousands of visitors attend. Elsewhere, Ottery St Mary in Devon is known for carrying burning tar barrels through the streets.

There are survivals in Westminster itself. The original undercroft is gone, destroyed in the great fire of 1834, but the lantern Fawkes is said to have carried that night is kept at the Ashmolean Museum in Oxford. Since the Popish Plot scare of 1678, the cellars of the Palace of Westminster have been ceremonially searched by the Yeomen of the Guard before each State Opening of Parliament, a ritual descended directly from the night Fawkes was caught.

Small details and modern echoes

Would it have worked? A 2005 television experiment built a replica of the old House of Lords and blew it up with a similar quantity of explosives; the room was destroyed. In reality, some of the plotters’ gunpowder had decayed and had to be replaced, so no one can be certain.

Fawkes himself has had a strange afterlife. Once the archetypal villain, he became a symbol of rebellion through the graphic novel and film V for Vendetta, and the stylised Guy Fawkes mask has been worn by protesters around the world. Bonfire Night is also no longer only English: it is marked in other parts of the UK and in some Commonwealth countries, notably New Zealand.

In modern Britain the organised display usually takes centre stage, and many of those attending are celebrating neither the King’s deliverance nor anyone’s punishment. They are enjoying a local ritual of late autumn, and concerns about pets, noise and safety have pushed many families towards big public displays rather than back-garden fireworks. That transformation is worth noticing: an anniversary once used to draw a hard boundary around belonging has become an occasion on which neighbours gather. The fireworks are fleeting, but the history beneath them deserves a longer look.
