Major occasion · Sunday 8 November 2026

Diwali — a welcome written in light

Diwali begins with light, but it does not end with one explanation. Small oil lamps, called diyas, stand along windows, courtyards and thresholds; homes are cleaned, sweets exchanged and families gathered. Its name comes from the Sanskrit Deepavali, a row or series of lamps. The familiar description “festival of lights” is accurate, provided we remember that different communities see different histories and sacred meanings in those flames. Diwali is a shared season, not a single story that everyone is required to tell.

There is an important correction to our autumn retrospective: in 2026 the principal Diwali day falls on Sunday 8 November, so it is tomorrow as I write on 7 November in Europe/London. Celebrations extend over several days, and public programmes may begin much earlier. Unlike Halloween, Diwali moves through the Gregorian calendar. The main Lakshmi Puja observance is associated with amavasya, the new-moon lunar day commonly described as falling in Kartika in the Hindu lunisolar calendar. Regional month-naming systems and ritual calculations differ, so local calendars can vary. Broadly, the festival falls in October or November; an astronomical new-moon instant alone does not supply every community’s festival date.

For many Hindus, particularly in northern India, its best-loved narrative is Rama’s homecoming. In the tradition associated with the Ramayana, Rama returns to Ayodhya with Sita after exile and the defeat of Ravana. Lamps welcome him home and celebrate the restoration of righteous rule. This is a sacred and literary account, not an event whose Gregorian anniversary historians can reconstruct. Its power is more intimate than that: the long absence ends, loved ones return, and a town lights the way. The festival makes homecoming something that can be enacted again.

Lakshmi, the goddess of prosperity and good fortune, is central to many households’ observance. Cleaning and illuminating the home becomes a welcome for her, while rangoli patterns made with coloured powders or flowers transform the entrance. Puja brings worship into the centre of the evening. In some business communities the season also involves accounts and financial renewal. Other regional traditions give prominence to other deities and episodes. The broad themes of light over darkness and knowledge over ignorance are helpful introductions, but cannot replace that variety of lived religious practice.

For Jains, Diwali commemorates Mahavira’s final liberation from the cycle of rebirth. According to Jain tradition, lamps were lit when the light of his presence had departed, giving visible form to the wisdom that remained. For Sikhs, the season includes Bandi Chhor Divas, commemorating the release of the sixth Guru, Guru Hargobind, from imprisonment. Sikh tradition tells how he secured freedom for 52 princes as well, with a specially prepared cloak they could hold. The emphasis is not simply personal deliverance but freedom shared with others. These are distinct religious meanings, not footnotes to a single Hindu explanation.

In Britain, Diwali belongs both to private homes and to the public life of cities. Leicester’s Golden Mile is an especially familiar expression: its lights and community celebrations make the festival visible far beyond the families keeping it religiously. The city’s checked 2026 programme culminates on 8 November, with puja, food, performance and a laser-show finale. That is a useful reminder that the modern festival changes its outward forms without losing its welcome. Whether the setting is a temple, a gurdwara, a family sitting room or a Leicester street, a small light says something generous: there is room here for hope, and room for you.
