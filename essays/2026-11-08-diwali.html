Major occasion · Sunday 8 November 2026

Diwali — a welcome written in light

Diwali begins with light, but it does not end with one explanation. Small oil lamps called diyas stand along windowsills, courtyards and doorsteps; homes are cleaned, sweets are shared and families gather. The name comes from the Sanskrit Deepavali, a row or series of lamps. “Festival of lights” is an accurate description, as long as we remember that Hindus, Sikhs, Jains and some Buddhists see different histories and sacred meanings in those flames. Diwali is a shared season, not a single story that everyone is required to tell.

In 2026 the main day of Diwali, the evening of Lakshmi Puja, fell on Sunday 8 November. The wider festival ran from Dhanteras on Friday 6 November to Bhai Dooj on Wednesday 11 November.

Why the date moves

Unlike Halloween or Bonfire Night, Diwali moves around the Gregorian calendar, falling in October or November. It follows the Hindu lunisolar calendar, in which months follow the moon and an extra month is added every few years to keep the calendar in step with the seasons. The main night is the Amavasya, the new moon, of the month of Kartika in the northern reckoning (or the end of Ashvin in calendars used in western and southern India). The darkest night of the month is the one chosen for the most lamps.

The precise day is worked out from tithis, lunar days that can begin at any hour. Lakshmi Puja is performed in the early evening while Amavasya is in force. In 2026, according to Drik Panchang’s calculations for New Delhi, Amavasya ran from 11.27am on 8 November to 12.31pm on 9 November, so Sunday evening was the only evening that qualified. Because the new moon was still in force at sunrise on Monday, that day became a quiet “gap” day, and Govardhan Puja and Bhai Dooj fell on Tuesday 10 and Wednesday 11 November in India. Some calendars made for other countries give those later days a day earlier, so local practice can differ.

The five days

For many Hindus Diwali is a five-day festival, each day with its own customs.

Dhanteras comes first, on the thirteenth lunar day of the dark fortnight. It is associated with Dhanvantari, the physician of the gods, and with prosperity, and it is a traditional day to buy something new for the home: a steel or brass pot, a silver coin, a new broom. In the evening many households place a lamp outside facing south, for Yama, the lord of death.

Naraka Chaturdashi, often called Choti Diwali or “little Diwali”, remembers Krishna’s victory over the demon king Narakasura. In much of southern India this dawn, with its ritual oil bath, is Deepavali itself. In 2026 the dawn observance fell on the same Sunday as Lakshmi Puja.

The third day is the heart of the festival: Lakshmi Puja on the new moon night, when the goddess of wealth and good fortune is welcomed into clean, brightly lit homes, usually together with Ganesha, the remover of obstacles. In Bengal, Odisha and Assam, the same night is given to the worship of the goddess Kali.

The fourth day is Govardhan Puja, remembering how Krishna lifted Mount Govardhan to shelter the people of Braj from the storms sent by Indra. Temples offer the Annakut, a “mountain” of food. In Gujarat it is also the start of the new year, and in Maharashtra and Karnataka it is Bali Pratipada.

The fifth day is Bhai Dooj (also Bhau Beej, Bhai Phonta or Bhai Tika), when sisters mark their brothers’ foreheads with a tilak and pray for their wellbeing, and brothers give gifts in return.

Rama, Lakshmi and many other stories

For many Hindus, particularly in northern India, Diwali’s best-loved story is Rama’s homecoming. In the tradition associated with the Ramayana, Rama returns to Ayodhya with Sita and his brother Lakshmana after fourteen years of exile and the defeat of Ravana. The people light lamps to welcome him home. This is a sacred and literary account, not an event whose anniversary historians can date. Its power is more intimate than that: the long absence ends, loved ones return, and a town lights the way.

Lakshmi is central to many households’ observance. Cleaning and decorating the home is a way of welcoming her, while rangoli patterns made with coloured powders, rice or flowers brighten the entrance. In many business communities the season also involves closing old accounts and blessing new account books, a practice called Chopda Puja in Gujarat. Other regions tell other stories: Krishna and Narakasura, Kali, or the return of the generous demon king Bali. The broad themes of light over darkness, good over evil and knowledge over ignorance are a helpful introduction, but they cannot replace that variety of lived practice.

Jain and Sikh meanings

For Jains, Diwali marks the nirvana of Mahavira, the 24th Tirthankara, his final liberation from the cycle of birth and death. Jain tradition tells that lamps were lit when the light of his presence had gone, so that his wisdom would remain visible. For many Jains the following day begins a new year.

For Sikhs, the season is Bandi Chhor Divas, the “day of liberation”. It commemorates the release of the sixth Guru, Guru Hargobind, from imprisonment in Gwalior Fort by the Mughal emperor Jahangir, and his return to Amritsar. Sikh tradition tells that he refused to leave without 52 imprisoned princes, and secured their freedom by having a cloak made with 52 tassels, so that each could hold on and walk out with him. The emphasis falls not on personal deliverance alone but on freedom shared with others. The Golden Temple in Amritsar is lit up and fireworks fill the sky. Newar Buddhists in Nepal also keep the festival, honouring Lakshmi, and in Nepal as a whole the season is celebrated as Tihar, with days honouring crows, dogs and cows as well as Lakshmi.

How old is it?

Diwali is very old, but it was not invented on a single date. Scholars generally see it as a blending of ancient harvest and lamp festivals. It is mentioned in Sanskrit texts such as the Padma Purana and the Skanda Purana, which were composed over many centuries in the first millennium. Lamps, the end of the harvest, the turning of the season towards winter, and the financial new year of merchants have all fed into it. In December 2025 UNESCO inscribed Deepavali on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, describing it as a festival that brings together people of many ages, backgrounds and beliefs.

Diwali in Britain and around the world

The main day of Diwali is a public holiday in India, Nepal and a number of countries with large Hindu communities, including Fiji, Guyana, Malaysia, Mauritius, Singapore, Sri Lanka, Suriname, and Trinidad and Tobago. In the UK it is not a bank holiday, but it is one of the most visible religious festivals in public life. Leicester’s celebrations are among the largest outside India. In 2026 the traditional lights once again lit the Golden Mile on Belgrave Road, the Wheel of Light returned, and Diwali Day on Sunday 8 November brought a Diwali Village to Cossington Street Recreation Ground, with puja led by the Leicester Hindu Festival Council, food stalls, a funfair, a stage finale by the Aakash Odedra Company with music from the Philharmonia Orchestra, and a laser show to close the night. London, Birmingham, Manchester and other cities hold their own events, and temples and gurdwaras around the country are full.

Small things worth knowing

Fireworks and Diwali now go together in many places, but they are a later addition to a festival of lamps, and air pollution from fireworks has become a serious concern in Indian cities, with some courts and governments restricting them. Sweets such as barfi, ladoo and jalebi are given to neighbours and colleagues regardless of faith. Gambling with cards is a traditional Diwali pastime in some families, said to bring good luck for the year.

Whether the setting is a temple, a gurdwara, a family sitting room or a Leicester street, a small light says something generous: there is room here for hope, and room for you.
