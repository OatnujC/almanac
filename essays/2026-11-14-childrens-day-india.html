Children’s Day (Bal Diwas), India

In India, 14 November is Children's Day, or Bal Diwas. Schools across the country swap ordinary lessons for assemblies, games, performances and treats, and it is a day for celebrating children and thinking about their rights, health and education. The date is the birthday of Jawaharlal Nehru, India's first prime minister, who was born on 14 November 1889 and is still remembered by many as Chacha Nehru, “Uncle Nehru”, because of his well-known affection for children.

Why 14 November?

Children's Day did not always fall on this date in India. It was previously celebrated on 20 November, the date the United Nations later made World Children's Day, marking the adoption of the Declaration of the Rights of the Child in 1959 and the Convention on the Rights of the Child in 1989. Nehru died in May 1964, and after his death Parliament passed a resolution making his birthday the country's official Children's Day. India has marked it on 14 November ever since, which is why its date differs from the international one.

The man behind the day

Nehru was born in Allahabad (now Prayagraj) into a prominent Kashmiri Pandit family. His father, Motilal Nehru, was a leading lawyer and a senior figure in the Indian National Congress. Jawaharlal was educated at Harrow School and Trinity College, Cambridge, and trained as a barrister in London before returning to India. There he became a close ally of Mahatma Gandhi in the independence movement, and spent, in total, around nine years in British prisons. On the night of 14 to 15 August 1947 he gave his celebrated “Tryst with Destiny” speech as India became independent, and he served as prime minister until his death in 1964, the longest tenure of any Indian prime minister.

His reputation for loving children rests on many small stories and on his writing. While he was in prison he wrote letters to his young daughter Indira, later prime minister herself, explaining the history of the world to her; they were published as Letters from a Father to His Daughter and later Glimpses of World History. He often said, in various forms, that children are the future of a nation, and that the way they are brought up will shape the country they grow into. His governments put money into schools, universities and scientific institutes, including the first Indian Institutes of Technology, which is why the day is often tied to education.

Nehru remains a debated figure in Indian politics, as founding leaders often are, and opinions differ on his record. But Children's Day itself is a warm, uncontroversial occasion, and the government and leaders across parties usually pay tribute on the day.

How it is marked today

The celebrations happen mainly in schools. In many schools the roles are reversed for a day, with teachers putting on songs, plays or dances for their pupils. There are fancy dress competitions, storytelling and poetry recitals, quizzes, sports and games, and campuses often turn into small fairs with stalls, prizes and sweets. Children frequently get the day off uniform, and some schools organise trips or film screenings. Many children receive small gifts, chocolates or books.

Beyond the fun, the day is used to draw attention to the serious side of childhood in India: child labour, nutrition, access to schooling and safety. Charities and children's organisations run campaigns, and the media carry features on children's rights. Leaders traditionally pay respects at Shanti Van in Delhi, the memorial where Nehru was cremated.

Around the world

Many countries have their own children's days on different dates. A large number of former Soviet bloc countries mark International Children's Day on 1 June, Japan celebrates Children's Day on 5 May, and the UN's World Children's Day falls on 20 November, less than a week after India's. India's choice of 14 November makes its celebration one of the few tied to a particular person's birthday, a link that gives the day its distinctive character: part school festival, part remembrance of the man who made the future of India's children one of his themes.
