Minor occasion · Sunday 8 November 2026

World Town Planning Day — how places become liveable

World Town Planning Day, also called World Urbanism Day, is kept each year on 8 November. It asks a simple, practical question: how should towns and cities be arranged so that people can live, work, move about and meet each other well? Streets, homes, parks, transport and the less visible systems beneath them are easy to take for granted until they fail. This is a day for noticing the craft that tries to hold those things together, and the many people, not only planners, whose decisions shape the places we live.

Why 8 November

The observance was started in 1949 by Carlos María della Paolera, a professor at the University of Buenos Aires, to raise public and professional interest in planning, at home and abroad. The date is fixed, and in 2026 it fell on a Sunday, making this the day’s 77th anniversary. The International Society of City and Regional Planners (ISOCARP) says it is now celebrated in some thirty countries on four continents, and it is supported by national planning bodies including the Royal Town Planning Institute in the UK and Ireland, the American Planning Association and the Canadian Institute of Planners.

A very old idea

People have planned settlements for thousands of years. Cities of the Indus Valley, such as Mohenjo-daro, were laid out with streets and drains more than four thousand years ago. In ancient Greece, Hippodamus of Miletus, in the fifth century BCE, became so associated with the regular grid of streets that it is still called the Hippodamian plan, although grid towns existed before him. The Romans planned their military camps and colonial towns on a grid too, and the outlines of some survive under British towns today.

Modern town planning, though, is largely a response to the industrial city. In the nineteenth century, overcrowded housing, polluted water and disease in fast-growing cities like Manchester, Paris and London led to new public health laws and to sweeping projects. Between 1853 and 1870, Baron Haussmann tore down much of medieval Paris to build wide boulevards, parks and sewers, creating the city’s familiar look and much controversy. In Barcelona, the engineer Ildefons Cerdà designed the Eixample in 1860, a grid of blocks with cut-off corners that still defines the city.

British contributions

Britain has played a large part in the story. In 1898 Ebenezer Howard published To-Morrow: A Peaceful Path to Real Reform, later reissued as Garden Cities of To-morrow, proposing self-contained towns combining the advantages of town and country, surrounded by green belts. Letchworth Garden City was begun in 1903 and Welwyn Garden City in 1920, and the idea spread around the world. The Housing, Town Planning, etc. Act 1909 was the first British law to use the term “town planning”, and the Town Planning Institute, now the Royal Town Planning Institute, was founded in 1914.

The biggest change came after the Second World War. The Town and Country Planning Act 1947, which came into force in 1948, required permission for most new development and asked local councils to prepare development plans. It is the foundation of the modern UK planning system. Alongside it came the New Towns, from Stevenage, the first to be designated in 1946, to Milton Keynes two decades later, built to give people from crowded and war-damaged cities new homes, and the green belts that still ring many English towns.

Planning today

Planning is rarely glamorous and often controversial. It is where arguments about housing, roads, wind farms, high streets, flooding and nature come to be settled, or at least argued over. In the UK, debates about how many homes to build and where are constantly in the news. Planners also work on less visible questions: how far people have to travel to a doctor or a school, whether streets are safe to walk and cycle on, how towns can cope with heavier rain and hotter summers.

For 2026, the Global Planners Network, which brings together national and international planning associations, chose the theme “Great communities are built on planning”. The Royal Town Planning Institute describes planning as working best when it is shaped with communities, drawing on local knowledge and lived experience as well as professional expertise, especially as places face housing affordability, climate change, health inequalities and rapid technological change.

How the day is marked

Planners and communities mark the day with lectures, school competitions, awards, fundraisers, exhibitions and street festivals. In 2026 the RTPI ran an online programme around the date, with sessions on water resilience, planning with children and young people, artificial intelligence and data, and affordable housing lessons from Europe. Universities and planning schools often hold open events.

A note on related days

World Town Planning Day is easy to confuse with World Cities Day, a United Nations observance on 31 October, which focuses on urbanisation and sustainable cities more broadly. The two make a natural pair, a week apart. Both invite the same everyday curiosity: next time you cross a square, wait for a bus or walk through a park, it is worth asking who decided it should be like this, and how it might be better.
