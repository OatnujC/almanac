Major occasion · Monday 9 November 2026

Fall of the Berlin Wall — the night the border opened

On the evening of Thursday 9 November 1989, after 28 years, the Berlin Wall stopped working. No army broke it, no treaty abolished it, and no one in charge quite meant it to happen that night. A confused announcement at a press conference, broadcast on television, sent thousands of East Berliners to the checkpoints, and in the end the guards let them through. Within hours people were dancing on top of the Wall at the Brandenburg Gate. The anniversary is one of the great dates of the twentieth century, and also one of its most surprising.

Why the date is fixed

This is a straightforward anniversary of a single night, so it falls on 9 November every year; in 2026 it is a Monday. It is not a public holiday in Germany. When the country was formally reunified on 3 October 1990, that date became the national day, the Day of German Unity. There has been a recurring debate about making 9 November the holiday instead, but the date carries too much else: the proclamation of a German republic in 1918, Hitler’s failed Munich putsch in 1923 and, above all, the Kristallnacht pogrom of 1938. Germans sometimes call it their Schicksalstag, the “day of fate”. On 9 November, joy and grief share a calendar square.

A city divided

After the Second World War, Germany and its capital were divided among the four victorious Allies. Berlin lay deep inside the Soviet zone, which in 1949 became the German Democratic Republic (GDR), or East Germany; West Berlin, the American, British and French sectors, became an island of the West. Until 1961 the sector boundary inside the city was relatively easy to cross, and it became the main escape route. Around 3.5 million East Germans, about a fifth of the population, left before the Wall went up, many of them young and skilled.

In the early hours of Sunday 13 August 1961, East German police and soldiers sealed the border. Barbed wire came first, then concrete. Over the years the barrier grew into a system: two walls with a “death strip” between them, raked sand to show footprints, watchtowers, dogs, floodlights and anti-vehicle trenches. The last version, built from 1975, used about 45,000 reinforced concrete sections 3.6 metres high. The barrier around West Berlin was about 155 kilometres long. The East German government called it the “Anti-Fascist Protection Rampart”; West Berlin’s mayor Willy Brandt called it the “Wall of Shame”.

Escaping was dangerous. Around 5,000 people got across. Researchers at the Centre for Contemporary History in Potsdam have confirmed at least 140 deaths connected with the Wall, among them Peter Fechter, an 18-year-old who was shot in 1962 and left to bleed to death in the death strip in full view of the West. The Wall became the Cold War’s most powerful image. John F. Kennedy told West Berliners “Ich bin ein Berliner” in 1963, and Ronald Reagan stood at the Brandenburg Gate in 1987 and said, “Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall!”

The summer and autumn of 1989

By 1989 the Soviet Union under Mikhail Gorbachev was reforming and no longer willing to send tanks to prop up its allies. Poland and Hungary were changing fast. Hungary began dismantling its border fence with Austria in the spring, and on 19 August a “Pan-European Picnic” on the border briefly opened a gate through which hundreds of East Germans fled. Thousands more followed through Hungary, or crowded into West German embassies in Prague and Warsaw. At home, peaceful demonstrations grew week by week, especially the Monday marches in Leipzig, with crowds chanting “Wir sind das Volk” (“We are the people”). Erich Honecker, East Germany’s leader since 1971, was replaced by Egon Krenz on 18 October. On 4 November a vast demonstration filled Alexanderplatz in East Berlin.

Krenz’s government tried to relieve the pressure by drafting new travel rules. On 9 November a text was approved that would let East Germans apply to travel or emigrate without the old conditions, including across the border into West Berlin. It was meant to start the following day, with an orderly process of applications.

The press conference

That evening the Politburo spokesman Günter Schabowski gave a long, televised press conference. Shortly before it, he had been handed a note about the new regulations, but he had not been part of the discussions and had not been properly briefed. Near the end, at 6.53pm, a question from the Italian journalist Riccardo Ehrman led him to read out the new rules. Asked when they would take effect, he shuffled his papers and replied that, as far as he knew, it was immediately, without delay.

The news spread at once. West German television, which most East Germans watched, led its evening bulletins with it. Crowds began gathering at the crossing points, waving their identity cards and demanding to be let through “as Schabowski said”. The guards had no orders. At the Bornholmer Straße crossing, the officer in charge, Harald Jäger, faced a crowd of thousands and could get no clear instructions from above. Some time between about 10.45 and 11.30pm (accounts differ), rather than use force, he opened the gates. Other crossings followed. East Berliners walked and drove into the West, greeted with flowers, champagne and tears. Some climbed onto the Wall itself.

Afterwards

In the days and weeks that followed, people known as Mauerspechte, “wall woodpeckers”, chipped away souvenirs with hammers and chisels. New crossing points were opened; the Brandenburg Gate opened on 22 December. Official demolition began in June 1990. East Germany held its first free elections in March 1990, and on 3 October 1990 the two German states were reunified.

It was not all straightforward. Reunification brought freedom and opportunity but also unemployment, closed factories and a sense among many East Germans that their lives and experience had been brushed aside. Thirty-seven years on, Germany still debates the economic and political differences between east and west. The joy of the night remains real, but historians increasingly tell the story of 1989 as one of East Germans’ own courage, not just a gift from the politicians.

How it is marked today

Berlin marks the anniversary every year. The Berlin Wall Memorial on Bernauer Straße, where a stretch of wall, death strip and watchtower has been preserved, holds a commemoration for the people who died there, and candles and flowers are left in the slots of the Wall’s remains. Round-number anniversaries bring larger events: in 2009 a line of giant foam dominoes was toppled along the old route, and in 2014 a “border of light” of 8,000 illuminated balloons traced about 15 kilometres of the former Wall through the city centre before being released, one after another, into the night sky. Exhibitions and concerts often run over several days, and because of the other anniversaries on 9 November, ceremonies frequently also remember the victims of the 1938 pogrom.

Remnants of the Wall can still be visited. The East Side Gallery beside the Spree is a long stretch covered in murals; a section survives next to the Topography of Terror museum; and a double row of cobblestones traces much of the old line through the city centre. A cycling and walking route, the Berliner Mauerweg, follows the course of the former border around West Berlin. Pieces of the Wall also stand in museums and public spaces around the world.

Small details and common mix-ups

The Wall did not “fall” in one night in any physical sense; it was opened, and the concrete came down over the following year. Schabowski’s slip is often told as a pure accident. It was certainly unplanned, but it happened because the regime was already trying to open the border under pressure from its own people. And it was not only Berlin: the border between East and West Germany as a whole, nearly 1,400 kilometres long, opened at the same time.

One last irony: for a few weeks East Germans could travel west more freely than West Germans could travel east. Until visa-free travel for Westerners began on 23 December 1989, visiting the East still meant applying for a visa in advance and changing a minimum amount of money each day. It is a fitting detail for an anniversary about something that had seemed permanent and turned out not to be.
