Major occasion · 16 November 2026

Chhath Puja: Usha Arghya

Before first light this morning, on the banks of the Ganges at Patna, by village ponds in Bihar and Jharkhand, on Mumbai's beaches and at temporary pools in Delhi and Kathmandu, families stood in the cold and the dark, waiting. As the first sliver of the sun appeared over the horizon, the people keeping the Chhath vow raised their bamboo trays of fruit and sweets and poured water towards it. This is Usha Arghya, the dawn offering, the fourth and final day of Chhath Puja, and with it one of the most demanding and best-loved festivals in the Hindu calendar comes to an end.

Why today

Chhath follows the Hindu lunisolar calendar. It begins on the fourth day of the bright fortnight of the month of Kartik, six days after Diwali, and its name comes from the word for six, because the main evening offering falls on Shashthi, the sixth lunar day. Usha Arghya is held at sunrise on the seventh day, Saptami. According to Drik Panchang for New Delhi and the Indian press, Chhath 2026 ran from Friday 13 to Monday 16 November: Nahay Khay on the 13th, Kharna on the 14th, Sandhya Arghya, the offering to the setting sun, yesterday evening, and Usha Arghya this morning. The New Delhi almanac has the Shashthi tithi ending at 2:00am on 16 November, after which Saptami prevails.

Because the offering is tied to the actual sunrise, its moment varies from place to place. The Economic Times listed sunrise at about 6:07am in Patna, 5:50am in Kolkata, 6:44am in New Delhi and 6:46am in Mumbai, all India time. For a sense of scale in UK terms, the Patna sunrise came at about 12:37am London time, so the ritual was over in Bihar while most of Britain slept. Families in the UK and other diaspora communities make their offering at their own local sunrise.

The dawn offering

For the vratins, the men and especially women who keep the Chhath vow, this morning ends an extraordinary fast. After the Kharna meal of jaggery rice pudding on the evening of the 14th, they have gone around 36 hours without food or even water, through a day of cooking offerings and an evening of standing waist-deep in water at sunset. Many families also kept the night vigil of kosi, lighting clay lamps under a little canopy of sugarcane stalks.

Well before dawn, the household walked back to the ghat, the steps or bank of a river or pond, carrying the same baskets of offerings: thekua, the crisp fried biscuits of wheat, jaggery and ghee; fresh fruit, coconuts and whole stalks of sugarcane; and sweets made without salt, onion or garlic. The vratins stepped into the water facing east and waited. When the sun rose, they offered arghya, water and the contents of their trays, to Surya, the Sun god, and prayed to Chhathi Maiya, the mother goddess believed to watch over children, for the health and long life of their families. Chhath songs in Bhojpuri, Maithili and Magahi rose over the water as they did.

Breaking the fast: Parana

After the offering comes Parana, the breaking of the fast. As the Economic Times notes, there is no single fixed clock time for it; it follows the dawn offering, local sunrise and the family's customs. Often the vratin first takes a little water or ginger and some of the prasad, the blessed offerings, before a proper meal. The prasad, above all thekua, is then shared with family, neighbours and anyone who comes by, and it is considered a blessing to receive some. For many families, the long walk home from the ghat in the early morning light, tired, hungry and happy, is one of the most treasured moments of the year.

Why the rising sun matters

Chhath is unusual among major Hindu festivals in having no temple image at its centre. Devotion is directed to the Sun itself, visible to everyone, thanked as the source of life, light and harvest. By making offerings first to the setting sun and then to the rising one, devotees honour the whole cycle of the day, ending and renewal, and the festival is often described as one of the few occasions in Hindu tradition when the setting sun is worshipped as well as the rising one. Ending the festival at dawn gives it a hopeful shape: it closes with light, a new day and the breaking of a fast.

Old roots, new reach

Chhath's origins are told through several stories, linking it to Sita after her return to Ayodhya, to Kunti and her son Karna, child of the Sun, and to Draupadi in the Mahabharata. Historians generally see it as a survival of very ancient sun worship blended with the folk traditions of the eastern Gangetic plain, and an Indian government background note describes it as rooted in Vedic sun worship. It is famous for its simplicity and equality: rich and poor bring much the same baskets of seasonal produce to the same water, and there is no priest required for the central offering.

Migration has carried Chhath far from Bihar. Labourers who left eastern India in the 19th and early 20th centuries took it to Mauritius, Fiji, Suriname, and Trinidad and Tobago, and newer migrants have brought it to the Gulf, the United States and the UK. Families from Bihar who now live in big Indian cities often travel home for it, filling trains for days beforehand. India is now working on a bid to have Chhath recognised on UNESCO's list of intangible cultural heritage, with reports of a possible multinational nomination involving countries including the UAE, Suriname and the Netherlands.

With today's sunrise, the ghats empty, the lamps are put away and the thekua is shared out. The festival is over for another year, but for millions of families it remains the moment that, more than any other, means home.
