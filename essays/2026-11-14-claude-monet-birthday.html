Minor occasion · Saturday 14 November 2026

Claude Monet born (1840)

Claude Monet was born in Paris on 14 November 1840, 186 years ago today. More than any other painter, he gave the world Impressionism: the attempt to catch a fleeting moment of light and atmosphere in quick, broken strokes of colour. This year has an extra resonance, because Monet died on 5 December 1926, so 2026 is also the centenary of his death, and museums in France and beyond have been marking it.

A boy from Le Havre

Although he was born in Paris, Monet grew up in the Channel port of Le Havre in Normandy, where his family moved when he was about five. As a teenager he was known locally for sharp caricatures of townspeople, which he sold in a framer's shop. There he met the landscape painter Eugène Boudin, who persuaded him to try painting outdoors, directly in front of the subject. Monet later said it was as if a veil had been torn away. Painting outdoors, en plein air, made possible by new portable tubes of paint, became the foundation of his whole career.

In Paris in the early 1860s he studied in the studio of Charles Gleyre, where he became friends with Pierre-Auguste Renoir, Alfred Sisley and Frédéric Bazille. These young painters were frustrated by the official Salon, which favoured highly finished historical and mythological subjects, and they were drawn instead to modern life, landscapes, and the play of sunlight on water and leaves.

The painting that named a movement

In 1874, tired of rejection by the Salon, Monet and his friends organised their own exhibition in the former studio of the photographer Nadar in Paris. Among Monet's entries was a hazy view of the harbour at Le Havre in the early morning, with an orange sun above grey-blue water, titled Impression, soleil levant (Impression, Sunrise). The critic Louis Leroy seized on the title to mock the whole group as “Impressionists”. The insult stuck, and the painters soon adopted it with pride.

For years Monet was very poor. He moved repeatedly, often owing money, and his first wife, Camille, who appears in many of his paintings, died in 1879 after years of hardship. Success came slowly through the 1880s, helped by the dealer Paul Durand-Ruel, who found buyers for Impressionist works in the United States.

Series and Giverny

In 1883 Monet settled at Giverny, a village on the Seine between Paris and Rouen, where he would live for the rest of his life. As his reputation grew he painted in series, returning again and again to the same subject at different times of day and in different seasons to record how the light changed it: haystacks, poplars, the façade of Rouen Cathedral. He also came to London several times, and his series of the Houses of Parliament, Charing Cross Bridge and Waterloo Bridge seen through fog, painted from the Savoy Hotel and St Thomas' Hospital, are among his most famous works.

At Giverny he created the gardens that became his great subject: a flower garden in front of the house, and across the road a water garden with a Japanese-style footbridge and a pond full of water lilies. For the last 30 years of his life he painted the water lilies, the Nymphéas, about 250 times, even as cataracts clouded his eyesight. The climax was a cycle of huge, wall-sized water lily paintings that he gave to the French state as a monument to peace after the First World War. They were installed in two oval rooms at the Musée de l'Orangerie in Paris, which opened to the public in 1927, a few months after his death.

Monet today

Monet's house and gardens at Giverny are now one of France's most visited attractions, drawing hundreds of thousands of visitors each year between spring and autumn. His paintings hang in museums around the world, including the National Gallery in London, and they regularly sell for enormous sums at auction. On his birthday, and especially in this centenary year, the simplest way to remember him is to look at the light: a sunrise over water, mist on a river, the changing colours of a November afternoon, all things he spent his life trying to paint.
