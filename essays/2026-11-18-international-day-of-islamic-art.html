Minor occasion · 18 November 2026

International Day of Islamic Art

Today is the International Day of Islamic Art, kept every 18 November since UNESCO proclaimed it at the 40th session of its General Conference in 2019. The day was proposed by Bahrain. Its purpose, in UNESCO’s words, is to raise awareness of past and contemporary artistic expressions of Islam, and of the contribution of that art to civilisation. It is also meant to encourage cultural diversity, freedom of expression, the protection of heritage, and conversation between cultures.

Why a day for Islamic art

Islamic art is not one style, and it is not confined to religious buildings. It is the name given to a family of arts that grew, over more than 1,400 years, in societies shaped by Islam, from Spain and West Africa to the Middle East, Central Asia, India and beyond. It includes the architecture of mosques, palaces, gardens and cities; the arts of the book, from Qur’anic manuscripts to scientific treatises and poetry; calligraphy, which holds a special place because of the status of the written word; ceramics, metalwork, textiles and carpets; and the geometric and vegetal ornament that many people first recognise as “Islamic.” Those forms travelled and changed. They absorbed late Roman, Persian, Central Asian and other traditions, and they in turn influenced art far outside the Muslim world, in Europe and elsewhere.

UNESCO’s day is careful to mention contemporary work as well as the great medieval and early modern monuments. Islamic art did not stop being made, and living artists continue to work with calligraphy, geometry, architecture and new mixed forms. UNESCO has even asked, in its material for the day, whether visitors have heard of calligraffiti, a modern meeting of calligraphy and street art. The point is not to freeze a heritage in a museum, but to treat it as a living language as well as a historical one.

Heritage, and the need to protect it

A day of celebration is also, in UNESCO’s framing, a day about protection. Mosques, shrines, libraries and old city quarters have been damaged or destroyed in wars and by neglect, and objects have been looted and sold on. Marking Islamic art as a shared inheritance is a way of saying that this heritage does not belong only to one nation or one community, and that its loss diminishes everyone. The same thought runs through UNESCO’s wider work on World Heritage and on the intangible heritage of music, craft and performance. The day sits alongside other UNESCO dates that touch the same cultural world, including World Arabic Language Day.

How it is marked

UNESCO invites anyone to take part with debates, conferences, workshops, concerts, and exhibitions. Museums of Islamic art, from Doha and Cairo to London, Paris, Berlin and Kuala Lumpur, often use the date for talks or special displays. In 2026 the Islamic World Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization, ICESCO, discussed with Bahrain a larger programme of events, including workshops, a seminar and competitions for young people, continuing the role Bahrain has played since it first proposed the day.

The sources do not record a single historical event on 18 November that explains the choice of date, and it is better to say so than to invent one. Bahrain proposed 18 November, and UNESCO accepted it. What matters is what the day is for: a chance, once a year, to look carefully at one of the world’s great artistic traditions, in both its historic and its present forms, and to treat the looking as a kind of dialogue rather than a claim.
