Major occasion · 19 November 2026

World Toilet Day

It is not the most glamorous subject on the calendar, and that is rather the point. World Toilet Day, held every year on 19 November, exists because the things people are too embarrassed to talk about tend to be the things that never get fixed. According to the United Nations, 3.4 billion people, about four in every ten people on Earth, still live without access to safe sanitation. The day is an official UN observance, coordinated by UN-Water, and its aim is simple: to get people talking, and then acting, on what the UN calls the global sanitation crisis.

This year: safe toilets, equal futures

Each year UN-Water picks a theme, and for 2026 it is sanitation and gender equality, with the campaign slogan “Safe toilets. Equal futures.” The UN’s argument is that toilets are not just a matter of plumbing but of fairness. With safe and secure toilets, alongside soap, water and menstrual hygiene facilities, girls can stay in school and women can work. In hospitals and clinics, decent sanitation protects women patients and health workers. In markets, bus stations and other public places, toilets make it possible for women and girls to move about independently and take part in public life.

The flip side is stark. The UN says that unsafe sanitation exposes women and girls to disease, danger and abuse, and that having to use broken or dirty toilets, or to go outside, brings stigma and shame. Its fact sheet for this year notes that one in five adolescent girls has to share unhygienic toilets with other households or go out in the open, that fewer than one in three schools worldwide have menstrual waste bins in girls’ toilets, and that 1.7 billion people have nowhere to wash their hands with soap and water at home. It also points out that women are under-represented in the people who run sanitation: in 79 countries they hold fewer than half of the government jobs in water, sanitation and hygiene. The campaign’s message is that women must help design and manage sanitation services, working alongside men, so that those services work for everybody. The campaign is led by UN-Water’s expert group on drinking water, sanitation and hygiene, co-coordinated by UNICEF and the World Health Organization.

A Singaporean businessman and a summit

World Toilet Day began not at the UN but with one determined man in Singapore. Jack Sim grew up poor, started his first business at 24 and, by his own account, ran 16 businesses before retiring at 40. He then took up a challenge from Singapore’s prime minister, Goh Chok Tong, who had said a gracious nation could be measured by the cleanliness of its public toilets. Sim founded the Restroom Association of Singapore in 1998, and on 19 November 2001 he launched the World Toilet Organization, a non-profit dedicated to ending the sanitation crisis. That same day he held the first World Toilet Summit in Singapore, and he called it the first “World Toilet Day”. The organisation’s 25th-anniversary history notes that the summit led a Singapore minister to announce a grant that helped upgrade 400 coffee shop toilets, and that invitations soon came from South Korea, China and beyond. A World Toilet College followed in 2005.

For more than a decade, Sim lobbied governments to make the day official. According to the organisation, the answer he kept hearing was that toilets were “too frivolous”. The breakthrough came through Singapore’s own government. On 24 July 2013 the UN General Assembly adopted by consensus a resolution called “Sanitation for All”, tabled by Singapore and co-sponsored by 122 countries, designating 19 November as World Toilet Day. It was Singapore’s first resolution before the General Assembly. Sim later recalled watching the vote live at 3am Singapore time and seeing the gavel come down with no objection. UN News reported at the time that the Assembly urged countries to encourage changes in behaviour and policy, and to end open defecation, which it called extremely harmful to public health. That is why the date is 19 November: it is the World Toilet Organization’s founding day. This year, 2026, is the organisation’s 25th anniversary.

A different theme every year

Since 2013 UN-Water has been the official convener, running the World Toilet Day website and choosing each year’s theme. Since 2016 the theme has been shared with World Water Day in March, drawing on the UN’s annual World Water Development Report. Past themes give a sense of how wide the subject is: toilets and nutrition (2015), toilets and jobs (2016), wastewater (2017), nature-based solutions, with the slogan “When Nature calls” (2018), leaving no one behind (2019), climate change (2020), valuing toilets (2021), groundwater (2022), accelerating change (2023), toilets as a place for peace (2024) and sanitation in a changing world (2025). UN-Water has said that 2027 will explore water, sanitation and health.

Behind all of these sits one of the UN’s Sustainable Development Goals. Goal 6 aims to ensure water and sanitation for all, and its target 6.2 is to end open defecation and provide access to sanitation and hygiene by 2030, paying special attention to the needs of women and girls. The UN has repeatedly warned that the world is off track.

How it is marked

World Toilet Day is mostly marked through campaigns, reports and events organised by UN agencies, charities, local groups and volunteers. Organisations often choose the day to publish research on sanitation. In various years there have been “Urgent Run” fun runs, in places from German schools to Senegal and Singapore, and in Singapore a monument to UN World Toilet Day stands at the Marina Barrage. The World Toilet Organization says that each year NGOs, businesses and governments come together to discuss sanitation under the year’s theme, and the UN encourages anyone to download its campaign materials and spread the word.

It may raise a smile, and the organisers do not mind if it does. Jack Sim’s whole approach has been to break the taboo with humour. His organisation sums up the philosophy in his own words: “What we don’t discuss, we can’t improve.”
