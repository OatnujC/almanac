Indigenous Veterans Day — honour, and an overdue reckoning

Every year on 8 November, Canada marks Indigenous Veterans Day. The Canadian Department of National Defence notes that the day was established in 1994. It honours the service and sacrifice of First Nations, Inuit and Métis veterans, and it also asks the country to remember that many of those veterans were not treated equally when they came home.

Indigenous people have served in Canadian military efforts for generations, including the War of 1812 and the South African War, then through the two world wars, Korea and later operations. According to the Department of National Defence, more than 4,000 Indigenous people served in the First World War; more than 3,000 First Nations members served in the Second World War, alongside Inuit and Métis people; and more than 12,000 are estimated to have served in the twentieth century, with 500 losing their lives. In the Second World War, Indigenous languages such as Cree were used to encode military communications.

Many who had served shoulder to shoulder with other Canadians returned to find they were denied the same benefits, land grants and help with civilian life. The day exists alongside Remembrance Day, not instead of it: a distinct date, three days earlier, so that this particular history is not folded into a more general silence and lost. It is marked with ceremonies, storytelling and quiet reflection.
