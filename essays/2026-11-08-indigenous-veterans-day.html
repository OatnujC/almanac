Minor occasion · Sunday 8 November 2026

Indigenous Veterans Day — honour, and an overdue reckoning

Every year on 8 November, Canada marks Indigenous Veterans Day. It honours the First Nations, Inuit and Métis people who have served in Canada’s armed forces, and it asks the country to remember something harder too: that many of those veterans came home to find they were not treated as equals. In 2026 the day fell on a Sunday, the same day as Remembrance Sunday in the UK.

Why 8 November

The day sits three days before Remembrance Day on 11 November, deliberately separate from it. Canada’s Department of National Defence says the observance was established in 1994; it began in Winnipeg, Manitoba, and has since spread across the country. Having a distinct date matters. On a general day of remembrance, the particular story of Indigenous service, and of the injustice that followed it, could easily be folded into a wider silence and lost. Indigenous Veterans Day makes room for it. The day was also written into Manitoba law in 2025.

A long tradition of service

Indigenous people fought alongside British forces in North America long before Canada existed, including in the War of 1812, and served in the South African War of 1899 to 1902. The scale of their contribution in the twentieth century was remarkable, especially as many had to overcome enormous obstacles to enlist: learning a new language, adapting to an unfamiliar culture, and travelling great distances from remote communities just to reach a recruiting office.

In the First World War, more than 4,000 Indigenous people served in uniform. In some areas, one in three able-bodied men volunteered, and in a few communities, such as the Head of the Lake Band in British Columbia, every man between 20 and 35 enlisted. Many brought skills honed by hunting, such as patience, stealth and marksmanship, and became snipers and scouts. Indigenous soldiers earned at least 50 decorations for bravery. Henry Louis Norwest, a Métis from Alberta, was one of the most famous snipers in the Canadian Corps and was awarded the Military Medal and bar. Francis Pegahmagabow, an Ojibwa from Ontario, was another celebrated sniper and scout.

In the Second World War, more than 3,000 First Nations members served, along with an unknown number of Inuit and Métis recruits, most of them in the Canadian Army. Some served as “code talkers”: Cree speakers such as Charles “Checker” Tomkins translated sensitive radio messages into Cree so that they could not be understood if the enemy intercepted them. Communities at home also contributed money, clothing and food, and allowed reserve land to be used for airfields and training ranges.

Several hundred Indigenous people served in the Korean War. Among them was Tommy Prince, an Ojibwa from Manitoba and one of Canada’s most decorated Indigenous soldiers, who had already fought in the Second World War. Veterans Affairs Canada estimates that more than 12,000 First Nations, Métis and Inuit people served in the great conflicts of the twentieth century, and that at least 500 of them died.

Coming home

The Department of National Defence puts it plainly: many Indigenous veterans “returned to a country that failed to honour them equally”. After the First World War, the Soldier Settlement Act of 1919 gave land to returning soldiers, some of it taken from reserves, while most First Nations veterans who applied were instead given only a certificate to use reserve land that already belonged to their own communities. Because of their legal status under the Indian Act, many could not receive the pensions, grants and benefits that other veterans received. After the Second World War, many again could not access the full benefits of the Veterans’ Land Act, and support programmes were run from towns and cities far from remote reserves. Veterans who had served side by side with non-Indigenous comrades came home to residential schools, restricted rights and a lack of recognition.

Recognition came slowly. Indigenous veterans’ organisations campaigned for decades, and Indigenous Veterans Day itself grew out of that effort. In 2001 the National Aboriginal Veterans Monument was unveiled in Ottawa. It is topped by a bronze eagle, beneath which stand four figures from different Indigenous groups, with a wolf, a bear, a bison and a caribou looking out from its corners, animals that represent spiritual guides in many Indigenous cultures.

How the day is marked

Ceremonies are held at the monument in Ottawa and at legion halls, community centres and memorials across Canada, often combining military tradition with Indigenous practices such as smudging, drumming and prayers led by Elders. Canadian Armed Forces members and the public are invited to attend, read the stories of Indigenous veterans, or simply reflect. Indigenous people continue to serve today, in peacekeeping missions, in Afghanistan, and with the Canadian Rangers, reservists in the North and remote coastal areas whose knowledge of the land helps maintain Canada’s presence there.

The day is both commemoration and correction. It honours service and sacrifice, and it acknowledges that the gratitude owed to these veterans was, for a long time, withheld.
