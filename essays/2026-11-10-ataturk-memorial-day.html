Major occasion · Tuesday 10 November 2026

Atatürk Memorial Day (Turkey)

At 09:05 this morning in Turkey, which is 06:05 here in the UK, sirens sounded across the country and millions of people stopped where they stood. Cars halted in the middle of roads, pedestrians froze on pavements, and classrooms and offices fell silent. This is the annual commemoration of the death of Mustafa Kemal Atatürk, the soldier and statesman who founded the modern Republic of Turkey. He died at that minute on 10 November 1938, and 2026 marks the 88th anniversary.

Why 10 November, and why 09:05

The date and time are simply those of his death. Atatürk had been ill for more than a year, and in 1938 doctors diagnosed cirrhosis of the liver. He spent his last months in Istanbul, at first on his new yacht Savarona and then in a room at the Dolmabahçe Palace, the grand waterfront palace on the Bosphorus that served as his official residence in the city. His death certificate, signed by nine medical professors, recorded that he died there at 09:05 local time on 10 November 1938. He was 57.

That minute is now fixed in Turkish public life. The clocks in Dolmabahçe Palace are traditionally kept stopped at 09:05, and the nationwide silence happens at exactly that time each year. It is not a public holiday, so schools and businesses stay open, but the moment of stillness is observed almost everywhere.

Who Atatürk was

Mustafa Kemal was born in 1881 in Salonica, then an Ottoman city and now Thessaloniki in Greece. He became an army officer and made his name in the First World War at Gallipoli in 1915, where he commanded Ottoman troops against the Allied landings, including British, Australian and New Zealand forces. That is a battle remembered on both sides, and it gives the anniversary a particular resonance in Britain, Australia and New Zealand.

After the Ottoman defeat in 1918, the empire's lands were occupied and divided by the victorious powers. Mustafa Kemal led the Turkish nationalist movement in the War of Independence from 1919 to 1922, which ended with the Treaty of Lausanne in 1923. On 29 October 1923 the Republic of Turkey was proclaimed, with Ankara as its capital and Mustafa Kemal as its first president.

What followed was a sweeping programme of reforms. The sultanate and then the caliphate were abolished, a secular legal code replaced religious law, the Latin alphabet replaced Arabic script for writing Turkish in 1928, and women gained the right to vote and stand in national elections in 1934. The same year, a law required every family to take a surname, and parliament gave him the name Atatürk, meaning "father of the Turks".

The funeral and Anıtkabir

The day after his death, parliament in Ankara elected İsmet İnönü as the new president. Atatürk's body lay in state at Dolmabahçe, where thousands of mourners filed past. On 19 November the coffin was carried to the battlecruiser Yavuz, taken by sea to İzmit and then by funeral train to Ankara. The state funeral there on 21 November was attended by dignitaries from seventeen nations.

His body rested at the Ethnography Museum in Ankara for fifteen years while a permanent mausoleum was built. That mausoleum, Anıtkabir, was designed by the architects Emin Onat and Orhan Arda after an international competition. Construction began in 1944, and on 10 November 1953, the fifteenth anniversary of his death, Atatürk's remains were moved there in a great ceremony. Anıtkabir stands on a hill in Ankara and is approached along the Lion Road, an avenue lined with stone lions leading to a vast ceremonial square. Its style blends modern Turkish neoclassicism with echoes of the Hittite, ancient Greek, Seljuk and Ottoman cultures of the land.

How the day is marked

The day centres on Anıtkabir, where the state holds an official ceremony led by the country's senior leaders, who lay wreaths and sign the memorial book. Ordinary people come in large numbers too, often queuing for hours. Across Turkey, flags fly at half-mast, schools hold assemblies with poems and readings, and television and radio broadcast tributes. The image that sticks for many visitors is the 09:05 silence itself: a busy street in Istanbul or Ankara suddenly still, drivers standing beside their cars with heads bowed.

Turkish communities abroad, including in Britain, hold their own memorial events at embassies, consulates and community centres. Coming the day before Armistice Day here, it is a reminder that 10 and 11 November are days of remembrance in more than one country.
