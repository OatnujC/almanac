Minor occasion · Thursday 12 November 2026

Philae lands on a comet (2014)

Twelve years ago today, on 12 November 2014, a small European lander the size of a washing machine dropped gently onto the surface of a comet more than 500 million kilometres from Earth. It was the first time any spacecraft had landed on a comet. The lander, Philae, then did something nobody had planned: it bounced, sailed across the comet for two hours and came to rest wedged in a shady crack. The landing was a triumph and a cliffhanger at the same time, and millions of people followed it live.

The Rosetta mission

Philae travelled to the comet aboard the European Space Agency’s Rosetta orbiter. Rosetta set off from Kourou in French Guiana in 2004 on a journey of ten years, using flybys of Earth and Mars to build up speed and spending 31 months in deep-space hibernation before waking in January 2014. It arrived at its target, Comet 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko, named after the two Soviet astronomers who discovered it, on 6 August 2014, and became the first spacecraft to orbit a comet. The comet turned out to be a strange double-lobed shape, often compared to a rubber duck.

Comets matter to scientists because they are left-overs from the birth of the solar system, about 4.6 billion years ago, preserved in the deep freeze of space. Studying one up close could reveal what the raw building blocks of the planets were made of, and even whether comets helped deliver water and the ingredients of life to the young Earth.

Two names from Egypt

The names come from the story of how ancient Egyptian writing was decoded. The Rosetta Stone carried the same text in Greek and Egyptian scripts. An obelisk from Philae, an island in the Nile, carried a bilingual inscription including the names of Cleopatra and Ptolemy in hieroglyphs, and, as ESA explained when it named the lander, this gave the French scholar Jean-François Champollion the final clues he needed to crack the hieroglyphs. Just as the stone and the obelisk unlocked an ancient civilisation, the mission hoped to unlock the secrets of comets. The name Philae was suggested in a competition by Serena Olga Vismara, a 15-year-old from Arluno near Milan. The planned landing site was named Agilkia, after the island to which the temples of Philae were moved when the Aswan dams flooded their original home.

Landing day

Philae was released from Rosetta on the morning of 12 November and spent about seven hours drifting down towards the comet. Because the comet’s gravity is so weak, the lander had three ways to hold on: ice screws in its feet, two harpoons, and a small thruster on top to push it down while the harpoons fired. The night before, attempts to arm the thruster failed. ESA decided to go ahead anyway.

At 15:35 GMT Philae touched down at Agilkia, almost exactly on target. Because radio signals took around 28 minutes to cross the gap, the confirmation reached mission control in Darmstadt, Germany, about half an hour later, to cheering. But neither the harpoons nor the thruster worked, and the ground at Agilkia proved much harder than expected, so the ice screws could not bite. Philae rebounded off the surface. ESA’s later reconstruction showed it flying across a depression on the comet’s smaller lobe, hitting the edge of a cliff at 16:20 GMT, touching the surface several more times and finally settling at 17:31 GMT in a place later named Abydos, in the shadow of a cliff.

Three days of science

Philae was lying at an angle in a spot that got little sunlight, so its solar panels could not recharge its batteries. Running on its main battery, the lander completed about 80 per cent of its planned first science sequence. Its instruments sniffed the comet’s gases and dust, measured the hardness of the ground and sent back pictures from the surface. In the early hours of 15 November, the battery ran out and Philae fell silent.

There was a postscript. As the comet moved closer to the Sun, Philae woke up and contacted Rosetta on 13 June 2015, but only eight brief, intermittent contacts were made before 9 July, and it was never heard from again. Its exact resting place remained a mystery until 2 September 2016, when Rosetta, flying just 2.7 kilometres above the surface, photographed the lander jammed in a dark crack with two of its legs visible. ESA announced the find a few days later, with less than a month of the mission left. On 30 September 2016 Rosetta itself was steered down to a controlled impact on the comet, ending the mission alongside its little companion.

Remembering Philae

The anniversary is not a public occasion, but it is warmly remembered by space fans, and ESA and the German Aerospace Center, DLR, which led the consortium that built Philae, regularly mark it. Part of the appeal was the way the landing was shared. Rosetta and Philae had cheerful cartoon versions and social media accounts, and millions of people watched the bounces unfold online, cheering a robot as if it were a person. Even its imperfect landing became part of the story: it showed how hard it is to touch down on a small, unknown world, and how much could still be learned when things went wrong. For a few days in November 2014, a lander named after an island in the Nile had the whole world looking up.
