Major occasion · Saturday 14 November 2026

World Diabetes Day

World Diabetes Day falls on 14 November every year. It is the world's largest diabetes awareness campaign, led by the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), and it reaches, by the federation's own count, an audience of more than a billion people in over 160 countries. It is also an official United Nations observance, one of only a handful of health days to have been given that status by a full resolution of the General Assembly.

Why 14 November?

The date honours a person rather than an event. Frederick Grant Banting was born on 14 November 1891 in Alliston, Ontario, a farming town north of Toronto. He trained as a doctor, served as a battalion medical officer in the First World War, where he was wounded and decorated for bravery, and afterwards set up a modest surgical practice in London, Ontario. It was while preparing a lecture there in 1920 that he became gripped by an idea: that the pancreas might hold an internal secretion capable of controlling blood sugar, if only it could be isolated before the organ's digestive juices destroyed it.

In 1921, working in a borrowed laboratory at the University of Toronto under the physiologist John Macleod, Banting and a young student assistant, Charles Best, set out to test that idea. Their experiments, later refined with the help of the biochemist James Collip, produced an extract that could lower blood sugar. In January 1922 it was given to a 14-year-old Toronto boy, Leonard Thompson, who was dying of diabetes. After an improved extract was used, he recovered. Before insulin, a diagnosis of what we now call type 1 diabetes was, in most cases, a death sentence; afterwards it became a condition that people could live with for decades.

The breakthrough was recognised astonishingly quickly. The 1923 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to Banting and Macleod. Banting, who felt strongly that Best deserved the credit, shared his half of the prize money with him, and Macleod in turn shared his with Collip. At 32, Banting remains among the youngest people ever to receive the medicine prize. He was knighted in 1934 and died in a plane crash in Newfoundland in 1941, while on wartime medical research work.

From a federation campaign to a UN day

World Diabetes Day was created in 1991 by the IDF and the World Health Organization, in response to growing alarm about the rising number of people with diabetes around the world. For its first fifteen years it was mainly a campaign of the medical and patient community. That changed in December 2006, when the UN General Assembly passed Resolution 61/225, which recognised diabetes as a chronic, debilitating and costly disease and designated 14 November as a UN observance from 2007 onwards.

The campaign that won the resolution also gave the cause its symbol: a simple blue circle. Blue is the colour of the sky and of the UN flag, and the circle stands for unity and for the global diabetes community coming together. Each November you will see it on lapel pins, social media profiles and, after dark, on lit-up landmarks around the world.

The 2026 theme: early action

Each year has a theme, and the IDF has chosen “Early action on diabetes” for 2026 and 2027, with this year's focus on early diagnosis and risk reduction. The reasoning is sobering. The federation estimates that around 252 million people are living with diabetes without knowing it, and that by 2050 the number of people living with the condition could reach 853 million. The World Health Organization notes that more than 40 per cent of people with diabetes worldwide are undiagnosed, and that member states have signed up to a target of 80 per cent of people with diabetes being diagnosed by 2030.

The campaign's key messages are practical. Know the common symptoms: increased thirst, frequent urination, tiredness and blurred vision. Remember that type 1 diabetes can develop at any age, and is often missed or mistaken for type 2 in adults. Recognise that type 2 diabetes usually develops gradually and may show few symptoms for years, so that for many people the first sign is a complication affecting the eyes, kidneys, heart or nerves. And make sure that diagnosis is followed by affordable, uninterrupted care. The WHO also stresses diabetes stigma: the words and images people use about the condition matter, and everyone living with it deserves support rather than blame.

How it is marked

On the day itself, health services and charities run free screening sessions and risk checks, schools and workplaces hold awareness events, and buildings from city halls to famous monuments are lit up in blue. In the UK, Diabetes UK uses the day to encourage people to check their risk of type 2 diabetes, and many pharmacies and GP surgeries promote checks around this time. In Banting's own country there is a moving reminder of the day's origins: in London, Ontario, the house where he had his idea is now Banting House, a museum, and in front of it burns the Flame of Hope, lit in 1989, which is meant to stay alight until a cure for diabetes is found.

More than a century after a frightened teenager in Toronto received the first insulin injection, World Diabetes Day is a reminder both of how far medicine has come, and of how many people still do not get the diagnosis and care that discovery made possible.
