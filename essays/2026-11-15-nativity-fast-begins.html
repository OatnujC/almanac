Minor occasion · 15 November 2026

Nativity Fast begins (Eastern Christianity)

While much of Western Europe is still a fortnight away from Advent, a large part of the Christian world begins its preparation for Christmas today. For Orthodox churches that follow the Revised Julian calendar, and for the Eastern Catholic churches of the Byzantine tradition, 15 November is the first day of the Nativity Fast, a 40-day season of fasting, prayer and almsgiving that runs until Christmas Eve, 24 December. It is sometimes called Winter Lent, and sometimes Philip's Fast, because it begins on the day after the feast of St Philip the Apostle on 14 November.

Why it starts today

The Nativity Fast lasts 40 days, echoing the 40 days of Great Lent before Easter and the many forty-day periods in the Bible, from Moses on Mount Sinai to Christ's fast in the wilderness. Counting back 40 days from Christmas Eve lands on 15 November. The Gregorian date depends on which calendar a church follows. Churches using the Revised Julian calendar, which currently matches the Gregorian, such as the Greek, Romanian and Bulgarian churches, begin today. Churches that still keep the older Julian calendar, including the Russian, Serbian and Georgian churches, the Patriarchate of Jerusalem and Mount Athos, celebrate Christmas on 7 January in the modern calendar, so for them the fast begins on 28 November, which is 15 November in the Julian reckoning. The Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox churches keep a related "Fast of the Prophets" from 24 November to their Christmas on 7 January.

What the fast involves

In the Orthodox tradition, fasting means a change of diet as much as eating less. During the Nativity Fast the traditional rule asks people to give up meat, poultry, eggs and dairy, and on many days also fish, olive oil and wine. It is gentler than Great Lent: fish, wine and oil are allowed on weekends and many weekdays, and on certain feast days within the season, such as St Andrew on 30 November and St Nicholas on 6 December. The last days, from 20 to 24 December, are stricter, and Christmas Eve, called Paramony, is traditionally a day without solid food until the first star appears in the evening sky. As always in Orthodox practice, the sick, the very young and old, and nursing mothers are exempt, and people are encouraged to agree any adjustment with their priest. In the Byzantine Catholic churches the rules today are largely voluntary.

The point, Orthodox writers stress, is spiritual rather than merely physical. Controlling the appetite for food is seen as a way to learn control of other passions and to turn the mind towards God, and fasting is meant to go together with prayer, repentance and generosity to others.

A season of prophets

The Nativity Fast has its own rich liturgy. Along the way the Church remembers the Old Testament prophets who foretold Christ's coming, among them Obadiah on 19 November, Nahum, Habakkuk and Zephaniah in early December, Haggai on 16 December, and Daniel and the Three Holy Youths on 17 December. The three young men who survived the fiery furnace in the Book of Daniel are especially loved in this season, both for their faithful fasting and because their unharmed survival in the flames is read as an image of the Virgin Mary carrying God within her without being consumed. A great feast falls inside the fast, the Entry of the Theotokos into the Temple on 21 November, after which Christmas hymns begin to be sung on Sundays. Two Sundays before Christmas the Church honours the Forefathers, from Adam to the prophets, and on the Sunday before Christmas the Gospel reading is the genealogy of Christ from St Matthew.

East and West

The Western Church once had something similar: an early-medieval fast running from St Martin's Day on 11 November, sometimes called St Martin's Lent, which eventually became the shorter season of Advent, now four Sundays long in the Roman rite and six weeks in the Ambrosian rite of Milan. Advent's themes look to three "comings" of Christ, while the Eastern fast focuses above all on celebrating the Incarnation itself.

For Orthodox households, today often means clearing the fridge of cheese and butter and turning to beans, lentils, vegetables and, on the right days, fish. It also marks the true start of the Christmas season: 40 days of quieter eating, longer prayers and anticipation, ending in the midnight services and feasting of the Nativity.
