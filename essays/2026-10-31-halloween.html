Major occasion · Saturday 31 October 2026

Halloween — making friends with the dark

Halloween is the evening when the familiar world puts on a strange face. A pumpkin grins beside a front door; children become witches, skeletons or whatever character has captured their imagination; a suburban street briefly turns into a small theatre. In Britain today it can feel like a thoroughly modern commercial celebration. Yet its mixture of play, hospitality, unease and remembrance has a much longer history, rooted in these islands as well as reshaped across the Atlantic.

Its date is fixed: 31 October, which fell on a Saturday in 2026. The name gives the clearest explanation. Halloween is a shortening of All Hallows’ Eve, the evening before All Hallows’, or All Saints’ Day, on 1 November. A hallow is a saint or holy person. All Souls’ Day follows on 2 November, traditionally a day of prayer for the departed. Together these Christian observances made the turn from October to November a season in which the living thought especially about the dead.

Behind and alongside that Christian calendar stands Samhain, the Gaelic seasonal festival associated with the end of harvest and the beginning of winter. In Irish tradition this was a threshold in the year, a time particularly suited to stories about encounters with the otherworld. The connection helps explain why Halloween feels poised between warmth and exposure, plenty and scarcity, the household and whatever waits beyond it. But it is worth resisting the neat claim that every modern Halloween custom is an unchanged survival from ancient Celtic religion. Evidence for early practices is incomplete, and centuries of Christian observance, local folklore and reinvention intervene. Halloween has several ancestors, not one birth certificate.

One of its most touching predecessors is souling. Around the season of All Saints and All Souls, people went from house to house asking for food, sometimes receiving small soul cakes in exchange for prayers for the household’s dead. English Heritage describes this as a possible precursor to trick-or-treating. The relationship is suggestive rather than a simple, unbroken line, but the resemblance is lovely: a stranger arrives at the door, a little performance or promise is offered, and something sweet changes hands. The modern encounter retains the shape of an older exchange of charity and remembrance, even when neither side knows it.

Disguise adds another strand. Guising, especially associated with Scotland and Ireland, made visiting in costume a neighbourhood performance rather than merely a demand for sweets. Older lanterns could be carved from turnips; the pumpkin became the familiar emblem as the celebration developed in North America, where that large autumn fruit offered an excellent canvas for a face. Migration carried customs abroad, and American popular culture later helped carry their transformed versions back. Calling Halloween simply an American import therefore misses the round trip.

Today the festival ranges from quiet pumpkin carving to elaborate horror attractions, with apple games, parties and ghost stories somewhere between. For most participants the fright is carefully bounded: the monster is a friend beneath a mask, the haunted house has an exit, and the night ends at home. Religious remembrance continues too, although not everyone who enjoys Halloween shares it. Perhaps the festival’s enduring gift is that it lets people approach frightening things sideways. Darkness, death and uncertainty become stories that can be told together, with enough laughter and lamplight to make them bearable.
