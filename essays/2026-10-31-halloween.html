Major occasion · Saturday 31 October 2026

Halloween — making friends with the dark

Halloween is the evening when the familiar world puts on a strange face. A pumpkin grins beside a front door; children become witches, skeletons or whatever character has captured their imagination; an ordinary street briefly turns into a small theatre. In Britain today it can feel like a thoroughly modern, commercial celebration. Yet its mixture of play, hospitality, unease and remembrance has a much longer history, rooted in these islands as well as reshaped across the Atlantic.

Why 31 October

Its date is fixed: 31 October, which fell on a Saturday in 2026. The name gives the clearest explanation. Halloween is a Scots-flavoured shortening of All Hallows’ Eve, the evening before All Hallows’ Day, better known as All Saints’ Day, on 1 November. A “hallow” is a saint or holy person, and “e’en” is the Scots contraction of “even”, meaning evening. All Souls’ Day follows on 2 November, traditionally a day of prayer for the dead. Together these three days, sometimes called Allhallowtide, made the turn from October to November a season in which the living thought especially about the dead.

The Church did not always remember all the saints on 1 November. Early Christians honoured the martyrs collectively on various dates, often in spring; in 609 Pope Boniface IV dedicated the Pantheon in Rome to Mary and the martyrs on 13 May. By around 800, churches in Ireland and Northumbria were keeping a feast of all saints on 1 November, and in 835 that date was adopted across the Frankish empire with the backing of Pope Gregory IV. Some historians think Irish or Northumbrian churchmen at Charlemagne’s court helped bring the date into wider use.

Samhain

Behind and alongside that Christian calendar stands Samhain, the Gaelic festival marking the end of the harvest and the beginning of winter, the “darker half” of the year. Because the Celtic day was reckoned from sunset, it began on the evening of 31 October. Samhain appears in the earliest Irish literature, from around the ninth century, where it is a time of great gatherings and feasts and a moment when the burial mounds stood open as doorways to the Otherworld. In later folk tradition it was when cattle came down from summer pastures, bonfires were lit, and the aos sí, the spirits or fairies, were thought to be especially close. The Irish name for Halloween is still Oíche Shamhna, “Samhain night”.

That connection helps explain why Halloween feels poised between warmth and exposure, plenty and scarcity, the household and whatever waits beyond it. But it is worth resisting the neat claim that every modern Halloween custom is an unchanged survival from ancient Celtic religion. Evidence for early practice is thin, scholars still argue about how much Samhain and the Christian feasts shaped each other, and the popular idea of Samhain as the “Celtic New Year” comes from nineteenth-century writers and is disputed. Halloween has several ancestors, not one birth certificate.

Souling, guising and the first trick-or-treaters

One of its most touching predecessors is souling. From at least the fifteenth century, in parts of England, Wales and continental Europe, poor people, often children, went from door to door at Allhallowtide asking for soul cakes, small spiced cakes given in return for prayers for the household’s dead. English Heritage describes this as a possible precursor to trick-or-treating. The relationship is suggestive rather than a simple, unbroken line, but the resemblance is lovely: a stranger arrives at the door, a little performance or promise is offered, and something sweet changes hands.

Disguise adds another strand. Guising, especially associated with Scotland and Ireland, made visiting in costume a neighbourhood performance rather than merely a demand for sweets: the guisers were expected to sing, recite or tell a joke for their reward. A writer in Ayr in 1890 described small boys running about in their “fause-faces”, false faces, carrying “bits o’ turnip lanthrons”. The phrase “trick or treat” itself is North American; the earliest known uses come from Canada around 1917 to 1924, and it spread through the United States in the following decades. It only became common at British front doors from the 1980s, helped along by films such as E.T.

Turnips before pumpkins

The jack-o’-lantern also has a British and Irish past. In the nineteenth century, people in parts of Ireland, Scotland, Wales and England hollowed out turnips or mangel-wurzels, carved grotesque faces into them and put a candle inside. An Irish folk tale explains the name: Stingy Jack, a drunkard who tricked the Devil, was refused entry to both heaven and hell, and wanders the earth with only a burning coal in a hollowed turnip to light his way. The term “jack-o’-lantern” originally meant the will-o’-the-wisp, the strange flickering light seen over marshes. In North America the much larger and softer pumpkin offered an excellent canvas, and it became the familiar emblem. Migration carried customs abroad, and American popular culture later carried their transformed versions back. Calling Halloween simply an American import misses the round trip.

Apples, nuts and fortunes

For centuries Halloween in Ireland and Scotland was the great night for divination, especially about love and death. Apples and nuts were everywhere. Young people bobbed or “dooked” for apples in tubs of water, peeled an apple in one long strip and threw the peel over their shoulder to see what initial it formed, or roasted pairs of nuts by the fire to see whether a couple would burn quietly together or jump apart. Robert Burns gathered many of these customs in his 1785 poem “Halloween”. Some survive as party games; most have faded.

Is it related to the Day of the Dead?

People often wonder whether Halloween and Mexico’s Día de Muertos, the Day of the Dead, are the same festival. They are cousins, not twins. Día de Muertos is celebrated mainly on 1 and 2 November, the same dates as All Saints’ and All Souls’, and it grew from a meeting between Spanish Catholic observance of those days and Indigenous Mesoamerican traditions of honouring the dead. Historians debate how much of today’s celebration has truly pre-Hispanic roots and how much was shaped in the twentieth century as a symbol of Mexican national identity. Families build ofrendas, home altars with photographs, candles, marigolds and the favourite food and drink of the departed, and visit and decorate graves. The tone is affectionate and often humorous rather than frightening: sugar skulls, and La Calavera Catrina, the elegant skeleton lady first drawn by the printmaker José Guadalupe Posada. UNESCO lists Mexico’s Indigenous festivity dedicated to the dead as Intangible Cultural Heritage.

So the two share a season and a Christian calendar, and both put death at the centre of a celebration. But Halloween’s roots run mainly through Ireland, Scotland and England, while the Day of the Dead is essentially Mexican and Mesoamerican, and its purpose is welcoming the dead home rather than playing with fear. In recent decades each has borrowed from the other. Mexico City’s big Day of the Dead parade, for example, was first held in 2016, after the opening scene of the James Bond film Spectre showed an imaginary one.

How it is marked today

Today Halloween ranges from quiet pumpkin carving to elaborate horror attractions, with apple games, parties and ghost stories in between. The first purpose-built haunted attraction on record opened in England, at Liphook in Hampshire, in 1915. Many churches keep All Hallows’ Eve with services and vigils, and some hold “light parties” as an alternative. Some modern Pagans observe Samhain as a religious festival in its own right. In Britain, Halloween now competes with Bonfire Night for attention, and for fireworks, just five days later.

For most participants the fright is carefully bounded: the monster is a friend beneath a mask, the haunted house has an exit, and the night ends at home. Perhaps the festival’s enduring gift is that it lets people approach frightening things sideways. Darkness, death and uncertainty become stories that can be told together, with enough laughter and lamplight to make them bearable.
