Major occasion · 20 November 2026

The Mexican Revolution: 116 years on

On 20 November Mexico remembers the beginning of its revolution in 1910. Today is the 116th anniversary of the date Francisco I. Madero chose for an uprising against the rule of Porfirio Díaz. It is an occasion of national historical importance, associated with civic ceremonies and parades. There is a calendar distinction in 2026: the anniversary is Friday 20 November, while the associated official day off was observed on Monday 16 November. A commemoration’s historical date and its working holiday do not always coincide.

The immediate background lay in the presidential contest of 1910 and the frustration of opposition to Díaz. Madero’s Plan of San Luis rejected the election results and called on Mexicans to rise at six in the evening on 20 November. The Ministry of Defence’s historical account identifies this summons as the reason the date became the revolution’s starting point. Such a date does not mean discontent suddenly appeared everywhere at once. It gives an organised political call a place in a much wider history of grievances and resistance.

Different groups brought different aims to the struggle. A government historical overview links the movement with the conditions experienced by workers and peasants under the Porfiriato. Regional leaders included Emiliano Zapata in the south and Francisco Villa and Pascual Orozco in the north. The revolution therefore contained struggles over political representation, land and social conditions, as well as the immediate effort to end Díaz’s rule. Keeping those differences visible helps explain why the fall of a president did not bring a simple or permanent settlement.

Díaz resigned in May 1911, but the conflict continued. Madero became president and was then overthrown and killed in 1913. Opposition to Victoriano Huerta brought further armed struggle, followed by deep divisions among the revolutionary forces themselves. The Constitution of 1917 was a central outcome, but it is misleading to imagine that a single document ended every conflict. Mexico’s National Archives emphasises how the revolution became not only a sequence of events but a body of symbols and memories shaping the modern state.

Public commemoration acquired its own history. Banco del Bienestar’s account describes an unofficial relay race in 1928 and the national status given to the anniversary parade tradition in 1936. Sporting and civic display became ways to connect public participation with the revolution’s memory. Today the anniversary still belongs to Mexico’s official civic calendar, which calls for the national flag to be raised. Local arrangements vary. The established tradition is worth describing without assuming that a particular historical parade route or programme has remained unchanged in 2026.

For JT, this is a good date to look beyond a familiar revolutionary portrait and ask which part of the story it represents. A demand for a fair election, a struggle for land and a later account of nationhood can all occupy the same anniversary while carrying different meanings. In the UK the occasion is chiefly encountered through Mexican history, communities and cultural life rather than a British public holiday. Reading the original call alongside later historical accounts reveals how one evening in the calendar became the shared reference point for years of upheaval and a continuing argument about justice.
