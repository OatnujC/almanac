Minor occasion · 23 November 2026

Alexander Litvinenko’s death: 20 years on

Alexander Litvinenko died in University College Hospital in London on 23 November 2006. Today marks twenty years since his death. It is a historical anniversary, not an established national day of observance. The date deserves a place in a British almanac because the killing, the investigation and his family's pursuit of answers became matters of lasting public importance. Remembering it also means keeping the person and the bereaved family visible behind an extraordinary and often sensationalised story.

Litvinenko had worked for the Russian security services and later came to Britain. His death followed a period of illness after he ingested the radioactive substance polonium-210. The UK public inquiry concluded that he received the fatal dose while drinking tea at the Millennium Hotel's Pine Bar in Mayfair on 1 November 2006. That is the inquiry's finding, not something this entry independently establishes. A careful account needs to identify who reached such conclusions and the process through which they were reached.

The inquiry's report records that Litvinenko was pronounced dead at 9.21pm UK time on 23 November. It describes how the cause of his illness became clear only shortly before his death, when specialist tests identified polonium contamination. That detail explains some of the case's disturbing character. Doctors were treating a severely ill patient while confronting an exceptionally unusual cause, and the significance of the evidence emerged alongside the final hours of his life. The anniversary falls on the death date, rather than the earlier poisoning date.

The subsequent legal history is central to what the case means. The statutory public inquiry reported in January 2016. The Home Secretary's statement to Parliament set out its conclusions, including the finding that the operation was probably approved by Nikolai Patrushev and Vladimir Putin. The word “probably” is part of that conclusion and should remain part of any faithful summary. An inquiry's assessment, a criminal conviction and an international court judgment are different things; an almanac should not blur them into a single undifferentiated verdict.

In 2021 the European Court of Human Rights ruled in Carter v. Russia, the case brought by Litvinenko's widow, Marina Carter, also known as Marina Litvinenko. The court found Russia responsible for his killing and also addressed the failure to conduct an effective investigation. Its press release provides a concise account of the decision. That judgment supplies another reason to remember the case through official documents as well as newspaper memories: it shows how a family's search for accountability continued over many years and across more than one legal system.

In the UK, the anniversary can be marked through reading the inquiry's conclusions and remembering the consequences of political violence for an individual family. Internationally, the European court's judgment places the case within the protection of the right to life. No particular twentieth-anniversary ceremony is asserted here, because a round anniversary does not establish that a public event has been organised. The act of remembrance available to any reader is to attend to the evidence and to the human cost with equal seriousness.

Twenty years creates enough distance for an event to become a shorthand reference. That shorthand can conceal the patient in a hospital, the family waiting for an explanation and the sustained work needed to establish what happened. Returning to the date restores some of that scale. On 23 November, the most useful remembrance is a precise one: a man died in London, official investigations reached grave findings, and the effort to obtain answers became an important part of the story that followed.
