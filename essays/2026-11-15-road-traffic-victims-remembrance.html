Major occasion · 15 November 2026

World Day of Remembrance for Road Traffic Victims

Every year on the third Sunday of November, candles are lit, names are read out and services are held in towns and cities around the world for people who have been killed or seriously injured on the roads. This is the World Day of Remembrance for Road Traffic Victims. It is a day for grief, but also for gratitude to the emergency services, and for a determination that fewer families should have to go through the same loss. It has a particular connection to Britain, because it was born here.

A mother's campaign

The story begins in south London on 27 October 1990. Mansoor Chaudhry, 26, was riding his motorcycle across a junction on a green light when a van driver crossed against a bank of red lights and ploughed into him. His mother, Brigitte Chaudhry, later told the BBC that two police officers came to her door to say her son had been killed, and then went away, leaving her with no information and no help. She learned what had happened only months later. The driver was charged with careless driving and fined £250; she said her son's death was not even mentioned in court.

Brigitte Chaudhry was struck by how differently a death on the road was treated from other violent deaths, however blameless the victim. She tracked down other bereaved families through letters to newspapers and through the Compassionate Friends, and documented their experiences. The first meeting of what became RoadPeace was held around her dining table in 1991, and the charity was launched on 8 February 1992 with the first helpline in the UK for road crash victims, which she staffed herself for years. Among its earliest campaigns was the call to say "road crash" rather than "road accident", on the grounds that most crashes are preventable.

Why the third Sunday of November

RoadPeace held the first day of remembrance in 1993. Brigitte Chaudhry has explained the thinking behind the date: she was moved by Britain's nationwide Remembrance Sunday, on the second Sunday of November, for those killed and injured in war, and felt that a day for those killed and injured in the "war on the roads" on the following Sunday would be fitting, November being the month of remembrance. She proposed it to the European Federation of Road Traffic Victims (FEVR), which adopted it in 1995 after some debate about timing. In 2026 the first Sunday of November fell on the 1st, which makes today, 15 November, the third.

From Britain to the United Nations

By 1998 the day was being observed not only in the UK but in countries including Argentina, Australia, Israel, South Africa and Trinidad. The World Health Organization added its support in 2003. On 26 October 2005, in resolution 60/5 on improving global road safety, the UN General Assembly invited all countries to observe the third Sunday in November each year as "the appropriate acknowledgment of victims of road traffic crashes and their families". Brigitte Chaudhry, who went on to serve as FEVR's president, created the day's international website, launched in 2008, and edited it until 2017. In 2015 FEVR received the Prince Michael International Road Safety Award for its part in creating and promoting the day.

The scale of the problem

The numbers behind the day are sobering. The UN's observance page cites WHO's 2018 global status report, which put annual road deaths at about 1.35 million; WHO's more recent reporting puts the figure at around 1.19 million a year, with tens of millions more injured. Road traffic injuries are the leading killer of children and young people aged 5 to 29. More than half of those killed are pedestrians, cyclists and motorcyclists, the so-called vulnerable road users, and the burden falls hardest on low- and middle-income countries. In 2020 the General Assembly proclaimed a second Decade of Action for Road Safety, 2021 to 2030, with the aim of halving road deaths and injuries by 2030.

The UN lists the day's objectives plainly: to remember all those killed and seriously injured; to acknowledge the crucial work of the emergency services; to draw attention to what it calls the generally trivial legal response to culpable road deaths and injuries; to push for better support for victims and their families; and to promote evidence-based action to prevent further deaths.

How it is marked

Observances vary enormously. In the UK, cathedrals and churches hold services of remembrance; Canterbury and Ripon cathedrals have both hosted them, and RoadPeace and local groups organise vigils and memorial events. Around a thousand people attended a service at Knock Shrine in Ireland in 2009. Israel has held candlelight parades, Australia multi-faith gatherings, Mexico theatre performances and Japan seminars. In India, groups gather to pin victims' photographs to memorial boards, light candles, hear from bereaved families and take a pledge to follow traffic rules, often alongside training in first aid during the "golden hour" after a crash and education about laws protecting Good Samaritans who stop to help.

For many families the day is simply a moment when a private loss is publicly recognised. As the UN's recent campaign put it, the aim is to turn memory into action, empathy into support and awareness into commitment, so that more people reach their destinations safely.
