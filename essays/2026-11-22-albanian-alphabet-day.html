Major occasion · 22 November 2026

Albanian Alphabet Day

An alphabet can look like a modest thing to celebrate: a row of letters, familiar enough to be taken for granted. For Albanian speakers, 22 November marks something much larger. Alphabet Day remembers the Congress of Manastir in 1908 and the effort to establish a shared basis for writing Albanian. In 2026 it is the 118th anniversary of the congress’s closing day, a celebration of language as a means of connection across borders and generations.

The congress met from 14 to 22 November in Manastir, the city now known as Bitola in North Macedonia. Its delegates addressed a problem with consequences far beyond spelling. Different writing systems made it harder to share books, teaching and public discussion. Agreement about an alphabet could support a common literary and educational life. The annual observance chooses the final date of that meeting, so it remains on 22 November regardless of the weekday.

The familiar summary is that the congress unified the Albanian alphabet. That captures its enduring importance, although the history of agreement and adoption is more involved than the image of a language being invented in one room. Albanian was already spoken and written. The achievement was a common direction for its written form, associated with the Latin-based alphabet used today. It helped make language a practical bond among communities whose political circumstances and religious backgrounds differed.

Today the date has official recognition as a public holiday in Albania. The 2026 holiday calendar lists 22 November, and because that is a Sunday the substitute day off is Monday 23 November. The anniversary and the working-day holiday should therefore be kept distinct. The Congress of Manastir is also part of Albanian cultural memory beyond Albania’s borders; the location of the original meeting is a useful reminder that the language’s history does not fit neatly within one modern state.

Contemporary observance includes attention to education, cultural heritage and the transmission of Albanian to children abroad. Albania’s foreign ministry has linked Alphabet Day with support for teachers in the diaspora, including learning materials, training and digital resources. Its earlier records also describe official visits to the Alphabet Museum in Manastir. These are concrete expressions of what an alphabet anniversary can mean: keeping a language available for everyday use, rather than treating it only as a ceremonial emblem.

In the UK, the date is relevant especially to Albanian-speaking families, teachers and cultural communities, rather than as a general British holiday. There is no need to invent a nationwide programme to appreciate its meaning. Reading with a child, learning how a name is pronounced, or asking someone about the language spoken at home can be a small but serious act of recognition. For people living between languages, the ability to write to relatives and read family stories is part of belonging.

Alphabet Day also offers a wider thought about public life. A shared writing system makes room for disagreement as well as unity: it allows people to argue, teach, publish and imagine together. The achievement remembered today is therefore more durable than any single slogan about national identity. It concerns the ordinary tools through which a community continues to speak for itself. A meeting that ended in November 1908 still reaches into classrooms, bookshelves and messages sent home today.
