Major occasion · Wednesday 11 November 2026

Polish National Independence Day

For most of Europe, 11 November is a day of mourning. For Poland it is a day of celebration. Narodowe Święto Niepodległości, National Independence Day, marks the day in 1918 when Poland began to exist again as an independent state after 123 years in which it had disappeared from the map altogether. It is a public holiday, and this year is the 108th anniversary.

A country partitioned

At the end of the eighteenth century the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, once one of the largest states in Europe, was carved up by its three powerful neighbours: Russia, Prussia and Austria. They took slices in 1772 and 1793, and with the third partition in 1795 Poland ceased to exist as a country. For the next 123 years Poles lived as subjects of three different empires, under pressure to become Russian or German.

The Polish government's own account describes how five generations kept the national spirit alive through uprisings, culture and quiet resistance. Risings in 1830 and 1863 were crushed, but language, literature, the church and family life kept the idea of Poland going.

What happened in November 1918

The First World War broke all three partitioning empires at once. Russia was in revolution, and by November 1918 Austria-Hungary had collapsed and Germany was on the verge of defeat. On 10 November, Józef Piłsudski, the soldier and independence leader who had been held by the Germans in the fortress at Magdeburg, arrived back in Warsaw. The next day, 11 November, the Regency Council, the governing body of the German-sponsored Kingdom of Poland, handed him command of the Polish army. On 14 November the Council dissolved itself and passed him all civil authority too, and soon afterwards he notified the world that an independent Polish republic had been reborn.

No single event can really be pointed to as the moment Poland came back to life. As the Polish parliament's own history notes, the date was a matter of debate between the wars, and it was chosen partly because it coincided with the Armistice at Compiègne that ended the war on the same day. It also sits alongside a cast of founding figures, including Roman Dmowski, Ignacy Jan Paderewski, Józef Haller, Ignacy Daszyński, Wincenty Witos and Wojciech Korfanty, statesmen from very different political camps who shared the goal of rebuilding the country.

A holiday that was banned

Perhaps surprisingly, 11 November only became an official state holiday by law in April 1937, so the independent Polish state formally celebrated it just twice, in 1937 and 1938, before the Second World War. Before that, it was marked mainly as a military occasion, with Piłsudski reviewing troops in Warsaw.

During the war the German and Soviet occupiers banned Polish national holidays. Afterwards, the communist government replaced it with its own holiday on 22 July, the anniversary of the Soviet-backed Polish Committee of National Liberation's 1944 manifesto, and abolished Independence Day in 1945. For four decades, 11 November was kept unofficially: people gathered for church services, and over time these were followed by marches and demonstrations for freedom. Poland's Institute of National Remembrance describes how the communist authorities, in their final months in February 1989 during the Round Table talks with the opposition, restored the holiday, giving back a date that mattered deeply to their opponents.

How it is marked today

Today the main state ceremony takes place in Warsaw at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier on Piłsudski Square, with the President, a changing of the guard and military honours. Flags fly from homes and balconies across the country, and many families take part in runs, concerts, picnics and patriotic singing events. Children often learn about the day at school in the days before.

In Warsaw, a large Independence March is also held each year. It is organised by nationalist groups and has at times been controversial, and other organisations hold their own separate, more family-oriented celebrations.

A delicious coincidence in Poznań

11 November is also St Martin's Day, and in Poznań, in western Poland, the two celebrations happily overlap. The city is famous for its rogal świętomarciński, a large crescent-shaped pastry filled with white poppy seed, nuts and dried fruit, and eaten in huge quantities on this day, together with a St Martin's parade. So for many Poles the day of independence is marked with flags, music and a very sweet croissant.
