Major occasion · Monday 9 November 2026

Kristallnacht — remembering the Night of Broken Glass

Kristallnacht, the “Night of Broken Glass”, was a violent pogrom carried out across Nazi Germany on 9 and 10 November 1938. By then “Germany” included Austria, annexed in March of that year, and the Sudetenland, taken from Czechoslovakia in the autumn. In a single night and day, synagogues were set alight, Jewish-owned shops were smashed and looted, homes were broken into and wrecked, and Jewish men, women and children were humiliated, beaten and killed. Tens of thousands of Jewish men were then sent to concentration camps simply because they were Jewish.

This is not an occasion to celebrate. It is a day of remembrance, and it is kept in Germany, Austria and Jewish communities around the world with vigils, readings of names and services at synagogues and memorials. What follows tries to set out what happened carefully and plainly, drawing mainly on the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum’s account.

Why 9 November

The date is the anniversary of the night itself. It is fixed in the ordinary calendar, so it falls on 9 November every year; in 2026 that is a Monday. It also sits in a cluster of German anniversaries. On 9 November 1918 the Kaiser’s abdication was announced and a republic proclaimed; on 9 November 1923 Hitler attempted his failed coup in Munich, the Beer Hall Putsch; and on 9 November 1989 the Berlin Wall opened. The overlap is no coincidence in one respect: in 1938 Nazi leaders were gathered in Munich precisely to commemorate the 1923 putsch, and that gathering became the place from which the pogrom was launched. It is one reason Germany has never made 9 November a national holiday, despite the joy of 1989.

The year before the violence

1938 was a turning point. Nazi Germany expanded its territory, annexing Austria in March and the Sudetenland in September and October, and it stepped up its campaign to drive Jews out of economic and public life. Five years of boycotts, propaganda, dismissals and discriminatory laws, including the Nuremberg Laws of 1935, had already pushed many Jews to try to leave. Leaving was hard. In July 1938 delegates from more than thirty countries met at Évian in France to discuss the refugee crisis, and almost none agreed to take in more people. The Zionist leader Chaim Weizmann had written two years earlier that the world seemed divided into places where Jews could not live and places they could not enter.

At the end of October 1938 the regime turned on Jews in Germany who held Polish citizenship. On 27 to 29 October, German authorities rounded up and deported more than 17,000 people, many of them born in Germany, and dumped them at the Polish border. Thousands were stranded for days in the border town of Zbąszyń in miserable conditions. This “Polish Action” was the first mass deportation of Jews from Nazi Germany.

The shooting in Paris

Among those deported from Hanover were Zyndel and Ryfka Grynszpan, who had moved to Germany in 1911. Their 17-year-old son Herschel was living in Paris. After learning what had happened to his family, he went to the German Embassy on the morning of 7 November and shot a junior diplomat, Ernst vom Rath. The regime seized on the attack at once. From 7 November, Propaganda Minister Joseph Goebbels directed the press to blame all Jews for the shooting, and in some places local Nazis began attacking synagogues and shops before the main pogrom started.

Vom Rath died of his wounds on the afternoon of 9 November. That evening Hitler, Goebbels and other party leaders were in Munich for the putsch anniversary. According to the Holocaust Memorial Museum, Hitler and Goebbels decided to unleash a nationwide riot, and at around 9.30 or 10pm Goebbels gave an inflammatory antisemitic speech to the assembled party officials, who then telephoned their home districts with instructions. Shortly before midnight the Gestapo chief Heinrich Müller ordered the arrest of 20,000 to 30,000 Jewish men. At 1.20am, Reinhard Heydrich told police forces not to interfere with what he called “demonstrations”, only to protect non-Jewish property, stop looting, seize synagogue archives and arrest young, healthy and wealthy Jewish men.

The night and the day

The violence was presented as a spontaneous outburst of popular anger. It was nothing of the kind: it was state-sponsored arson, vandalism and terror. Members of the SA, the SS and the Hitler Youth carried it out, sometimes in uniform and sometimes in plain clothes, and many ordinary Germans joined in. In small towns, attackers and victims often knew each other.

More than 1,400 synagogues and other Jewish religious buildings were destroyed, most of them burned. Fire brigades stood by with orders only to stop the flames spreading to neighbouring buildings. Torah scrolls were torn, burned or thrown into rivers, and in some places rabbis were forced to watch. Thousands of Jewish-owned shops had their windows shattered and their stock destroyed; the glass glittering on the pavements gave the night its later name. Homes were invaded in the middle of the night, furniture thrown from windows, books ripped and bedding slashed until streets were covered in feathers. Orphanages, old people’s homes and hospitals were attacked. Jews were forced to perform humiliating exercises, to dance, to sing Nazi songs, while crowds jeered.

The early official count was 91 Jews killed. Historians now know the toll was far higher: the Holocaust Memorial Museum says that hundreds died during the pogrom and its aftermath, through murder, wounds, shock and suicide.

The camps, the fine and the laws

From the early hours of 10 November police, helped by the SS and SA, arrested Jewish men across the country and often marched them through the streets. About 26,000 were sent to Dachau, Buchenwald and Sachsenhausen, the first time the regime imprisoned Jews on a mass scale purely for being Jewish. Conditions were brutal and hundreds died. Most were released after some weeks, often only after signing away businesses or proving they had plans to emigrate; wives and mothers frequently took great risks to secure their release.

The riot was called off at 4pm on 10 November, though violence continued in places. Then came the bill. At a meeting on 12 November, Hermann Göring announced that Jews would pay a collective “atonement” fine of one billion Reichsmarks, that they would have to pay for repairing the damage done to them, and that their insurance payments would be confiscated by the state. In the following weeks a flood of decrees barred Jews from running shops, attending public schools and receiving most welfare, and set out the forced transfer of Jewish businesses to non-Jewish owners. In effect, the victims were made to pay for their own persecution, and Jewish life in Germany was being dismantled by law.

How the world responded

The pogrom was front-page news. In the United States, President Franklin D. Roosevelt condemned it and recalled his ambassador from Berlin. In Britain, public opinion shifted enough for the government to waive visa requirements for refugee children under 17. Jewish and non-Jewish aid groups organised the Kindertransport: the first group of about 200 children landed at Harwich on 2 December 1938, and by the outbreak of war in September 1939 nearly 10,000 children, most of them Jewish, had come to the UK. Their parents were not allowed to come with them, and many of the children never saw them again.

For Germany’s Jews the message was unmistakable. Kristallnacht showed that there was no safe place left, not even their own homes, and many who had hoped to wait out the regime decided they had to leave if they could. Historians see the pogrom as a watershed on the road from persecution to genocide.

A name worth thinking about

“Kristallnacht”, or “Reichskristallnacht”, came into popular use in Germany around the time. Many people now find it uncomfortable, because “crystal night” sounds almost pretty and puts broken glass in the foreground rather than the arson, the beatings and the deaths. In German the events are now usually called the Novemberpogrome (November pogroms) or Reichspogromnacht. In English, “Kristallnacht” remains the most familiar name, and is often used alongside “November Pogrom”, as this page does, to make sure the word points to the violence and not just the glass.

How it is remembered today

In Germany the anniversary is marked with ceremonies in parliaments and town halls, services in synagogues, and gatherings at the sites of synagogues that were destroyed. Readings of names, vigils and school projects are common. In many towns, volunteers use the anniversary to clean the small brass Stolpersteine, or “stumbling stones”, set into pavements outside the last freely chosen homes of people murdered by the Nazis. Jewish communities elsewhere, including in Britain, hold services and talks, often with the involvement of the shrinking number of survivors and their families. In the UK, the Kindertransport story is part of the remembrance, a reminder that a decision to open a door saved thousands of lives, and that so many other doors stayed shut.

Perhaps the most useful thing the anniversary asks is attention to how it happened: years of propaganda and exclusion beforehand, a pretext seized upon, neighbours who joined in, others who looked away, a few who helped. Remembering that sequence carefully is one way of honouring the people whose homes, livelihoods and lives were destroyed.
