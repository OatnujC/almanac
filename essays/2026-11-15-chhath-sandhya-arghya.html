Major occasion · 15 November 2026

Chhath Puja: Sandhya Arghya

This evening, as the sun sinks over the Ganges plain, one of the most remarkable sights of the Hindu year unfolds. On riverbanks, around village ponds and beside specially dug pools in city parks, crowds of families gather in their best clothes. The women and men who have kept the Chhath vow wade into the water up to their waists, holding up flat bamboo winnowing trays heaped with fruit, sugarcane and sweets, and offer them, along with water, to the setting sun. This is Sandhya Arghya, the evening offering, the third and in many ways the central day of Chhath Puja.

What Chhath is

Chhath is an ancient festival native to eastern India and the Mithila region of Nepal, celebrated above all in Bihar, Jharkhand and eastern Uttar Pradesh, and in several provinces of southern Nepal. It is devoted to Surya, the Sun god, thanked as the source of all life on earth, and to Chhathi Maiya, a mother goddess described as Surya's sister and the sixth form of Prakriti (nature), who is believed to protect children and grant them long and healthy lives. Unusually for a major Hindu festival, it has no temple image at its heart: the object of devotion is the Sun itself, visible to everyone.

The festival lasts four days and three nights. It begins with Nahay Khay, a ritual bath and a simple pure meal; continues with Kharna, a day of fasting broken in the evening with a jaggery rice pudding; reaches its climax today with the offering to the setting sun; and ends at dawn tomorrow with Usha Arghya, the offering to the rising sun, after which the long fast is finally broken.

Why it falls today

Chhath follows the Hindu lunisolar calendar. Its name comes from the word for "six", because the main observance falls on Shashthi, the sixth day of the bright fortnight of the month of Kartik, six days after Diwali. Diwali fell on 8 November this year, so Chhath runs from Friday 13 to Monday 16 November 2026. Drik Panchang and Indian newspapers following the New Delhi almanac place Sandhya Arghya on Sunday 15 November, with the Shashthi tithi running from 11:23 pm (India time) on 14 November until 2:00 am on 16 November. The moment of offering is tied to local sunset, so it shifts from city to city: the Economic Times lists sunset at about 5:01 pm in Patna (roughly 11:31 am in the UK), 4:53 pm in Kolkata and 5:28 pm in Delhi. There is also a smaller spring Chhath, Chaiti Chhath, in the month of Chaitra, but the Kartik festival is by far the larger.

What happens on the day

For the person keeping the vow, known as a parvaitin or vratin, today falls in the middle of a fast of around 36 hours without food or even water, which began after the Kharna meal last night. The daylight hours are spent at home preparing the offerings. The most famous is thekua, a crisp, deep-fried biscuit of wheat flour, jaggery and ghee. Alongside it go rice laddus, kasar sweets, coconuts, bananas, sweet limes and whole stalks of sugarcane, arranged in bamboo baskets. Everything is vegetarian and cooked without salt, onion or garlic, and great care is taken over purity and cleanliness: houses, lanes and the paths to the water are swept and washed beforehand.

In the late afternoon the whole household walks in procession to the ghat, the steps or bank of a river or pond, often with the men carrying the heavy baskets on their heads. Some devotees make the journey by repeatedly prostrating themselves along the road. At the water, the vratins step in and, as the sun touches the horizon, raise the trays and pour water towards it. Chhathi Maiya is worshipped with the offerings, and the air fills with Chhath songs in Bhojpuri, Maithili and Magahi, a body of folk music so tied to the festival that hearing it on the radio tells people across north India that Chhath has arrived. Onlookers and neighbours come to help and to receive the vratins' blessings.

The night is not over when people return home. In many families the ritual of kosi bharai follows: five to seven sugarcane stalks are tied together into a little canopy, beneath which a dozen or more clay lamps are lit and thekua and fruit are offered. Before dawn the family heads back to the water to wait, standing in the cold, for the first sliver of the rising sun.

Stories and history

Chhath's origins are told in several ways. One tradition says that Sita performed the Surya Shashthi vow after she and Rama returned to Ayodhya; in Munger, a temple on a boulder in the middle of the Ganges, known as Sitacharan, is revered as marking her footsteps. Another links the festival to the Mahabharata: to Kunti, mother of the Pandavas, and to her son Karna, the child of the Sun god, and to Draupadi, said to have kept the vow for the Pandavas' victory. There is also the story of King Priyavrata, whose stillborn son was brought back to life by the goddess Shashthi, the reason Chhathi Maiya is honoured as a guardian of children. Some Hindi sources connect the spread of the festival with Varanasi and the medieval Gahadavala dynasty. Historians generally see Chhath as a survival of very old sun worship blended with the folk traditions of the eastern Gangetic plain.

Chhath today

For families from Bihar and eastern Uttar Pradesh, Chhath is the festival that brings everyone home, and in cities far from the Ganges people make do with temporary ponds, tanks and riverbanks; Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata and Kathmandu all host large gatherings. In some families, once someone has started keeping the vow they are expected to continue every year and pass it on to the next generation, pausing only after a death in the family. The diaspora carries it further still, with celebrations reported in the United States, Mauritius, Singapore, the Gulf and the United Kingdom.

What strikes many observers is the festival's simplicity and equality: everyone, rich or poor, brings much the same basket of seasonal produce to the same water, and gives thanks to the same sun. Tonight that sun sets on the third day; tomorrow at dawn, Chhath ends where it began, with light.
