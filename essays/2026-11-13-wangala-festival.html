Minor occasion · Friday 13 November 2026

Wangala, the Hundred Drums Festival

Today is a public holiday in the Indian state of Meghalaya for Wangala, the great harvest festival of the Garo people. It is often called the Hundred Drums Festival, and this year it is aiming for even more: the state government says the main celebrations at the Garo Heritage Village run from 11 to 14 November, with a performance featuring 1,000 drums. Schools across Meghalaya are closed for the day, which, falling on a Friday, gives children a long weekend.

The Garo are an Indigenous community who live mainly in the Garo Hills of western Meghalaya, with communities also in Assam, Nagaland and the Greater Mymensingh region of Bangladesh. They have their own language and a matrilineal society, in which family lines and property pass through women. Wangala is the most important event in their traditional year.

Why it falls now

Wangala is a post-harvest festival. Once the year’s crops are in, the Garo give thanks to Misi Saljong, the sun god, for blessing them with a good harvest. It also marks the end of the farming season and the arrival of winter, and is a time for families and villages to come together. Traditionally there is no single date: Wangala is held between September and December, with different villages choosing their own days. The date that appears in the official calendar, 13 November in 2026, is the general holiday set by the Meghalaya government for the state-level celebrations.

The rituals

In its traditional form, Wangala lasts two or three days, sometimes as long as a week, and is often celebrated jointly by two or three neighbouring villages. On the first day the village priest, known as the Kamal, carries out the rituals in the house of the Nokma, the village chief. They are called Rugala, the pouring of rice beer, and Cha·chat So·a, the burning of incense, offerings made in thanks for the harvest.

The best-known part comes on the final day: Dama Gogata, the dance with drums. Lines of men and women in colourful clothes and feathered headdresses dance to the beat of long, oval-shaped drums called dama, together with flutes and brass instruments. Other performances include Katta Doka, a kind of chanted, rhythmic storytelling, and songs that describe the festival itself.

Old faith and new festival

Most Garo today are Christian, and the old religion, known as Songsarek, survives in a smaller number of villages. It is in remote Songsarek villages such as Sadolpara in West Garo Hills that Wangala is said to be kept most closely to its traditional form. Elsewhere the festival has become a celebration of Garo culture and identity shared by people of all faiths.

The modern Hundred Drums Wangala Festival began on 6 and 7 December 1976 at Asanang, about 18 kilometres from Tura, the main town of the Garo Hills, and it has been held every year since. It is now a state-sponsored event with dance competitions and prize money, drawing troupes from across the Garo Hills and beyond, including Garo communities in Bangladesh, Karbi Anglong and Tripura, along with tourists from across India and abroad. Students in Tura learn the dance at school, and in towns a one-day version of the festival helps younger people stay connected to their roots.

For a visitor, the sound is what stays with you: dozens, now perhaps a thousand, long drums beating together across the hills, as a people who have farmed this land for generations say thank you for another year’s harvest.
