Minor occasion · 17 November 2026

Homemade Bread Day

Not every date in the almanac needs a war, a treaty or a revolution behind it. Today is also Homemade Bread Day, a modest American observance with one simple request: make a loaf yourself.

Where it came from

The day is usually credited to the National Homemade Bread Committee of Ann Arbor, Michigan, which set it up to encourage families to make bread at home. Researchers who track these days, such as Checkiday and Edible Events, date the earliest print mention to November 1983, and it has been kept on 17 November ever since the early 1980s. There is no deep reason for the date that anyone has recorded. It simply falls in the run-up to American Thanksgiving, when kitchens are warm and baking is on people’s minds, and in Britain it lands in the dark, damp weeks when a warm oven is at its most welcome. It is not tied to any brand or bakery chain, which is why it earns a place here rather than being skipped as a sales promotion.

Why homemade bread feels special

Bread is one of humanity’s oldest prepared foods, and for most of history nearly all of it was made at home or in a local bakehouse. A loaf needs only flour, water, salt and something to make it rise: either baker’s yeast or a sourdough starter, a living culture of wild yeasts and bacteria that bakers keep going for years, sometimes decades. The slowness is part of the appeal. Yeast dough needs kneading, then time to rise, then shaping and a second rise, and the whole process rewards patience more than skill.

Industrial baking changed that. In 1928 Otto Rohwedder’s slicing machine began producing pre-sliced loaves in Chillicothe, Missouri, giving the English language ‘the best thing since sliced bread’. In Britain, the Chorleywood bread process, developed in 1961 by the British Baking Industries Research Association at Chorleywood in Hertfordshire, used intense mechanical mixing to make dough rise far faster, and it went on to make most of the bread sold in the UK. It made bread cheap and soft and long-lasting, and also prompted a long backlash from people who missed the crust and flavour of slowly made loaves. That backlash, in the form of artisan bakeries and home baking, is the spirit of today.

How people mark it

There are no parades. People bake: a simple white loaf, a soda bread that needs no yeast or rising at all, a sourdough if they have a starter on the go, or quick breads such as banana bread. Food writers and libraries share recipes, and social media fills with photos under #HomemadeBreadDay. A nice tradition, where people keep it, is to give a loaf away to a neighbour or friend.

If you fancy joining in, Irish soda bread is the gentlest start: flour, bicarbonate of soda, salt and buttermilk, mixed, shaped and baked in under an hour. Whatever you make, the house will smell wonderful, and that, more or less, is the whole point of the day.
