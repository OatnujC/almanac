Minor occasion · 15 November 2026

King's Feast (Belgium)

Today Belgium celebrates the King's Feast: Fête du Roi in French, Feest van de Koning or Koningsfeest in Dutch, and Festtag des Königs in German. It is one of the more unusual royal occasions in Europe. It is not a public holiday for most people, it honours the King without being his birthday or the anniversary of his accession, and, by long custom, the King and Queen themselves do not appear at its best-known ceremony.

Why 15 November

The feast was created in 1866, under King Leopold II, a year after the death of Belgium's first king, Leopold I, and 35 years after independence. The idea, as Belgian broadcaster RTBF explains, was to create an occasion that would rally the young country around its king and its nation. The date was chosen because 15 November did double duty. In the German liturgical calendar it is the feast of Saint Leopold, the twelfth-century margrave of Austria and a patron saint of the House of Austria, the family to which the Belgian royal house traces its descent. In the general Roman calendar it is the feast of Saint Albert the Great. Between them, Leopold and Albert cover most of Belgium's kings: Leopold I, Leopold II and Leopold III, and Albert I and Albert II.

The date did not settle down immediately. RTBF counts six changes of date or name between 1910 and 1951. Under Albert I the feast moved to 24 November, then the feast of Saint Albert in the Belgian calendar; after the death of the queen mother on that very day in 1912, it moved again to 21 November. When Leopold III came to the throne in 1934 it returned to 15 November. During and after the Second World War, under the regency of Prince Charles, it was renamed the "Feast of the Dynasty", a name that caused lasting confusion; a government circular in 1953 pointed out that it was not the official title. When King Baudouin acceded in 1951 he fixed it as the King's Feast on 15 November, and his successors, Albert II and the present King Philippe, have kept it there.

A Te Deum without the King

The day opens with a Te Deum, the ancient Christian hymn of thanksgiving, sung in the Cathedral of St Michael and St Gudula in Brussels and in churches in other major cities. This year, according to the Brussels archdiocese, it takes place at 10am in the presence of Princess Astrid and Prince Lorenz, Prince Laurent and Princess Claire, along with civil and military authorities, diplomats and representatives of religious and philosophical communities. For the first time, eleven young people from different traditions, Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Protestant, Jewish, Muslim, freethinking, Buddhist and Hindu, will each speak a wish or prayer for peace as a gift from Belgium's youth to the King. The service ends, as usual, with the European anthem and the Brabançonne, the national anthem, and the royal family meets the public on the cathedral forecourt afterwards.

The King and Queen, though, stay away. RTBF calls it the peculiarity of the day: the sovereign is celebrated in his absence, and how he spends the morning is kept discreet.

The civil ceremony

Since 2001 a second, non-religious ceremony has been added. Members of the Chamber and the Senate gather at the Palace of the Nation, the home of the Belgian Federal Parliament, in the presence of members of the royal family, and the event honours people who have distinguished themselves through service to the country, often volunteers. The pairing of a religious service and a civil ceremony reflects modern Belgium, a country where Catholic tradition and secular institutions sit side by side.

A holiday for some

For most Belgians the King's Feast is an ordinary working day. Federal public services close and federal civil servants get the day off, but schools stay open, an arrangement that dates from administrative reforms in the second half of the twentieth century. This year, falling on a Sunday, it passes without any question of time off at all.

The German-speaking Community's day

There is one more layer. 15 November is also the official feast day of the German-speaking Community of Belgium, the small community of around 80,000 people in the east of the country around Eupen and Sankt Vith, whose territory became Belgian after the First World War. The Community gained cultural autonomy in 1983 and fixed its feast day by decree in 1990, choosing the King's Feast to underline its attachment to Belgium and the monarchy. Its own civil servants have the day off, and the East Cantons hold official ceremonies, concerts and cultural events. So in Eupen today, the King's Feast doubles as a celebration of a small community's language and identity, a fitting reminder of how many layers there are to the little kingdom at the crossroads of Europe.
