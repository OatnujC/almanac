Vertières Day: Haiti’s Day of Army and Victory

Today is Vertières Day in Haiti, officially the Day of Army and Victory, and the 223rd anniversary of the battle that ended French rule on the island and opened the way to independence. It is a national public holiday. On 18 November 1803, at Vertières, just south of Cap-Français, now Cap-Haïtien, the Indigenous Army led by Jean-Jacques Dessalines defeated French forces under General Donatien de Rochambeau. Less than two months later, on 1 January 1804, Dessalines proclaimed the independence of Haiti, the first Black republic of the modern world and the only state born of a successful revolt by enslaved people.

The last battle of a long revolution

The Haitian Revolution had been under way since 1791, when enslaved people rose up in the French colony of Saint-Domingue, then the most profitable sugar colony in the Caribbean. Toussaint Louverture emerged as its greatest general and, for a time, its governor. In 1802 Napoleon sent a large expedition to restore French control. Toussaint was arrested by a ruse and deported to France, where he died in prison. A line attributed to him, as he was taken away, has been repeated ever since: that in overthrowing him the French had only cut down the trunk of the tree of Black liberty in Saint-Domingue, and that it would spring back from the roots, for they were numerous and deep.

The roots did spring back. Jean-Jacques Dessalines, who had been one of Toussaint’s senior commanders, rose against the French in late 1802, correctly fearing that the expedition intended to restore slavery. By the autumn of 1803 the rebel army had taken most of the colony. The French still held a few positions, including Cap-Français, where Rochambeau, the expedition’s commander, was bottled up with the remains of his force.

18 November 1803

The battle was fought on the approaches to the city, under the guns of the French fort at Vertières. Dessalines’s officers included François Capois, remembered in Haiti as Capois-la-Mort, Capois the Death. The most famous episode of the day, told and retold in Haitian tradition, belongs to him. According to that account, Capois led his troops up a ravine through heavy fire; his horse was shot from under him; he got to his feet, drew his sword and ran on, shouting “Forward! Forward!” The story continues that Rochambeau, watching from the ramparts, ordered a sudden ceasefire, and that a French staff officer rode out to salute the general who had covered himself with such glory, before the fighting resumed. Whether every detail can be documented to a modern historian’s satisfaction, the episode has been part of how Haiti remembers the battle for generations, and Capois remains one of the heroes of the day.

A storm broke over the field. Under cover of the weather, Rochambeau pulled back, recognising that the position, and French rule on the island, were lost. The next day he sent an officer to negotiate. The terms allowed the remaining French troops a short period to embark and leave. The military struggle that had begun twelve years earlier was, in essence, over.

What the victory made possible

On 1 January 1804, at Gonaïves, Dessalines declared Haiti independent. The name itself was a restoration: “Ayiti” was a Taíno word for the island, taken up as a way of rejecting the colonial name Saint-Domingue. The new state was the second independent country in the Americas after the United States, and the first to abolish slavery completely. Its existence alarmed slaveholding powers, and diplomatic recognition was slow and costly. France long refused to accept what it had lost in battle, and later demanded a heavy indemnity as the price of recognition, a burden that shaped Haitian life for generations.

None of that erases what happened at Vertières. A colonial army sent to restore slavery was beaten by an army of people who had been enslaved, or whose parents had been, and by officers who had learned their trade in the revolution itself. The date became Haiti’s Day of Victory, and later the Day of Army and Victory, honouring both the battle and the armed forces that claim descent from Dessalines’s army.

How it is marked

Vertières Day is kept with military ceremony: reviews of the troops, wreaths, and speeches at monuments to the battle and to the founders of the state. This year, on the 223rd anniversary, a solemn ceremony was held at the National Palace in Port-au-Prince in tribute to the heroes of independence. The prime minister reviewed the Armed Forces, laid a wreath at the MUPANAH national museum, and the day was marked with the honours and parades that have become customary on 18 November. In Cap-Haïtien, close to the battlefield itself, the anniversary is felt with particular force.

For Haitians, and for anyone interested in the history of freedom, the day is larger than a national holiday. It remembers the moment when the most profitable slave colony in the Americas was lost to the empire that had held it, and when a new country, named in an Indigenous language, was about to be declared by people who had refused to be returned to bondage.
