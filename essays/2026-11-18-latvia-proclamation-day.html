Major occasion · 18 November 2026

Proclamation Day of the Republic of Latvia

Today is the Proclamation Day of the Republic of Latvia, the country’s national day, and the 108th anniversary of the meeting at which a new state was declared in a theatre in Riga. It is a public holiday across Latvia. People pin red-white-red ribbons to their coats, set candles in their windows, and, in the capital, turn out for a military parade, a torchlight procession and an evening concert in front of the Freedom Monument.

The day in 1918

The First World War had just ended. The Latvian lands had been part of the Russian Empire, then a battlefield, and by late 1918 Riga was still under German occupation, even as the German Empire itself was collapsing. Latvian politicians had already begun to organise. On 17 November they formed the People’s Council of Latvia, or Tautas padome, a body that claimed to speak for the country until a proper constituent assembly could be elected. Official English sometimes calls it the Latvian National Council.

On 18 November the Council gathered at what is now the Latvian National Theatre. In a solemn meeting it proclaimed Latvia an independent, democratic republic, united within its ethnographic borders of Kurzeme, Vidzeme and Latgale, and it named a provisional government led by Kārlis Ulmanis. The Council called on citizens to keep the peace and to support that government. It was a bold claim, made in a city that was not yet securely theirs.

A state that had to be defended

Independence on paper was one thing; holding the country was another. The new republic immediately found itself in a war of independence, fighting both Bolshevik forces and German-aligned troops, including the West Russian Volunteer Army of Pavel Bermondt-Avalov. That struggle is remembered on Lāčplēsis Day, 11 November, which marks the 1919 liberation of Riga and falls a week before Proclamation Day, so the two dates are kept as a pair in what Latvians now call Patriotic Month. The war ended with a peace treaty signed with Soviet Russia on 11 August 1920, in which Moscow recognised Latvia’s independence and sovereignty. De jure recognition by Britain, France, Italy, Belgium and Japan followed on 26 January 1921.

The first independence lasted barely a generation. In June 1940, under the shadow of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, the Red Army occupied Latvia and the country was annexed by the Soviet Union. Nazi Germany occupied Latvia from 1941 to 1945, after which Soviet rule returned and remained until the end of the Cold War. Latvians never accepted that the republic of 1918 had legally ceased to exist. On 23 August 1989 more than a million people in Latvia, Lithuania and Estonia joined hands in the Baltic Way, a human chain timed to the 50th anniversary of the pact that had consigned them to Soviet control. On 4 May 1990 the Supreme Council of the Latvian SSR declared that the Republic of Latvia founded on 18 November 1918 was being restored. On 21 August 1991, after the failed coup in Moscow, it restored independence in fact. Latvia joined NATO and the European Union in 2004.

The point matters. Latvia does not treat 1991 as the birth of a new country. It treats it as the recovery of the one proclaimed in the theatre in 1918. That is why the national day is still 18 November.

How it is marked

The customs are simple and widely kept. Many families place candles or lamps in their windows. People lay flowers at the Freedom Monument in Riga, the tall stone figure of Liberty holding three golden stars, which stand for the same three regions named in the 1918 proclamation. A torchlight procession has become a familiar part of the Riga evening, with marchers carrying flags and flames through the centre of the city. The president addresses the nation, and the anthem, Dievs, svētī Latviju (God Bless Latvia), is sung.

This year, for the 108th anniversary, the National Armed Forces held their parade at 1:30pm on 11 November Embankment in Riga, with allied units and the country’s defence and security services taking part, and a 21-gun salute fired from AB Dambis. From 11am to 4pm the public could walk around a display of military vehicles and equipment at Riga Passenger Port, and visit Latvian and Lithuanian navy ships at the berths. In the evening Freedom Square held a holiday concert called Brīvības ainavas (Landscapes of Freedom), followed at 8pm by the president’s address and the anthem, and then a light show that repeated until 11pm. The Staro Rīga light festival ran across the city the same evening. Latvian Television and Latvian Radio broadcast the parade.

A day about continuity

There is a particular gravity to a national day that has had to be kept through occupation as well as in freedom. For much of the twentieth century 18 November could not be celebrated openly in Riga. It was remembered in exile, and privately at home. That history is why the candles in the windows still matter. They are not only decoration for a public holiday. They are a way of saying that the state declared in a theatre in 1918 is the same one being honoured today.
