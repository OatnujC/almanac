Louis Daguerre’s Birthday

Louis-Jacques-Mandé Daguerre was born on 18 November 1787 in Cormeilles-en-Parisis, a small town just north-west of Paris. He was not, strictly speaking, a scientist, and he did not invent photography alone. What he did invent, and what the French government gave to the world in 1839, was the first photographic process that ordinary people could actually use: the daguerreotype. Today is his 239th birthday, a good moment to remember the day-lit copper plates that, for a couple of decades, seemed like a miracle.

From the theatre to the laboratory

Daguerre arrived in Paris in 1804 and trained as a stage designer. He was good at illusion. In 1822, with the painter Charles-Marie Bouton, he opened the Diorama, a theatre in which huge canvases, painted on both sides, changed scene as the lighting shifted from the front of the cloth to the back. Audiences came to see mountains, cathedrals and moonlight appear and dissolve. To paint those views accurately he used a camera obscura, the old optical box that throws a scene onto a ground glass, and he became a regular at the Paris shop of the opticians Chevalier. It was there that he heard about Nicéphore Niépce, a country inventor who had already managed to fix a faint image made by light.

The two men became partners. Niépce’s early “heliographs” took hours and were hard to see. After Niépce died in 1833, Daguerre kept working, now with Niépce’s son Isidore as his partner in the business, and found a faster way. He coated a silvered copper plate with iodine vapour, which formed light-sensitive silver iodide on the surface. A much shorter exposure in the camera produced a latent image, still invisible, which he then brought out with mercury vapour, before fixing the plate so that further light would not blacken it. The picture that appeared was extraordinarily sharp, a mirror-like unique original, not a print that could be inked over and over.

A gift from the French state

Daguerre first sought a commercial backer, without success. The physicist and politician François Arago took the invention up instead, and persuaded the French government to buy it. In 1839 the state granted Daguerre a lifetime pension of 6,000 francs a year, and Isidore Niépce 4,000, in return for the process. On 19 August 1839 Arago described the method in detail at a joint meeting of the Academy of Sciences and the Academy of Fine Arts in Paris. The terms were unusual and generous: France would not hold the process as a secret or a monopoly. It was published so that anyone could try it. (A separate British patent, taken out in Daguerre’s name, complicated matters in England, but on the continent the method spread with remarkable speed.)

Within months there were daguerreotype studios in the great cities of Europe and America. People sat, very still, for portraits. Streets, statues and shopfronts were recorded with a precision no pencil could match. Because a daguerreotype is a unique plate, there is something intimate about the surviving ones: you are looking at the very silver that faced the sitter, not at a copy. The process had limits. Exposures were still long by later standards. Early street views often look empty of traffic, because moving carriages and pedestrians vanished during the wait. It was also quickly overtaken. By the 1850s paper and glass-plate processes that could yield multiple prints were taking over, and the daguerreotype’s brief reign was ending.

The later years

Daguerre himself retired to Bry-sur-Marne, east of Paris, and died there on 10 July 1851, at 63. He had begun as a painter of theatrical light and ended as the man whose name, for a generation, meant photography itself. Other inventors have fair claims in the same story, above all Niépce, without whose earlier work the partnership would not have existed, and, in Britain, William Henry Fox Talbot, who was developing a negative-positive paper process of his own at the same moment. The birthday is not a claim that Daguerre did everything. It is a way of marking the man who made a usable photograph, and the day in 1839 when a government decided that the method should belong to everybody.

If you look at a daguerreotype now, in a museum, the plate still has to be tilted until the image catches the light. That little act of finding the angle is a reminder of how strange the first photographs must have seemed: a picture that was also a mirror, made by the sun, on a sheet of silvered copper.
