Major occasion · 22 November 2026

Christ the King

The church year has a different turning point from the diary on the kitchen wall. For Roman Catholics and many Anglicans, Sunday 22 November 2026 is the feast of Christ the King: a final gathering of the year’s themes before Advent begins next Sunday. Its full Catholic title is the Solemnity of Our Lord Jesus Christ, King of the Universe. Behind that grand language lies a practical question: what kind of power is worth trusting, and what does faithful service look like?

This is a moving Sunday rather than a fixed November anniversary. In the modern Roman calendar it occupies the last Sunday in Ordinary Time; the Church of England also places Christ the King on the Sunday next before Advent. Advent begins on 29 November in 2026, which gives today’s date. Other Christian calendars have different arrangements, so this should not be treated as a feast observed by every Christian community on the same day.

Pope Pius XI established the feast in 1925 through his encyclical Quas primas. He wrote in the unsettled years after the First World War, amid arguments about the place of religion in public life and the authority claimed by states. The observance originally belonged to October. Its later position at the end of the liturgical year gives it a particular shape: looking back over the story of Jesus while looking forward to Christian hopes about the fulfilment of history.

The language of kingship can sound remote from ordinary life, especially in a world where royal imagery often suggests wealth, military strength or inherited privilege. Christian worship gives the word a different centre of gravity. The feast concerns the authority of Jesus, understood through his teaching, suffering, resurrection and care for others. It invites worshippers to examine the things that command their own loyalty. That makes it more searching than a ceremonial celebration of crowns.

In the UK, people encounter the day through parish Eucharists, hymns, readings and sermons. The Church of England’s published lectionary names it explicitly, while Catholic parishes celebrate it as a solemnity. In England and Wales it is also the setting for World Youth Sunday, whose 2026 parish celebrations are scheduled for today. This brings younger members of congregations into the picture and asks churches to take their contribution seriously. That related observance has its own entry in this almanac.

Internationally, the US Catholic bishops connect the feast with reflection on religious liberty, while Catholic and Anglican communities elsewhere keep it within their own patterns of worship. It is a church observance rather than a worldwide civil holiday. Particular services and local customs vary; no single procession or national ceremony defines the day. Its continuity comes from the shared place it holds in the worshipping year.

There is something fitting about ending a year with an examination of service rather than a tally of achievements. Even a visitor who does not share the theology can recognise the questions being asked: whose needs have been noticed, which loyalties have become too powerful, and what might a more generous life require? Today closes one cycle of the Christian calendar. Next Sunday, Advent will begin another, with its quieter language of expectation and preparation.
