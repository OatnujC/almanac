Minor occasion · 19 November 2026

Brazil’s Flag Day

Brazil’s Flag Day falls on 19 November because the republican national flag was adopted on that date in 1889. Today is the 137th anniversary. The republic itself had been proclaimed only four days earlier, on 15 November, so the new government was deciding very quickly what the country’s changed political identity should look like. The result is one of the world’s most recognisable flags: a yellow diamond on green, containing a blue celestial globe, white stars and a white band bearing the national motto.

The design also carried continuity. Brazil’s imperial flag had already used green and yellow and a central emblem. The republican version retained the colours while replacing the imperial centre with a different vision of the nation. This made the flag a statement about political change without completely abandoning a familiar national appearance. Brazil’s Museum of the Republic explains that the flag was established by Decree No. 4 of 19 November 1889, which also dealt with other official symbols of the new republic.

There is a useful distinction between the flag’s birthday and the commemorative day. According to the museum, 19 November became Flag Day in 1908, following an initiative by members of Congress. Its creation offered the still young republic a civic celebration. The museum also traces an immediate motive to an incident in Argentina in which a Brazilian flag had been torn during a protest. A public symbol could gather together pride, diplomatic feeling and support for a political order that had already experienced considerable upheaval.

The stars make this flag especially rewarding to examine. The Museum of Astronomy and Related Sciences describes an idealised representation of the sky over Rio de Janeiro at the proclamation of the republic. The modern flag contains 27 stars representing the units of the federation. The celestial sphere is presented as though seen from outside, which reverses the familiar view from the ground. It is a constructed national image with an astronomical logic, rather than a casual sprinkling of decorative stars around the blue circle.

Its creators brought different skills to the task. Teixeira Mendes developed the conception with guidance from Miguel Lemos; Décio Vilares drew the design, and the astronomer Manuel Pereira Reis determined the stellar positions. The words Ordem e Progresso mean Order and Progress and express the influence of positivist ideas on the republican designers. The flag therefore holds several kinds of history at once: the survival of imperial colours, the political language of the republic and an attempt to give the new state a scientifically informed emblem.

Flag Day is marked through civic and educational ceremonies. A Minas Gerais state decree, for example, provides for public flag raising on 19 November and special commemorations in state schools. That gives a concrete picture of how the occasion enters ordinary life: through lessons, speeches and the shared act of looking towards a flag. In Britain it is chiefly a date to notice through Brazilian history and cultural connections, rather than a UK observance. A satisfying way to mark it is to look closely at the design, then at the history of its adoption. Something that seems instantly familiar at a football match becomes a compact account of a country remaking itself.
