Minor occasion · Tuesday 10 November 2026

Bestu Varas (Gujarati New Year)

Today Gujaratis around the world are wishing each other "Saal Mubarak" and "Nutan Varshabhinandan", both ways of saying Happy New Year. Tuesday 10 November 2026 is Bestu Varas, the Gujarati New Year, and it opens year 2083 of the Vikram Samvat calendar. For Gujarati families it is one of the happiest days of the year, a fresh start that comes right at the end of the Diwali celebrations.

Why the new year starts now

India has many traditional calendars, and they do not all begin the year at the same time. In much of northern and western India the Hindu new year begins in spring, on the first day of the bright fortnight of the month of Chaitra; that day is celebrated as Gudi Padwa in Maharashtra and Ugadi in the south, and in 2026 it fell on 19 March. Gujarat follows a different reckoning, sometimes called Kartikadi, in which the year begins on the first day of the bright fortnight of the month of Kartika, known in Gujarati as Kartak Sud Ekam. That is the day after the new moon of Diwali.

This year there is a small twist. Diwali night was Sunday 8 November, but the new moon was still in force at sunrise on Monday 9 November. A Hindu calendar day belongs to the lunar day running at sunrise, so Monday counted as a gap day, which some Gujarati families call a padtar or blank day, and the new year began at sunrise today. The same lunar day is Govardhan Puja, or Annakut, elsewhere in India.

What Vikram Samvat is

The Vikram Samvat era is traditionally linked to King Vikramaditya of Ujjain, a legendary ruler, and its years run about 57 years ahead of the Gregorian calendar, which is why 2026 to 2027 corresponds to Samvat 2083. It is the official calendar of Nepal and remains widely used across India for religious festivals, family occasions and, in Gujarat, for the business year too.

A new year for business

Gujarat has a long history as a trading region, and the new year has a strong commercial side. Traditionally, merchants close their old accounts in the run-up to Diwali and open fresh account books, called chopda, in a ceremony called Chopda Pujan, in which the new books are blessed with prayers to Lakshmi, the goddess of wealth. In 2026 that worship was held on Diwali itself. Many shops and offices then take a few days off, reopening on Labh Pancham, the "fifth day of profit", which this year falls on Saturday 14 November.

How the day is celebrated

Bestu Varas usually begins early. People bathe, put on new clothes and visit the temple to pray for a good year, and temples across Gujarat see huge crowds from first light. Homes are decorated with rangoli, colourful patterns made with powder or flowers at the doorstep, and with strings of lights left up from Diwali.

After that the day is all about visiting. Families call on relatives, friends and neighbours, touching the feet of elders for blessings and sharing plates of sweets and savoury snacks. Gujarati favourites like mathiya, crisp spiced wafers, and chorafali, light fried strips dusted with chilli, are classic Diwali and new year treats. In Gujarat the political leaders hold public receptions to exchange greetings with the people.

Tomorrow the festive cycle continues with Bhai Bij, the Gujarati name for Bhai Dooj, when brothers visit their sisters.

In Britain

Britain has one of the largest Gujarati communities outside India, particularly in Leicester and north-west London, and Bestu Varas is a big day here too. Temples hold early prayers and Annakut food offerings, and families spend the day visiting one another. Leicester's Diwali celebrations along Belgrave Road, often called the Golden Mile, are among the biggest outside India, and for many Gujarati families in the city today is the cheerful finale. If a Gujarati friend or colleague wishes you "Saal Mubarak" today, the happy reply is to wish them the same.
