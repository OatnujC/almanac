Major occasion · Wednesday 11 November 2026

Bhai Dooj

Today is Bhai Dooj, the fifth and final day of the Diwali festival in much of India. It is a festival about brothers and sisters. Sisters perform a small ceremony for their brothers, marking a tilak, a dot of vermilion or sandalwood paste, on their foreheads, waving a lamp in front of them and praying for their long life and happiness. Brothers in turn give gifts and promise to look after their sisters. It is warm, domestic and full of sweets, and a lovely way to close the five days of Diwali.

Why it falls today

Like the rest of Diwali, Bhai Dooj follows the Hindu lunar calendar rather than the Western one. It falls on the second day (dwitiya) of the bright fortnight of the month of Kartik, two days after the main Diwali night. This year Diwali's main day was Sunday 8 November, Govardhan Puja was on 10 November, and Bhai Dooj falls on Wednesday 11 November, as confirmed by India's tourism ministry, Drik Panchang and timeanddate.

The date can be a little tricky. According to Drik Panchang, the dwitiya lunar day began at 2:00pm on 10 November and ends at 3:53pm on 11 November (New Delhi time). Tradition says the tilak should be performed in the early afternoon, a period called Aparahna, while the dwitiya is still running. On 11 November it covers that whole window in India, so that is the main day; the recommended tilak time in New Delhi is 1:10pm to 3:20pm. The Times of India notes that because of the time difference, some families in the UK, the US and Canada following their local almanac may find the deciding afternoon falls on Tuesday 10 November, so dates abroad can differ by a day.

Next year Bhai Dooj moves back to Sunday 31 October 2027, as the lunar calendar shifts against the solar one.

The stories behind it

The most widely told story is about Yama, the god of death, and his twin sister Yamuna, the goddess of the river. Yamuna had long invited her brother to visit, and on this day he finally came. She welcomed him with a lamp, marked a tilak on his forehead and fed him a feast. Touched, Yama granted her a wish, and she asked that any brother who visits his sister on this day and receives a tilak from her should be protected from untimely death. That is why the day is also called Yama Dwitiya.

Another story says that after the god Krishna defeated the demon Narakasura, the event remembered earlier in Diwali, he visited his sister Subhadra, who welcomed him with lamps, flowers and sweets and marked his forehead with a tilak.

Many names, one idea

Bhai means brother, and dooj means second, after the second lunar day. The festival is known by many names across South Asia: Bhaiya Dooj in much of north India, Bhau Beej in Maharashtra, Bhai Bij in Gujarat, Bhai Phonta (or Bhai Phota) in Bengal and Bhai Tika in Nepal, where it comes at the end of the Tihar festival. The customs vary a little from region to region. In Bengal, sisters may fast until they have marked their brother's forehead; in Nepal the tika is multicoloured and sisters make garlands for their brothers.

How it is marked today

The day usually begins with sisters preparing a thali, a decorated plate holding a lamp, rice, vermilion or sandalwood paste, sweets and sometimes a coconut. Brothers sit facing them, often with their heads covered. The sister performs an aarti, circling the lamp before her brother, applies the tilak, offers sweets and prays for him. Brothers give gifts, money or clothes. Then the family eats together, often a festive meal that the sister has prepared, echoing Yamuna's feast for Yama.

Bhai Dooj is an optional, or "restricted", holiday in India, so many offices remain open, but the roads and trains fill up with siblings travelling to see each other. In the UK, with its large Hindu, Sikh and Jain communities, families mark it at home and in temples, sometimes on a nearby weekend, and siblings who live far apart often send a tilak or a rakhi-style greeting by post or video call.

It is often compared with Raksha Bandhan in the summer, another festival of brothers and sisters. The difference is in the gesture: at Raksha Bandhan sisters tie a thread on their brother's wrist, while at Bhai Dooj they mark his forehead and feed him, with the spotlight firmly on wishing him a long life.
