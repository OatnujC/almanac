Minor occasion · 18 November 2026

Croatia’s Day of Remembrance of the Victims of Vukovar and Škabrnja

Today is Croatia’s Day of Remembrance of the Victims of the Homeland War and the Day of Remembrance of the Victims of Vukovar and Škabrnja. It has been a national public holiday since 2020. This year is the 35th anniversary of 18 November 1991, the day the eastern city of Vukovar fell after an 87-day siege, and the day of a massacre in the village of Škabrnja, far to the west, near Zadar. The 2026 commemorations were held under the slogan “Courage, Sacrifice, Freedom.”

The fall of Vukovar

Vukovar stands on the Danube, in a part of Croatia that was, in 1991, newly independent and immediately at war. From late August that year the city was besieged by the Yugoslav People’s Army and Serb paramilitary forces, whose numbers and firepower far exceeded those of the Croatian defenders. For 87 days the town was shelled and fought over street by street. When it fell on 18 November, the city was in ruins. Thousands of people were dead, and many of the survivors were taken prisoner or driven out. After the surrender, wounded people and staff were taken from the Vukovar hospital; many of the men among them were murdered at a nearby farm at Ovčara. Thirty-five years later, the Croatian Parliament noted, more than 300 of Vukovar’s residents are still listed as missing.

Škabrnja, the same day

The same date is also remembered for what happened in Škabrnja, a village in the hinterland of Zadar, hundreds of kilometres from the Danube. On 18 November 1991, 58 people were killed there, a figure given both by the Croatian Parliament and, in a breakdown of 43 civilians and 15 Croatian defenders, by the Ministry of Defence. The victims included women, children and elderly residents. The two places, one a destroyed city, the other a village, are named together in the official title of the day because they fell into horror on the same date, in the same war, and have come to stand, in Croatian memory, for both suffering and resistance.

How the day is kept

The centrepiece in Vukovar is the Column of Remembrance, a silent walk of about five and a half kilometres. It starts at the National Memorial Hospital, named for Dr Juraj Njavro, the wartime hospital that held out through the siege, and ends at the Memorial Cemetery of the Victims of the Homeland War. Defenders and staff from 1991 customarily walk at the front. At the cemetery, wreaths are laid and candles lit, and a mass is said for the dead. This year, as in other years, thousands joined the column. Across the rest of the country people light candles on the eve of the day and on the day itself, and official ceremonies are held in Škabrnja and in other towns that lost people in the war of 1991 to 1995.

Croatia has other dates in its calendar for victory and for statehood. 18 November is not one of those. It is a day for the dead and the missing, and for a city that was almost erased and then slowly rebuilt. Thirty-five years is long enough for a generation to have grown up with no memory of the siege, which is one reason the walk from the hospital to the cemetery is repeated: so that the names, and the ruins from which the present city rose, are not left only to those who were there.
