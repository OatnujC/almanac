Major occasion · Sunday 8 November 2026

Remembrance Sunday — two minutes the country keeps together

Remembrance Sunday is a pause the country takes together. In churches, at village memorials and along Whitehall, people stand still for two minutes and remember those who died in war. For many it is also a family day: a relative’s name on a stone, a poppy on a coat, a service whose words have become familiar through long repetition. The mood is solemn rather than festive, and that is part of its meaning.

It falls on the second Sunday in November. In 2026 that is Sunday 8 November. Remembrance Day itself, also called Armistice Day, is always 11 November, the date in 1918 when the First World War’s guns largely fell silent, at the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month. Remembrance Sunday is the Sunday set aside for the main national ceremony, so that people can gather when more of them are free to do so. The two are related but not the same: one is a fixed anniversary, the other a moving Sunday observance.

The two-minute silence began on the first Armistice Day in 1919. Its immediate origin was a “three minutes’ pause” practised in South Africa during the final months of the war. The British War Cabinet discussed a similar observance that November, and on 7 November 1919 King George V issued a letter asking the country to pause at 11am. After the Second World War the language of Armistice Day widened into Remembrance, honouring those who served in that war as well, and in time the ceremonies came to include later conflicts.

The focus of the national ceremony is the Cenotaph on Whitehall in London. The word means “empty tomb”: no one is buried there, which is the point. It stands for those who died in conflict, especially those with no known grave or who were buried far from home, and it commemorates the fallen collectively rather than by name. Its inscription reads “The Glorious Dead”. The annual National Service of Remembrance now commemorates British and Commonwealth military and civilian servicemen and women from the two world wars and later conflicts.

This morning the service begins at 11am. The two-minute silence is marked by the firing of guns from the King’s Troop Royal Horse Artillery on Horse Guards Parade. The monarch leads the wreath-laying, joined by members of the royal family, political leaders, the armed forces, Commonwealth representatives and faith communities. The service lasts until about 11.25am, after which the Royal British Legion’s March Past of veterans moves by the memorial. No ticket is needed to watch from Whitehall, though space is limited. Across the UK, local services, parades and Fields of Remembrance offer a closer, more personal form of the same pause.

The red poppy became a symbol of remembrance after the First World War, when the flowers grew on the former battlefields of Belgium and northern France. The image was given lasting words by the Canadian military doctor John McCrae in his 1915 poem In Flanders Fields, and the Royal British Legion adopted the poppy after the organisation was formed in 1921. There is no required way to wear one, and no required date to start or stop. White poppies have also been worn, since the 1930s, as a symbol of remembrance and a commitment to peace. Whether someone stands at the Cenotaph, at a village cross or simply goes quiet at eleven, the day asks for attention rather than display: to remember particular people, and the cost of the conflicts that took them.
