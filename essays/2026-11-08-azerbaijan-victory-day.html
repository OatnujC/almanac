Minor occasion · Sunday 8 November 2026

Victory Day in Azerbaijan — a national holiday from a recent war

8 November is Victory Day in Azerbaijan (Zəfər Günü), a national holiday and a day off work. It commemorates the recapture of the city of Shusha on 8 November 2020, the decisive moment in the 44-day war with Armenian forces over Nagorno-Karabakh. For Azerbaijan it is a day of national celebration and of mourning for the soldiers who died. For Armenians, the same war brought defeat, loss and, three years later, the flight of almost the entire Armenian population of Nagorno-Karabakh. This page tries to explain the holiday without pretending that one official account is the whole story.

Why 8 November

Victory Day is a fixed anniversary. Shusha (known to Armenians as Shushi) fell to Azerbaijani forces on 8 November 2020. Two days later a ceasefire agreement, signed by Azerbaijan’s President Ilham Aliyev, Armenia’s Prime Minister Nikol Pashinyan and Russia’s President Vladimir Putin, ended the fighting from 10 November.

The date of the holiday changed almost as soon as it was created. On 2 December 2020 President Aliyev signed a decree making 10 November, the day the ceasefire took effect, Victory Day. The next day the decree was changed, moving the holiday to 8 November, the day of Shusha’s capture, because 10 November is already marked in Turkey, Azerbaijan’s close ally, as the anniversary of the death of Mustafa Kemal Atatürk. The decision shows how much Shusha mattered: a new station on the Baku Metro was named “8 November” at the president’s suggestion.

Background: a long conflict

Nagorno-Karabakh is a mountainous region inside Azerbaijan’s internationally recognised borders that had a majority Armenian population in the Soviet era. As the Soviet Union broke up, a war from 1988 to 1994 left Armenian forces in control of the region and of seven surrounding Azerbaijani districts. Hundreds of thousands of people on both sides were displaced, including the Azerbaijani population of those districts and of Shusha. For the next 26 years, international mediation, led by the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe’s Minsk Group, failed to produce a settlement.

On 27 September 2020 Azerbaijan launched an offensive. The war that followed was notable for the extensive use of drones, which played a large part in Azerbaijan’s success, and Turkey gave Azerbaijan military support. Several ceasefires brokered by Russia, France and the United States collapsed within hours. Official figures put the military dead at 3,825 for Armenia and 2,906 for Azerbaijan, and civilians were killed on both sides.

Why Shusha mattered

Shusha sits on a high plateau above Stepanakert (Khankendi to Azerbaijanis), the region’s main city, and controls the road to Armenia. Its capture made the Armenian position untenable. But its importance is cultural as well as military. Azerbaijanis regard Shusha as a cradle of their music and poetry, the home of poets, singers and composers, and in January 2021 it was declared Azerbaijan’s cultural capital. For Armenians, Shushi was also a historic town, with the Ghazanchetsots Cathedral, seat of the local diocese of the Armenian Apostolic Church. The cathedral was struck twice by shelling during the war, an attack Human Rights Watch described as a possible war crime. Both peoples have suffered violence in the town; in 1920 its Armenian quarter was destroyed, and in 1992 its Azerbaijani population was driven out.

After the war

The 2020 ceasefire left Armenian forces in part of Nagorno-Karabakh, protected by about 2,000 Russian peacekeepers, and returned the surrounding districts to Azerbaijan. That arrangement did not last. After a months-long blockade of the road linking the region to Armenia, Azerbaijan launched a rapid offensive on 19 and 20 September 2023. The breakaway authorities surrendered, agreed to dissolve themselves, and almost the entire Armenian population of the region, more than 100,000 people, fled to Armenia. Human rights organisations and many governments condemned what they described as ethnic cleansing; Azerbaijan rejects that description and says Armenian residents were free to stay.

Since then, the two countries have moved towards a peace agreement. On 8 August 2025, at the White House, their foreign ministers initialled the agreed text of a treaty. As of November 2026 it has not been signed or ratified: Azerbaijan wants Armenia first to change constitutional language it regards as a territorial claim, and Armenia is preparing a new constitution, though passing it will require a referendum.

How the day is marked

In Azerbaijan, Victory Day is celebrated with flags across the country, concerts, fireworks and official ceremonies. Families visit the graves of soldiers killed in the war, many buried in “Alleys of Martyrs” in towns and cities, and the day is closely tied to Memorial Day on 27 September, the anniversary of the war’s start, when the dead are mourned. In 2026, Turkey’s President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan joined President Aliyev in Shusha for the holiday, where Aliyev said he hoped the normalisation process with Armenia would bring concrete results.

For Armenians, there is no celebration on this date. The war and the loss of Nagorno-Karabakh are remembered with grief, and displaced families still hope to learn the fate of relatives missing since the fighting. Recording Victory Day in an almanac is a way of noticing how a very recent war turns into a national anniversary, and of remembering that the same date can mean triumph to one people and loss to another.
