Minor occasion · 19 November 2026

The Great American Smokeout

There was a time, the American Cancer Society reminds us, when smoking was practically everywhere: in restaurants, offices, shops, even lifts and aeroplanes, with film stars and cartoon characters selling cigarettes on television. The Great American Smokeout grew up in that world. Held every year on the third Thursday of November, it invites people who smoke to give up for one day and to use that day to start a plan to quit for good. This year it falls on 19 November, and the American Cancer Society is marking it as the Smokeout’s 50th anniversary.

A small-town idea

The Smokeout has humble roots. The American Cancer Society traces the idea to a 1970 event in Randolph, Massachusetts, where Arthur P. Mullaney asked people to give up cigarettes for a day and give the money they would have spent to a high school scholarship fund. Then, in 1974, Lynn R. Smith, of the Monticello Times, a small family-run weekly in Minnesota, led the state’s first “D-Day”, or Don’t Smoke Day. Minnesota Public Radio reported on the day itself, 4 January 1974, that it was the culmination of a four-week campaign urged on by Smith’s weekly editorials, and that 291 of the town’s 1,700 or so residents had pledged never to smoke again. Smith had been a heavy smoker himself and, his wife Margaret told the reporter, had “just plain quit” 17 years earlier. She explained the campaign’s twin aims: to encourage smokers to think about quitting by documenting the health hazards, and to stand up for non-smokers’ right to breathe clean air in public places.

The idea caught on. On 18 November 1976 the California Division of the American Cancer Society persuaded nearly a million smokers to quit for the day. That event counts as the first official Great American Smokeout, and in 1977 the society took it nationwide.

Fifty years of change

The Smokeout arrived just as attitudes to tobacco were beginning to shift, and the American Cancer Society credits it with helping to change them. Through the late 1980s and 1990s, many states and cities banned smoking in workplaces and restaurants, raised cigarette taxes, limited tobacco promotion and acted to discourage teenage smoking. The society says the share of American adults who smoke fell from 42% in 1965 to 10% in 2024.

Yet the anniversary is not purely a celebration. The society’s chief executive, Shane Jacobson, said this week: “We’ve made real progress over the past 50 years, but the war against tobacco isn’t over, and in many ways, the battles are now more complicated and more difficult to win.” Its advocacy arm, the American Cancer Society Cancer Action Network, has marked the 50th Smokeout with a report called The #Toxic Smoking Aesthetic, which looks at how smoking is being portrayed on TikTok and Instagram. It finds that cigarettes are increasingly shown as stylish, nostalgic and aspirational, particularly to young women and girls, using the same themes of glamour and belonging the tobacco industry relied on for decades. The society also points to the dismantling of the federal Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s Office on Smoking and Health and to new flavoured nicotine products as reasons to worry that progress could slip.

Why one day matters

The health case behind the day is stark. The American Cancer Society says smoking causes about 20% of all cancers and about 30% of cancer deaths in the United States, and accounts for about 80% of lung cancer deaths. It is linked not only to lung cancer but to cancers of the mouth, throat, oesophagus, stomach, bladder, kidney, pancreas, liver, cervix and bowel, among others.

The good news is that quitting helps at any age, and quickly. According to the society, within minutes of stopping your heart rate drops; within days nicotine levels in the blood fall to zero and carbon monoxide returns to normal; within a year or two the risk of heart attack drops dramatically; and after ten years the risk of lung cancer is about half that of someone who still smokes. Because nicotine is so addictive, many people need several attempts, which is why the Smokeout encourages people to make a plan, and to get help: medication, counselling, telephone quitlines and the support of friends and family all improve the odds, and combining methods works better than any one alone.

How it is marked

Today the Smokeout is marked across the United States with rallies, parades, stunts, information stands and, the society notes, even “cold turkey” dishes on school and workplace menus. Health departments, hospitals, employers and community groups use the day to promote quitlines and quit programmes, and campaigners use it to press lawmakers on tobacco policy. Its message has stayed the same for half a century: quitting can begin with a single day.
