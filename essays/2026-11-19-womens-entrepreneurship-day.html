Minor occasion · 19 November 2026

Women’s Entrepreneurship Day

Women’s Entrepreneurship Day is observed on 19 November to recognise women who create businesses and to encourage the support that allows more women to do so. It is an occasion for thinking about access to money, useful contacts, skills and the freedom to turn an idea into a livelihood. The date is fixed each year. It grew from a specific initiative rather than from a religious calendar or an anniversary of legislation, and its history is recent enough to follow through the organisers’ own records.

Entrepreneur and humanitarian Wendy Diamond founded the Women’s Entrepreneurship Day Organization in 2013. The first celebration followed on 19 November 2014 at the United Nations in New York. The organisation’s archived programme places it in the Delegates Dining Room, with speakers drawn from business, public life and development work. The venue is part of the story, but holding an event at the UN should not be confused with a General Assembly resolution establishing a UN international day. This is an organisation-led global observance with official proclamations and partners.

An account published immediately after the inaugural event describes an important inspiration. During a visit to Honduras, Diamond encountered the Adelante Foundation and its work providing small loans to women. That experience connected the idea of entrepreneurship with household security and children’s opportunities. The underlying ambition was broader than celebrating already famous business leaders. A small enterprise can matter enormously to the person who owns it, even when it never becomes a large company or attracts international attention.

The first year also linked the date to wider discussion. A US State Department programme for the 2014 Global Entrepreneurship Summit in Morocco dedicated 19 November to women’s entrepreneurship. It brought together women entrepreneurs and senior public figures for a day of discussion and exchange. Such gatherings connect recognition with an opportunity to meet people, exchange experience and discuss the obstacles involved in building a business. The useful test is whether the contacts and ideas gained there become something practical afterwards: a better plan, a helpful introduction or the confidence to keep going.

There is a date detail worth keeping straight in 2026. The organisation identifies 19 November as the annual observance, while advertising its New York summit for 13 November. The summit and the calendar day are related but separate. The organisation also works through global and student ambassadors and offers educational and business resources. Celebrations around the world can therefore take different forms and happen on dates chosen by local organisers. An anniversary in the calendar does not imply that every associated conference is happening today.

For a UK reader, a thoughtful way to mark the date could be to recommend a woman-owned business, share a useful professional introduction or offer time to someone developing an idea. Those are suggestions rather than claims about a particular local event. Around the world, the movement’s summits and ambassador network provide more formal settings for recognition and discussion. The most interesting question is what support will last after the speeches finish. A reliable customer, an accessible training opportunity or a fair hearing from a potential investor may be less visible than an award, yet matter more to the business taking its next uncertain step.
